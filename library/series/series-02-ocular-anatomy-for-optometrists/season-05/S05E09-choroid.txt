# Description: The choroid is the dark, richly vascular back half of the uveal tract, and in this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks we map its gross anatomy layer by layer. Learn where the choroid begins and ends, how the suprachoroidea moors it loosely to the sclera, how Haller's layer, Sattler's layer and the choriocapillaris are stacked together, and why the whole coat is anchored firmly only at the ora serrata and the optic nerve. Made for optometry students, practitioners, and anyone who wants a clear anatomical tour of the coat that feeds the outer retina.

Host: Then let us pin it in place first. Where exactly is the choroid, and how far does it run?

Guide: The choroid is the posterior part of the uveal tract, the middle vascular coat of the eyeball. Remember from the last few episodes that this coat is one continuous sheet. Iris at the front, ciliary body in the middle, choroid behind, all one structure serving different roles along its length. The choroid begins anteriorly at the ora serrata, the scalloped forward edge of the retina where the sensory retina and the choroid meet the pars plana, the flat rear zone of the ciliary body that we mapped in episode 47, Ciliary Body. From there it sweeps backward as a single cup shaped sheet, lying between the sclera outside and the retina inside, and it ends where the optic nerve pierces the coat at the back of the eye. In gross dimensions it is thickest at the back, about zero point two two millimetres at the posterior pole, thinning to about zero point one millimetres up at the ora serrata, which is why any section through the eye shows it as a delicate dark stripe that swells toward the nerve. In Series 1, in an episode called The Back of the Eye, we toured this territory from the inside, from the retina's side of the wall. Today we are standing on the other side of that wall, looking at the sheet of tissue the retina leans against.

Host: Before we take it apart, one first impression question. When you look into an eye, the fundus glows orange and brown. How much of that colour is actually the choroid?

Guide: A great deal of it. The choroid is a deeply pigmented, richly vascular sheet, brownish black in the fresh state, and anatomists have long compared the whole uveal coat to a dark grape, which is where the word uvea comes from, uva, the Latin for grape; peel the outer coats away and the dark, plump vascular mass does look like a grape. Under the microscope its tissue is stuffed with two things above all: melanocytes, the pigment producing cells, and blood vessels of every calibre. The pigment absorbs light that has already passed through the retina, so it stops photons from bouncing around inside the eye and smearing the image, exactly like the matte black interior of a camera. None of this pigment is seen directly through the pupil; you see only its effect, the dark backdrop that lets the retina stand out by contrast. The vessels make it a living cushion that also carries heat away, because an eye focused on bright sunlight is holding a small furnace at its back. Pigment for optics, plumbing for nutrition, and a heat sink for safety. That is the choroid's job description in one breath.

Host: One vocabulary check before we climb the layers, because I have heard three names used as if they mean the same thing. Uvea, vascular coat, tunica vasculosa. Do they?

Guide: They do, and it is worth clearing up, because the literature slides between them. The uvea is the whole middle coat, iris plus ciliary body plus choroid, and its name is that grape story. The vascular coat and the tunica vasculosa are the same structure described more soberly, the blood carrying middle tunic of the eye, and the name is honest, because this coat holds nearly all the vessels that live inside the eye. And the word choroid itself is a small etymology lesson. It comes from the Greek chorion, meaning an outer skin or membrane, joined to eidos, meaning likeness, so choroid means something like membrane like, a covering in the form of a delicate skin. Three names, one coat. From now on when you meet any of them, you can quietly translate: this is the story of the iris, the ciliary body, or, as today, the choroid.

Host: Now let us build it. Take me through the layers from outside inwards, from the sclera side toward the retina.

Guide: Most textbooks describe the choroid in three parts, and we will climb them like floors from the ground up. The ground floor, lying directly against the sclera, is the suprachoroidea, sometimes called the lamina fusca, the dark lamina. We met the sclera itself in episode 28, Scleral Structure, and you may remember that its inner surface is not smooth but grooved and ridged. The suprachoroidea is a thin, pigmented membrane of fine collagen bundles, elastic fibres, and flat branching cells, a lace of melanocytes and fibroblasts, and it follows those inner scleral ridges like a sheet laid over corrugated iron. Its flattened ribbons are arranged with slots and clefts between them, and those clefts continue inward between the larger vessels of the choroid proper. It is the mooring layer, the part that ties the whole vascular coat loosely to the wall of the eye, and it runs forward to become continuous with the delicate layer that rides on the surface of the ciliary muscle.

Host: You said loosely tied. Is there an actual space between the choroid and the sclera?

Guide: There is a potential space, and it has a name: the suprachoroidal space. In the healthy eye it is not an open cavity; the suprachoroidea simply holds the two coats in gentle contact, and the pressure inside the eye presses the choroid outward against the sclera, keeping everything apposed. But because the connection is loose, fluid can peel the choroid away from the sclera in disease or after surgery, especially when the pressure inside the eye drops. That collection is a choroidal effusion, and the anatomy explains its shape: the choroid lifts easily everywhere except at its anchor points, which we will come to shortly. The same space is now used deliberately in medicine as a delivery route, because a thin needle placed in the suprachoroid can bathe the back of the eye in a drug using very small volumes, right where the target tissue lives. And one more resident of this floor: the long and short posterior ciliary nerves travel forward inside the suprachoroidea on their way to the front of the eye.

Host: Those nerves keep coming up this season. Tell us briefly where they come from, because I know the full neuroanatomy is waiting for us in Season 10.

Guide: Briefly, then. Around the back of the eye the posterior ciliary nerves pierce the sclera close to the optic nerve and run forward within the suprachoroidea. There are short posterior ciliary nerves, arriving in a ring around the nerve, and, coming forward from either side, the two long posterior ciliary nerves. The long ones carry sensory fibres, so the choroid is not the silent, painless coat people assume; inflammation there can genuinely hurt, and those fibres join the nasociliary nerve, a branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, on their way back toward the brain. Travelling alongside them are sympathetic fibres that reach the vessel walls and serve as the vascular control wiring of the coat. We will build the complete ciliary nerve map in Season 10, Neuroanatomy for Optometrists, so for today it is enough to know that the choroid's innervation rides forward in its own outermost layer, and that its arteries and nerves enter together through the same gate at the back.

Host: Now the main floor. The middle of the three parts, the vessel layer. This is the choroid proper, I take it, and it is where the real mass of the coat lives.

Guide: It is. The vessel layer is the bulk of the choroid, and it is stacked in three vascular strata, outer to inner, like a hierarchy of pipes that get smaller as you descend. The outermost stratum is Haller's layer, named for Albrecht von Haller, the great eighteenth century Swiss anatomist and physiologist, and it holds the largest vessels, wide channels running roughly parallel to the eye's surface. Inside it lies Sattler's layer, where the vessels get progressively smaller, medium arteries and veins that taper as you move inward. And innermost of all is the choriocapillaris, a single dense sheet of capillaries, the body's thinnest vessels, spread flat against the retina's outer boundary. Between and around all these vessels sit melanocytes, which is why every stratum stays dark, and a modest amount of connective tissue holding the whole sponge together. Vessels and the tissue between them make up nearly the whole thickness here, which is why this middle floor is often simply called the vascular layer. And here is the number that makes the choriocapillaris special: its lumens run about twenty to fifty micrometres across, while an ordinary capillary in the body is about eight to ten. These are the widest capillaries anywhere in the human body.

Host: Widest capillaries in the body. Why would the choroid need vessels so much fatter than everywhere else?

Guide: Because the design brief is different. A normal capillary is built for exchange along a thin tube, but the choriocapillaris is built to present an enormous, thin, single layered surface pressed right against the outer boundary of the retina, and wide calibre lets it do that with minimal resistance. Its endothelial wall is fenestrated, which means pierced by tiny windows, so fluid, oxygen and nutrients pass out of it with almost no barrier at all. And the sheet is not a random sponge; each unit of it is arranged as a lobule, a little hexagonal tile of capillaries supplied at its centre and drained around its rim, and the tiles together form a network that, gram for gram, carries the highest blood flow of any tissue in the body. But I will stop at the tile edge, because the lobules, the arteries that feed them, and the four great draining trunks are exactly the territory of episode 73, Choroidal Circulation. Today we keep the pipes; the flow story is theirs.

Host: Then answer the obvious question. All that capillary carpet, pressed against the retina. What is it actually feeding?

Guide: It feeds the outer layers of the retina, the part that lives furthest from any other source of nutrition. Remember from Series 1, in The Retina as the Sensory Layer, that the retina is a layered nervous tissue with its light catching cells, the photoreceptors, parked at its outer edge, right against a single sheet of pigmented cells called the retinal pigment epithelium. Those photoreceptor outer segments are rebuilt constantly and are among the most metabolically demanding tissue in the body, and they sit next door to the choriocapillaris, separated only by the pigment epithelium and a membrane we will meet in a moment. Meanwhile the inner half of the retina is fed by a completely different pipeline, the central retinal artery. So the retina has a dual supply, two independent feeding systems meeting in one wall, and the anatomy of that partnership, including the watershed border strip where neither system dominates, is episode 71, Retinal Blood Supply. For today, hold the gross fact: the choroid is the life support system for the outer retina.

Host: And under the fovea, the very centre of vision, is the carpet the same everywhere, or is it thicker there?

Guide: It is densest exactly where the demand is greatest. The choriocapillaris is thickest and most crowded under the macula and the fovea, the tiny central pit where cone photoreceptors are packed at their peak, which is why the posterior pole is the deepest part of the whole coat. That is also why on optical coherence tomography, the scanning technique that measures living retinal and choroidal layers in micrometres with light, the choroid appears as a thick dark band under the fovea, commonly around two hundred and seventy to three hundred micrometres there in healthy adults, tapering steadily toward the front of the eye. When we reach episode 74, Photoreceptor Distribution, we will map rod and cone density across the whole retina, and that map will explain this vascular geography perfectly. Demand and supply are superimposed, and the choroid wears the proof on its surface.

Host: Which brings us to the innermost of the three parts, the one pressed right up against the retina. Bruch's membrane. Introduce it.

Guide: Bruch's membrane, sometimes called the lamina vitrea, the glassy layer, is the choroid's inner boundary sheet, a tough, transparent sandwich lying between the choriocapillaris outside and the retinal pigment epithelium inside. It is astonishingly thin, about two to four micrometres at the posterior pole and thinner still toward the front, and it behaves as the outer basement membrane of the pigment epithelium, sharing its outer face with the capillaries of the carpet. Structurally it is an elastic and collagen sandwich, and in full detail it is described in five layers, but I am going to stop there deliberately, because episode 76, Bruch's Membrane, is devoted to naming those five from the retina side toward the choroid and to how the membrane thickens and changes with age. One gross pearl for today: in ageing eyes the fatty waste deposits called drusen settle right on this interface, between the pigment epithelium and this membrane, which is why this thin sheet matters so much in retinal practice despite being invisible to the naked eye.

Host: Now the question that has been waiting since the suprachoroidal space. If the choroid is only loosely tied to the sclera, what actually holds this whole vascular sheet in place?

Guide: Two firm anchor points, and everything between them is deliberately slack. The first anchor is anterior, at the ora serrata, where the choroid joins the ciliary body and is genuinely fixed; this is also where the choriocapillaris stops, and the capillary carpet does not continue into the ciliary body. The second anchor is posterior, at the optic nerve, where the choroid is firmly blended with the tissues around the nerve's exit and with the sclera there. Between those two anchors the choroid lies against the sclera, held mainly by the suprachoroidea and by the outward push of the pressure inside the eye. That arrangement is elegant and slightly dangerous. It lets the coat accommodate changes in volume and slide gently during eye movement, but it also means that when the pressure inside falls, the choroid can be stripped away into that potential space, producing the choroidal effusion we described earlier, and the resulting detachment will stop neatly at the two anchors, because those are the points where it cannot be peeled.

Host: Let us follow the coat forward to its anchor. At the ora serrata, how exactly does the choroid hand over to the ciliary body? Do the three floors just stop?

Guide: They hand over rather than stop, and the handover is one of the loveliest transitions in the eye. The suprachoroidea continues forward as the supraciliary layer, riding on the surface of the ciliary muscle that we studied in episode 48. The vessel layer's large channels keep going and become the vessels of the pars plana and the ciliary processes, so the vascular supply simply continues into the next part of the tract. The choriocapillaris alone reaches the end of its territory and stops at the ora, because the pars plana does not need a capillary carpet of that density. And Bruch's membrane continues inward, becoming the boundary layer that runs beneath the pigment epithelium of the ciliary body and on toward the iris. So the coat does not fall off an edge; it changes costume. Hold that picture, because the season finale next week is entirely about the fact that there was never a seam at all.

Host: You mentioned that vessels and nerves pierce around the optic nerve. What exactly goes in and out at the back?

Guide: Around the optic nerve, the short posterior ciliary arteries and the short and long ciliary nerves punch through the sclera and enter the choroid, so the back of the coat is its supply gate. Among them the two long posterior ciliary arteries are the great travellers, running forward within or alongside the suprachoroidea all the way to the ciliary body, a highway you will hear about again when the arterial map is drawn in episode 73. The choroid also continues right up to the nerve's edge, and there the short posterior ciliary arteries join in a ring of anastomosis, a circular connection of vessels around the nerve head, which matters enormously in disease because it ties the coat's inflow together at one point. But naming that ring and tracing where each artery travels and drains is episode 73's business, so today we leave the gate itself as a landmark: vessels and nerves enter around the nerve, the coat is anchored there, and the plumbing map, arrival and departure, comes later in the series.

Host: Let us bring this into the clinic for a moment. An optometrist looking into an eye cannot see the choroid directly. What can we actually observe about it?

Guide: More than you might think, indirectly. Through the pupil the choroid shows itself as the background colour of the fundus, the orange brown glow produced by its pigment and its blood seen through the translucent retina in front of it. In some eyes, especially longer myopic eyes, the pigment is thin enough and the choroid stretched enough that you see pale stripes between darker bands, the tessellated or tiger striped fundus, which is literally the large vessel layer showing through a thinned coat. And modern imaging has made the choroid measurable: enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography slices through the pigment and shows the full thickness of the coat, so we can measure those two hundred and seventy to three hundred micrometres under the fovea, watch it thin with age, and track it over visits. The once hidden coat has quietly become a routine measurement in eye examination.

Host: Does the thickness vary between healthy eyes, or is that number the same for everyone?

Guide: It varies in a pattern this series has already taught us to expect. Remember that the eyeball itself stretches in myopia and stays shorter in hyperopia. The choroid follows: in shorter hyperopic eyes it tends to be thicker, and in longer myopic eyes it is thinner, sometimes strikingly so at the back, which again is why myopic fundi so often look tessellated. It also thins steadily with age, a slow attrition that runs alongside the ageing changes in Bruch's membrane we touched earlier. And there is an even stranger finding from research: the choroid's thickness can change by small amounts, tens of micrometres, over hours in response to the focus of images on the retina, which has made it a central character in the science of how the eye controls its own growth. None of this changes the gross architecture, the same three floors are present in every eye, but it changes the numbers, and numbers are what the imaging machine now hands us every day.

Host: One darker clinical thought before we close. The choroid is pigment rich and blood rich. Does that combination have a shadow side?

Guide: It does, and it is worth stating because it flows straight from the anatomy. Melanocytes live in abundance in this coat, and the choroid is also the most richly perfused tissue in the eye. That combination makes the choroid the commonest home of primary tumours inside the adult eye, uveal melanomas, which most often arise from its own melanocytes. The anatomical lesson is simply that a tissue full of pigment cells and served by torrential blood flow is a tissue where such growths can arise and find a route onward, which is why any raised or suspicious pigmented lesion seen deep behind the retina deserves respect and referral rather than casual watching. We stay out of oncology today, but notice how the pattern follows the histology: where the melanocytes and the vessels are densest, that is where the story tends to unfold.

Host: Sum it up for me, then, the way you like to close an anatomy tour. One breath, three layers, and the story of today.

Guide: Here is the choroid in one breath. A dark, vascular coat lying between sclera and retina, running from the ora serrata to the optic nerve, about zero point two millimetres thick behind and half that in front, and built in three floors. The suprachoroidea outside, a pigmented mooring sheet riding the grooved inner sclera, carrying the ciliary nerves and wrapping a potential space. The vessel layer in the middle, with Haller's layer of large vessels, Sattler's layer of medium ones, and the choriocapillaris as a wide lumen, fenestrated capillary carpet that feeds the outer retina and is densest under the fovea. And Bruch's membrane inside, the thin glassy boundary shared with the retinal pigment epithelium. It is anchored firmly only at the ora and at the nerve, pigmented to absorb stray light, entered and innervated around the optic nerve, and its circulation, the arteries, the lobules and the draining trunks, waits patiently for episode 73. Gross anatomy, done.
