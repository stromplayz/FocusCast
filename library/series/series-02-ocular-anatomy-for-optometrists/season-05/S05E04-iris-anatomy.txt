Host: Let us start with the name. Iris. In ordinary speech it is a flower, in mythology a goddess, in optometry a ring of colour around the pupil. Which came first, and what does the word actually mean?

Guide: The word is Greek, and it means rainbow. It was the name the Greeks gave to the goddess who carried messages between heaven and earth on a bridge of colour. Anatomists borrowed it for this structure precisely because the iris is the eye's one naturally coloured part, visible to the world without a single instrument. Your Series 1 self already met it in that role. Back in an episode called The Front of the Eye, we introduced the cornea as the clear window and the iris as the coloured ring just behind it, with the pupil as its opening. Today we go inside that ring. And in one sentence, the job is this: the iris is a living diaphragm, a light-controlled aperture that changes how much light enters the eye's interior by changing the size of its central hole. Every camera you have ever held copied the idea. The adjustable blades of a camera aperture are still called its iris, a quiet compliment from engineers to anatomy.

Host: A diaphragm with a hole, then, and a very old name for a very small machine. Before we admire the engineering, give me the dimensions. How big is this structure, and how thin are we actually talking?

Guide: In the average adult eye the iris spans about twelve millimetres across, root to root, so it fills the gap between cornea and lens like a drum skin stretched inside an angular frame. The interesting number is thickness, because it is not uniform. At the collarette, a ridge on its surface that we will visit properly later, the iris is at its fattest, roughly half a millimetre. From there it thins in both directions, and it is thinnest at its root, where it hangs onto the ciliary body, so thin that this attachment strip is almost a membrane. That is why blunt trauma to the eye so often finds the root: the weakest part of a stretched diaphragm takes the load. I am deliberately not giving you pupil size numbers today. Series 1, in an episode called Pupil Examination, taught you how the living pupil is measured, and episode six of this season, Pupil Anatomy, gives that measurement its anatomy, the ranges through life and the aperture's slightly off-centre position. The iris is the fabric. The pupil is the cut-out, and the cut-out gets its own episode.

Host: So the root is the anchor. Walk me to it. Where exactly does the iris hang from, and how does that fit into the angle territory we covered last episode?

Guide: Back in episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle, we walked that region structure by structure, from the Schwalbe line across the trabecular meshwork to the ciliary body face. Now stand in that same dissection and look at the iris. The root, the outermost rim of the iris, inserts into the anterior surface of the ciliary body, roughly at its midpoint, just behind the scleral spur, the small ledge at the outer boundary of the angle. That single attachment is the iris's only fixed anchor in the eye. Everything else floats. It leaves the iris suspended in aqueous humour, in front of the lens, forming the complete partition between the anterior chamber, which we measured in episode one, and the posterior chamber, which we explored in episode two. And notice the geometry that follows. Because the root anchors so far forward and the lens bulges so close behind, the iris sits under gentle constant tension, like a trampoline mat clipped at its edges. Lose that anchor in blunt injury and you get an iridodialysis, a torn root, with the iris edge pulling away and a puddle of blood where tissue used to hold.

Host: Let us peel it, then. You promised a walk from surface to depth. How many layers does this half millimetre actually contain, and in what order?

Guide: Anatomists describe five components, and I will name all five now so the map is on the wall. Front to back. First, the anterior border layer, the iris's outermost sheet. Second, the stroma, the soft connective tissue body that makes up most of the thickness. Third, the muscle layer, which contains two muscles we will simply point at today: the sphincter pupillae, a ring of smooth muscle around the pupil, and the dilator pupillae, a thin sheet running radially across the iris. Their positions, their nerve supplies, and how they move the pupil are next episode's work, and next episode is called Iris Muscles. Fourth and fifth, the posterior pigment epithelium, which is really two closely applied layers of densely pigmented cells forming the dark curtain on the back of the iris. So the plan is honest. Today we take layers one, two, four and five in detail, we name layer three and step politely over it, and next episode we step straight into it. Nothing about how the pupil moves will sneak in early.

Host: Layer one, then. The anterior border layer. The name sounds slightly redundant, like a border that is also a layer. What am I actually looking at when I study the front surface of someone's iris?

Guide: You are looking at a condensation of the outermost stroma, a sheet about one cell layer deep, made of flattened fibroblasts, the cells that weave connective tissue, and star-shaped melanocytes, the pigment cells, all knitted together over the whole front of the iris. And here is the surprise that makes it special. It is not an epithelium. Nearly every internal surface in the body carries an epithelial lining, and the cornea, whose six layers we spent all of season four on, wears one proudly on its front. The iris does not. Its anterior surface is naked stroma, cells and collagen exposed directly to the aqueous humour, with no covering sheet at all. That has two consequences worth keeping. First, aqueous flows in and out of the surface freely, so the iris is nourished and cushioned by the very fluid it separates and regulates. Second, the sheet is incomplete by design. All over the iris, and especially near the collarette and near the root, the border layer opens into gaps, and those gaps have names. Which brings us to the crypts.

Host: Crypts. I have heard students use that word loosely for any little dark fleck on the iris. Set the record straight. What is a crypt, exactly, and where do they live?

Guide: A crypt is a true gap in the anterior border layer, a little window through which you look straight into the loose stroma beneath, and often right down to the pigment curtain behind it. The classic ones are Fuchs crypts, and they come in two families. Pupillary crypts sit close to the collarette, strung along that ridge. Ciliary crypts sit further out, in the outer third of the iris, near the root. Between the windows, the surface shows a honeycomb of small polygons, and that mesh of fine borders is the normal texture of the layer, not a finding. Why would the iris punch holes in its own front wall? Because it can afford to. With aqueous washing both of its faces, diffusion does the provisioning, and the crypts simply speed it up, letting fluid soak the stroma directly. Think of it as ventilation. A sponge works better with open pores, and the iris, which must change shape constantly and quietly, benefits from a front wall that never traps fluid against it.

Host: Which brings us to layer two, the stroma. You said it makes up most of the thickness. After the tidy, disciplined corneal stroma we spent a whole episode on, what kind of tissue is this one?

Guide: Delightfully, it is the corneal stroma's opposite number. Back in season four, episode four, Corneal Stroma, we admired that tissue's rigid discipline: layered sheets called lamellae, uniform spacing, an engineering built for transparency through perfect order. The iris stroma is loose, spongy, and irregularly arranged, a soft web of collagen fibres, fibroblasts, melanocytes, blood vessels and fine nerves, with fluid-rich spaces between them. Order would be wasted here, because the iris does not need to be perfectly clear. It needs to be freely movable, plump with fluid when it wants to be, and able to fold into shallow grooves without tearing. The thickness at the collarette is about half a millimetre, as we said, and the stroma is where almost everything you read as eye colour actually lives. Its looseness has a clinical edge too. Pigment released anywhere behind or within the iris can drift through it and settle on the lens or on the drainage structures of the angle, and the spongy stroma neither traps it nor cleans it quickly.

Host: You mentioned blood vessels living inside the stroma, and the iris has a reputation for bleeding spectacularly when it is injured. Is that reputation anatomical?

Guide: Entirely anatomical, and the anatomy is elegant. The iris is richly vascular, and its supply begins in a ring. The major arterial circle, known in the Latin as the circulus arteriosus major, sits at the base of the iris, right where the root meets the ciliary body. It is an anastomosis, a natural loop, formed by the two long posterior ciliary arteries meeting branches of the anterior ciliary arteries. From that loop, radial vessels run across the iris toward the pupil like spokes, coiling gently so they can stretch as the tissue moves, and near the collarette they anastomose again into a second ring, the minor arterial circle, the circulus arteriosus minor. In adults that second circle is often incomplete, more a ghost of a ring than a true one, but it marks the collarette's position faithfully. Now, the pearl behind the bleeding reputation. Iris vessels are built like pipes wearing heavy winter coats. Their walls carry unusually thick sleeves of collagen and very little elastic tissue, so they do not snap shut the way ordinary vessels do. When they leak, they ooze, and they keep oozing. That is the anatomy behind a slow, layered hyphema, blood settling into the anterior chamber after injury.

Host: Two arterial circles, radial spokes, heavy collagen coats. Now for the landmark everyone learns first. The collarette. Define it precisely, because I suspect students draw it in the wrong place.

Guide: The collarette is the wavy ridge circling the iris about one and a half millimetres outside the pupillary margin, and it is the single most useful landmark on the surface. Four facts meet at that line. First, it is where the iris is thickest, our half millimetre point. Second, it marks the minor arterial circle we just described, even where that circle is incomplete. Third, it divides the surface into its two zones: everything inside the collarette is the pupillary zone, everything outside it is the ciliary zone, and the two differ subtly in texture and in crypt pattern. Fourth, and this is a lovely piece of personal history, the collarette marks where the pupillary membrane attached before birth. Every one of us wore a thin vascular sheet across that opening in the womb, and when it faded away in the final months before birth it left its footprint as the collarette and its little tags. What remains visible at the pupil's edge in the living eye, and what a stubborn remnant looks like, is story material for episode six, Pupil Anatomy. The collarette is the iris's own dashed line between past and present.

Host: Give me the rest of the surface landscape. If a student has ten seconds at the slit lamp, what should they catalogue on an iris, and how much of what they see is simply normal variation?

Guide: Catalogue five things, and chart them as separate entries. The collarette, with its position and any tags. The crypts, pupillary and ciliary. The contraction furrows, the shallow concentric grooves near the root, which deepen when the pupil widens because the dilator muscle pulls the tissue into folds. The pigment frill at the pupil edge. And the root itself, where the surface dips away toward the angle. Then allow for the range of normal. Blue irises often show little pale flecks in the mid stroma called Brushfield spots, a variant so common in blue eyes that it is barely remarkable on its own, though a full dense ring of them belongs in a different conversation. Brown irises look flatter and busier, their crypts partly veiled by pigment. Furrows are deeper in some adults than others, and both crypts and furrows grow less prominent with age as the stroma loses a little of its fullness. Symmetry between the two eyes is your baseline. The finding is never one unusual iris. It is one unusual iris sitting beside an ordinary one.

Host: Colour, then. We have danced around it for ten minutes. Why is an iris blue, or brown, or green, when the pigment cells underneath are the same colour in all of us?

Guide: Because iris colour is a story about amount and location, not about different inks. Every iris has two permanent facts. First, the posterior pigment epithelium, that curtain on the back surface, is dark brown in everybody, from the palest blue eye to the deepest brown. Second, the stroma contains melanocytes, and their number is roughly the same in all of us. What differs is how much melanin those melanocytes actually produce and pack into their cells. In a brown eye, the anterior border layer and the stroma are loaded with melanin, dense enough that light stops at the surface, and the iris reads brown the way any pigmented tissue does. In a blue eye, the stroma carries almost no melanin. Light enters the tissue, scatters off the fine collagen fibres, and the short wavelengths scatter most, so the iris looks blue for the same reason the sky looks blue, by scattering rather than by pigment. That is the Tyndall effect running in your bathroom mirror. Green and hazel eyes sit between, with moderate stromal melanin. And in albinism, where melanin is missing throughout the body, the iris is translucent: shine a light from the side and it glows through the tissue, which is why those eyes guard the pupil so badly and dread the sun so much.

Host: You keep saying curtain. Take me behind the stroma. What exactly is this posterior pigment epithelium, and what is it doing back there?

Guide: The back of the iris is upholstered in pigment, two cell layers pressed tightly together, together a tenth of a millimetre or less in thickness. The anterior pigmented epithelium faces the stroma, and its cells are modified so that a broad zone of each cell is packed with contractile filaments. I will say no more than that, because those filaments belong to next episode's muscle story. I have already promised the sphincter and the dilator their own hour, and I intend to keep the promise. The posterior pigmented epithelium, behind it, is a layer of tall, densely pigmented columnar cells, so loaded with melanin that no internal detail of the cell shows through. Together the two layers form an unbroken light-proof sheet across the whole back of the iris, continuing around the pupil margin, where a tiny collar of pigment spills onto the anterior surface, and running outward to the root, where they become the two epithelial layers of the ciliary body. Functionally, the curtain is the iris's blackout lining. It guarantees that light enters the eye only through the pupil, never leaking through the tissue itself, which is exactly what an adjustable aperture requires.

Host: Nerves next. The iris is famously sensitive territory: anyone with a disgruntled iris tells you about it fast. What carries that sensation, and what else rides along the same wires?

Guide: Sensation travels in the long posterior ciliary nerves, branches of the nasociliary nerve, which itself belongs to the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve. Those nerves run forward alongside the long posterior ciliary arteries, enter the iris at the root, and spread their fine endings through the stroma, which is why inflammation of the iris is genuinely painful and famously refers to the brow and around the eye. The same nerve bundles also carry the motor traffic for the two muscles, the parasympathetic and sympathetic fibres that will drive the pupil one way and then the other. But I am going to park the motor nerves deliberately at the root, alongside the muscles they serve, because next episode, Iris Muscles, is precisely about them. Which muscle sits where, which branch of the nervous system commands which, and how two opposite jobs produce one working aperture. I will offer only one taste, so the anatomy stays honest tonight. Those two muscles are built from tissue unlike any other muscle in the body, and that odd origin is why their nerve supplies sound so surprising. Save it. Next time.

Host: Relationships, then. We know the root anchors to the ciliary body. What about the other side? Does the iris rest on the lens, or does it float?

Guide: It floats, barely brushing the lens. The narrow gap between iris and lens is the posterior chamber we mapped in episode two, filled with freshly made aqueous that must flow forward through the pupil into the anterior chamber. So the iris is a partition bathed in its own product on both faces, fluid behind, fluid in front, and that balance of pressures is what holds it in its gentle forward curve. Two consequences make good clinical sense. If the lens is removed, as in cataract surgery without an implant, the iris loses its support behind and trembles with every eye movement, a shimmer called iridodonesis that tells you instantly that the anatomy has changed. And if the pupillary flow is blocked, say by adhesions where an inflamed iris has stuck to the lens at the pupil margin, aqueous keeps accumulating behind the iris with nowhere to go, and the pressure balloons the root forward like a sail. That configuration is called iris bombe, and the drainage structures we studied in episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle, pay the price. Same tissue, two very different silhouettes, purely from fluid geometry.

Host: Before the recap, I want the optometrist's answer. Of everything we have covered tonight, what are the pieces of iris anatomy you would want a graduate to carry into the consulting room on day one?

Guide: Three, and they are all landmarks before they are pathology. First, the iris is the only piece of the uveal tract you can inspect with the naked eye, no drop, no lens, no special light, so it is your free preview of the uveal system. Use it. The contour of the iris surface even speaks about the angle hidden behind the cornea: a flat, planar iris seen in profile suggests a wide angle, while a bowed one raises the caution we developed in episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle. That is anatomy doing screening for you at first glance. Second, chart the collarette and the crypts on every patient. They are the fixed reference points for describing anything you find: a notch at two o'clock relative to the collarette, pigment dusting beyond the ciliary crypts, a segment of furrow gone missing. Vague descriptions are unreadable at review, and precise ones take only seconds. Third, remember the root. Its thinness explains traumatic dialysis after blunt injury, and its position at the angle explains why even small iris wounds shed blood and pigment into the neighbourhood of structures we will meet properly in episode seven, Ciliary Body.

Host: And the closing pearl? Give me the one that ties the whole episode together.

Guide: Here is my pearl. The iris is a free-standing organ of the anterior segment, and almost nothing about it is held in place by anything solid. It has one anchor, the root. It has no epithelium in front, so aqueous moves freely through its surface. Its vessels are stiff-sleeved and slow to stop bleeding. Its muscles, when we meet them next time, pull on a pigment curtain rather than on bone or tendon. Everything about its design trades rigidity for freedom of movement, because its entire job is to change shape quickly and quietly, many times a day, without the person it serves ever noticing. When you look at a patient's iris you are watching tissue that never rests and never complains, until something blocks the fluid or tears the root. Learn its landmarks well enough to hear the complaint early, and you have turned a pretty coloured ring into a genuine diagnostic surface.

Guide: Five takeaways. One: the iris is the most anterior part of the uveal tract, a contractile diaphragm about twelve millimetres across and roughly half a millimetre thick at its thickest, anchored only at its root to the ciliary body. Two: front to back it is anterior border layer, stroma, the two muscles we name but reserve for next time, and the two-layered posterior pigment epithelium, with no epithelium at all covering the front. Three: the surface landmarks are the collarette about one and a half millimetres from the pupil, the pupillary and ciliary crypts, and the contraction furrows near the root. Four: the blood supply is the major arterial circle at the base, feeding radial vessels that end in the often incomplete minor circle at the collarette, and sensation rides the long posterior ciliary nerves of the trigeminal. Five: colour is stromal melanin quantity, not different pigments, and the dark curtain behind guarantees that light enters only through the pupil. Hold those five, and the iris is yours.
