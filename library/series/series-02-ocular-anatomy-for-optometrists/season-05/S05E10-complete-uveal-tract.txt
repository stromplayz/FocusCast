# Description: The season finale of Focus Cast by focuslinks assembles the uveal tract as one continuous middle coat, from the iris at the front, through the ciliary body, to the choroid behind. This capstone episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists walks the two seams that knit iris, ciliary body and choroid into a single garment, then follows the blood on its entire journey: short and long posterior ciliary arteries, anterior ciliary arteries, the major arterial circle of the iris, and the four vortex veins that carry it away. A full season synthesis for optometry students, with the anatomical reasons behind iridodialysis, the deep ciliary flush and sectoral choroidal disease. Next season: Lens and Accommodation Anatomy.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to a small occasion for us, because tonight is the last night of a season. We are deep in Series 2, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, inside the season called Anterior Chamber and Uveal Tract, and this is episode ten, which answers to a grand name, Complete Uveal Tract. Grand, but honest. For nine episodes we have been walking the front rooms and machinery of the eye, one structure at a time, and tonight we step back far enough to see the whole thing as it truly exists in the living eye, not as three separate topics but as one continuous, blood-soaked coat. Iris, ciliary body, choroid, one garment running from the pupil margin all the way to the optic nerve. And because a coat built of blood vessels deserves its plumbing, we will also follow the blood itself, all the way in and all the way out. Let me bring in our guide.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome, everyone. I want to set expectations for a finale like this, because a capstone is a strange kind of anatomy lesson. Almost nothing tonight is new. The genuinely new structures of this season are behind us; tonight we mostly re-see what we have already learned, arranged differently, the way a finished jigsaw puzzle looks nothing like the pile of pieces it came from. Here is the plan, in three movements. First, we place the uveal coat among the coats of the eye and remind ourselves what the word uvea actually means. Second, we walk the two great seams, the places where iris, ciliary body and choroid are literally stitched into one piece of tissue. And third, the centerpiece: the route blood takes through this coat, every artery in, every vein out, with the clinical rewards that anatomy pays us for knowing it. The reward tonight is different from other nights. When a season ends like this, anatomy stops being a list of names and starts being a machine you can picture running. Then a season wrap, and a look ahead to the next season.

Host: Then let us place it first, because I remember from Series 1 that the eye was described in coats, like an onion with its layers, and I confess I have been waiting for this reunion all season. We kept meeting this coat at the edges of things without ever stopping to admire it. Refresh that picture for us. Where does the uveal tract sit among the layers of the eye, and where did we first meet it?

Guide: Picture three nested garments. The outermost is the fibrous coat, the tough white sclera and its transparent front window, the cornea, and we have already given the cornea a whole season of its own in Season 4, and the sclera and conjunctiva theirs in Season 3. The innermost is the neural coat, the retina, the light-catching tissue, and that is the great story of Season 8. Between them lies the middle coat, and that is tonight's subject, the uvea. Back in Series 1, in the episode The Back of the Eye, we named it in passing as the vascular coat, the layer crowded with blood vessels. The name itself is a small poem. Uvea comes from uva, the Latin word for grape, because when the outer coats are stripped away the inner eye does look like a dark grape, glistening, violet-brown, and heavy with vessels. One grape-dark coat, three named parts from front to back: the iris, the ciliary body, and the choroid.

Host: And that is the part that still astonishes me. The iris is a moving shutter. The ciliary body is a workshop. The choroid is a sponge of blood. How can one continuous coat carry three jobs that look so completely unrelated to each other?

Guide: By taking one set of raw materials and arranging it three ways. The raw materials are the same throughout: loose vascular tissue, pigment cells, smooth muscle wherever movement is needed, all wrapped around a dense network of vessels. In the iris, that package becomes a thin, muscular diaphragm. We studied its stroma, its crypts and its collarette in episode four, Iris Anatomy; its two muscles, sphincter and dilator, in episode five, Iris Muscles; and the pupil they frame in episode six, Pupil Anatomy. In the ciliary body, the same tissue becomes a triangular ring, described in episode seven, Ciliary Body, whose inner face carries the ciliary processes, the factory that manufactures the aqueous humour we watched flowing in episode two, Posterior Chamber Anatomy, and whose muscle, from episode eight, Ciliary Muscle, tugs on the lens when we focus. And behind them all, the same tissue spreads out into the choroid, episode nine, the vascular blanket that feeds the outer retina. Same cloth, three tailoring decisions.

Host: Tonight you keep promising seams, the stitches that hold the garment together. Start at the front, then. Where does the iris actually join the ciliary body, and why should we care about that particular junction?

Guide: At a junction we have literally stood at before. Follow the iris backward and it narrows to its root, and the root is anchored into the front of the ciliary body, just behind the scleral spur, that little inward shelf where the white sclera folds in to frame the drainage angle. In episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle, we walked that whole region step by step, from Schwalbe's line, the fine rim where the drainage meshwork begins, across the trabecular meshwork to the ciliary body face. The root of the iris is the seam, and it is also the thinnest part of the entire iris. Anatomy loves to point at weak places, and here is a famous one. A blunt blow to the eye can tear the iris away at exactly this line, an injury called iridodialysis, literally a splitting of the iris from its root. The seam is real tissue, and real tissue can be torn.

Host: And at the back? Where does the ciliary body finally end and the choroid begin?

Guide: At the ora serrata, and the name draws its own picture. Ora means edge or rim, and serrata means saw-toothed, because the boundary is genuinely scalloped, a jagged little frontier. This is where the flattened back zone of the ciliary body, the pars plana, hands over to the choroid, and from there the choroid runs all the way back to the optic nerve, as we mapped in episode nine, where we also met the suprachoroidea, the flattened vascular sheet that forms the choroid's outermost face. But here is the synthesis point that makes the coat truly one piece. Beneath the ciliary body and beneath the choroid lies a potential cleft, the suprachoroidal space, a thin plane that normally is barely a space at all, just layers lying quietly against each other. That same cleft runs unbroken from the region of the ciliary muscle at the front to the back of the eye. One continuous plane under one continuous coat. Surgeons can open it deliberately; a bleed can open it accidentally. But anatomically it is one plane, not three.

Host: You said pigment cells are one of the shared raw materials of the coat. How far does that pigment sheet actually run? Is the pigment in the iris the same family as the pigment behind, or are they strangers?

Guide: Better than family, it is literally one sheet of tissue, cell joined to cell, from the pupil margin all the way to the back of the eye. Start at the pupil with the posterior epithelium of the iris, deeply pigmented and two cells thick. That layer is continuous with the pigmented epithelium covering the ciliary body, and at the ora serrata it continues without interruption as the retinal pigment epithelium, the single layer we will spend real time on in Season 8. So there is one continuous pigmented ribbon, from pupil margin to optic nerve, lining the inner face of the entire uveal tract. And here is the detail that ties our own season together: the dilator muscle we met in episode five is itself built from the front edge of that same ribbon, muscle fibers grown inside epithelial cells. Once you see that, the uvea and the retina stop being neighbors and start being one wrapped bundle.

Host: Beautiful. Now give me the tour of the finished garment, the way you would walk a student around it once before an examination, with the numbers attached to each region.

Guide: Walk it front to back. The iris is a disc roughly twelve millimetres across, thin as paper at its root and thickest near the collarette. Behind it, the ciliary body is a ring, triangular in a cut through the eye, about six millimetres wide on the nasal side of the eye and close to seven on the temporal side. Of that width, roughly two millimetres belong to the pars plicata, the ridged front zone wearing its crown of some seventy ciliary processes, and about four millimetres belong to the smooth pars plana behind. Then comes the choroid, the widest stretch of the coat, from the ora serrata to the optic nerve, thickest at the back of the eye at about a fifth of a millimetre, thinning to roughly half that as it approaches the ora. Three regions, three thicknesses, one coat. The numbers differ. The tissue does not. And threaded through the front of it all, at the very center, sits the pupil, which we measured across a lifetime in episode six, Pupil Anatomy, and which is worth remembering here as an opening in this coat rather than a separate part of the eye.

Host: Now the centerpiece you promised. This coat is built around blood vessels, so let us follow the blood itself. Where does it enter the uvea, and which artery delivers it?

Guide: The story starts just behind the eye, with the ophthalmic artery, the branch of the internal carotid artery that serves the whole orbit, slipping into the orbit through the optic canal alongside the optic nerve itself, a doorway we toured back in Season 1, episode four, Orbital Openings and Communications. Its uveal deliveries arrive in three named consignments. First, ten to twenty small branches called the short posterior ciliary arteries, which head straight for the back of the eye around the optic nerve. Second, exactly two long posterior ciliary arteries, one on the nasal side and one on the temporal side. Third, the anterior ciliary arteries, which travel forward with the four straight muscles that turn the eye, riding on them like passengers; the muscles themselves are Season 9 territory, so tonight we only wave at them. One honest footnote. The ophthalmic artery also sends the central retinal artery down the core of the optic nerve to feed the retina, but that is a separate highway with its own episode, number seventy-one, Retinal Blood Supply, waiting in Season 8.

Host: Take the short ones first, since they arrive at the back of the eye in a crowd. What happens to them the moment they reach the sclera?

Guide: They pierce the sclera in a small ring around the optic nerve, and immediately two things happen. These are slender vessels, more of them than you would ever guess from the name, which is exactly why they can partition the choroid so neatly. Some of their branches form a little arterial ring around the nerve head itself, the circle of Zinn and Haller, a named loop that feeds the crowded region where choroid, retina and optic nerve all meet. The rest spread forward into the choroid, and here is the detail that matters most tonight: they do not merge into one mixed bloodstream. Each short posterior ciliary artery claims a sector, a wedge of choroid spreading from the back of the eye toward the ora serrata, and that wedge is essentially private territory, with borders that do not freely interconnect with the neighbors. The choroid is not fed like one big lake. It is fed like farmland divided into paddocks, one artery to a paddock.

Host: A paddock, an enclosed field with a fence around it. Explain why that architectural choice matters when the anatomy turns clinical.

Guide: Because blocked plumbing produces geographically precise damage. If one short posterior ciliary artery fails, the wedge of choroid it owns starves, and the injury appears as a sharply outlined, wedge-shaped sector of damage while the choroid on either side of the line stays perfectly healthy. Disease in this coat follows the boundaries of the plumbing. That is why choroidal lesions in textbooks look like maps of territories rather than random blotches, and once you know the supply is segmental, the shapes stop being mysterious. The same straight lines explain why an old defect in the fundus can stop abruptly halfway across, one wedge dark and its neighbor untouched. It is the same lesson we took from episode three with the drainage angle: anatomy first, and the clinical picture falls out of it like fruit from a branch. The uvea may be the best teacher of that lesson in the whole eye, because its blood supply is drawn in straight territorial lines.

Host: Now the two long ones. They carry the word long in their name, so I assume the distance they travel is the story.

Guide: Distance and discipline. The two long posterior ciliary arteries run forward between choroid and sclera, inside that suprachoroidal plane we spoke of, one on each side of the eye like two long corridors, nasal and temporal. And here is their discipline. The whole way forward, through the entire length of the choroid, they give off essentially nothing. They do not water the choroid at all. They are express trains that skip every station until they reach the ciliary body. Only there, up at the front of the coat, do they finally leave the plane, slip into the tissue of the ciliary body, and go looking for company. Who they find is the next piece of tonight's story.

Host: Company, meaning the anterior ciliary arteries you mentioned arriving with the straight muscles. Describe the meeting, and what gets built when it happens.

Guide: The anterior ciliary arteries come forward from the four straight muscles, and in the region of the ciliary body they meet the two long posterior ciliary arteries and join them in a linking called an anastomosis, which simply means vessels laced into a shared loop. Together they form the major arterial circle of the iris, a complete ring lying in the ciliary body near the root of the iris. This ring is the distribution hub of the whole front half of the coat. From it, branches push inward to the ciliary processes, the very faucets that fill the posterior chamber with aqueous, as we watched in episode two. From it, radial branches enter the iris and run toward the pupil margin like spokes of a wheel, the fine radial pattern visible in almost every iris you will ever examine, the pattern we studied in episode four. And smaller branches loop backward, recurrent branches running the other way to water the front of the ciliary body and the peripheral choroid. One circle, and everything downstream of it connected.

Host: Here is my slit-lamp question, the one I have been saving all season. A red eye sits down in your chair. Why should the pattern of redness on the front of the eye send our thoughts back to these uveal arteries?

Guide: Because those same arteries are shared landlords. The anterior ciliary arteries do not only feed the ciliary body and the iris; they also send fine branches into the episclera around the limbus, the frontier ring where the clear cornea shades into white sclera. So when the iris or the ciliary body becomes inflamed, the deep vessels around the cornea are called into service, and the eye shows a dusky violet flush hugging the edge of the cornea, the deep ciliary flush, rather than the bright red wash of the superficial conjunctival vessels that streams back from the eyelids. Notice also that the flush is deepest right at the corneal edge and fades outward, the exact opposite of a conjunctival pattern, and that gradient is the anatomy speaking. That distinction is one of the great anatomical triage signs in eye care. Series 1 met the red-eyed patient in an episode called Patient With Red Eye, and now you hold the plumbing behind that sign. The redness is not decoration. It is the blood route announcing itself.

Host: And every route needs an exit. The arteries have had their whole scene. Where does the blood leave the uveal tract?

Guide: By whirlpool. Gather up the veins, and you find that the veins of the choroid, the ciliary body and the iris all drain backward, coaxing their blood away from the front of the coat toward the back. These are wide, thin-walled channels with room to pool, which is one reason the coat looks so dark and so full when you meet it in the clinic. In each of the eye's four quadrants the veins converge and spiral together into one collecting trunk, a vortex vein, and there are traditionally four of them, one per quadrant. Just before each one pierces the sclera, a few millimetres behind the equator, it widens into a little chamber called an ampulla, the eye of the whirlpool where the spiraling veins gather. After the sclera, the vortex veins hand their blood to the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins, the great venous trunks of the orbit, and those drain onward toward the cavernous sinus, a wide venous lake at the base of the brain. Nearly every drop the uvea borrows leaves through those narrow posterior doors.

Host: Four doors for the entire coat. Tell me why the vortex veins matter so much as surgical landmarks, and why the tidy number four refuses to stay tidy.

Guide: Because surgery at the back of the eye lives and dies by landmarks. When a retinal detachment is repaired, when a scleral buckle is placed, the surgeon is working in exactly those quadrants where the veins exit, and the ampullae are visible, identifiable structures that must be avoided. The traditional count is four, but anatomists have counted five, six, sometimes more, and the positions vary from eye to eye, which is why every textbook hedges and every surgeon looks before cutting. The name, by the way, is exact rather than poetic. Vortex means whirlpool, and the veins from both sides of each quadrant genuinely spiral into the ampulla like water circling a drain. As for the finest architecture of the choroid's own circulation, the capillary-level story, that has a home waiting in Season 8, episode seventy-three, Choroidal Circulation. Tonight we keep to the highway map.

Host: Let us talk about scale, because it still surprises me. For all its millimetres of thickness, how much of the eye's blood supply does this coat actually claim?

Guide: An outrageous share. Roughly eighty-five percent of all the blood entering the eye goes to the uvea, and the overwhelming majority of that goes to the choroid. Per gram of tissue, choroidal flow is among the highest in the entire body, blood moving faster through it than through almost any other organ you could name. Why so greedy? Three reasons, and they are the season in miniature. The choroid is the pantry of the outer retina, whose photoreceptors have no other source of nutrition and must be fed by diffusion, as we learned in episode nine. The ciliary processes need steady pressure to manufacture aqueous, as we saw in episode two. And the flood doubles as a heat exchanger for the working retina, carrying warmth away from continuously active tissue. One coat, three metabolic services, paid for in blood. And the bill is no accident, because the supply we have just mapped, the paddocks and the express lines, exists precisely to keep three services running at three different speeds.

Host: Then answer the question in tonight's title honestly. Why should this be one tract at all? Nature could have built three separate structures with three separate supplies. What does continuity actually buy?

Guide: Continuity buys a single housekeeping system wrapped around the neural eye. Look at what the coat does as a whole. Its pigment, one unbroken ribbon of melanin-bearing cells from iris to choroid, swallows stray light wherever light would otherwise scatter and blur the image. Its muscles, all crowded into the front third, run the two great adjustments of daily vision, the pupil's shutter and the focusing pull on the lens, whose wiring we followed in episodes five and eight. Its middle section runs the factory, the aqueous. Its back section runs the pantry and the radiator. And all of it shares one blood supply plan, one venous exit strategy, and one autonomic control system, with sympathetic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion riding along the arteries to steady the flow. It is one coat because it is one job: to serve the delicate neural layer with light control, nutrition and climate, from the pupil margin to the optic nerve. Try to peel any one part away and you would have to reinvent it, because the neural eye has nowhere else to shop.

Host: You have earned a party trick, then. Give me the whole blood route in one breath, from the heart to the cavernous sinus. I want to hear how short it really is.

Guide: Here it is. Blood leaves the heart, climbs the internal carotid artery, steps off into the ophthalmic artery, and splits three ways. Short posterior ciliary arteries pierce the sclera around the optic nerve and own the choroid in wedges. Two long posterior ciliary arteries run forward in the suprachoroidal plane and skip every station until the ciliary body. Anterior ciliary arteries ride the straight muscles forward. All three consignments meet and lace together in the major arterial circle of the iris at the front door of the coat, which feeds the ciliary processes, sends radial spokes into the iris, and drops recurrent branches back across the anterior choroid. Then the blood gathers into veins, sweeps backward, spirals into four vortex veins a few millimetres behind the equator, pierces the sclera, and empties into the ophthalmic veins and the cavernous sinus. In, through, and out. One loop, one coat.
