This is that spotlight, episode five, Iris Muscles, the forty-fifth episode of the series. Series 1 gave the whole front of the eye one friendly tour in the episode The Front of the Eye, and now we slow all the way down, because the two muscles hiding in this thin, colorful curtain are some of the most elegant and most clinically loaded anatomy you will ever own. Welcome, Guide. Walk us in.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome, especially to anyone who has caught themselves staring at a patient's pupil this week and wondering what actually moves it. Here is the frame for today. The iris is a living diaphragm, a curtain with an adjustable hole in the middle, and it contains exactly two muscles to do every bit of the adjusting. They sit in different layers, they answer to different halves of the autonomic nervous system, and their geometries are opposite: one is a ring that squeezes, the other is a fan that spreads. Our plan holds three questions in order. First, where exactly does each muscle sit, layer by layer, because in anatomy position is destiny. Second, whose nerves drive each one, and along what route those nerves actually travel to reach a muscle buried inside the eye. Third, how does the geometry turn nerve impulses into pupillary movement. One promise before we start: the pupil itself, its sizes through life and its little surface landmarks, is next episode's business. Today belongs entirely to the muscles.

Host: Then start with the one everyone can picture, the muscle right at the edge of the hole. What is its name, and what kind of muscle are we dealing with?

Guide: It is called the sphincter pupillae, which is Latin for the purse string of the pupil, and the name is almost a picture. It is smooth muscle, so let me unpack that phrase first, because smooth means something specific. Muscle in the body comes in three flavors. Skeletal muscle moves your limbs and obeys your will. Cardiac muscle pumps the heart. Smooth muscle is the involuntary workhorse: no stripes under the microscope, no conscious control, driven entirely by the autonomic nervous system. The walls of the gut, the blood vessels, the airways, all smooth muscle, and so is the sphincter pupillae. Now the position. It is a narrow circular band, about one millimetre across, embedded in the deepest part of the stroma, the connective tissue body of the iris that episode four mapped, and it hugs the pupillary margin like a ring of stitching around the hole. Its fibers run circumferentially, around the clock face of the pupil, so when every fiber shortens at once, the whole ring tightens and the opening shrinks. The cells are joined to their neighbors by tiny bridges that let them contract as one continuous band, with no tendon and no bone, anchoring directly into the stroma they move against. And like any living muscle it needs plumbing: it sits richly supplied, between the two arterial circles of the iris, the major circle out at the iris root and the minor circle near the collarette, that landmark from episode four.

Host: You hinted earlier that this muscle is a bit of a rebel. Where does it come from? I assumed all muscle rolls off one standard embryonic assembly line.

Guide: Most muscle does, and this is where the sphincter earns its reputation. Nearly all the muscle in your body, skeletal and cardiac and smooth alike, is built from mesoderm, the middle embryonic layer, the sheet that also builds bone, blood, and connective tissue. The sphincter pupillae is not. It differentiates from neuroectoderm, the outer embryonic sheet that also builds your brain and your retina, specifically from the margin of the optic cup, the embryonic pocket whose inner wall becomes the sensory retina. Let that sit for a second: this is a smooth muscle that, in lineage terms, is a close cousin of the retina. As the optic cup margin folds forward to become the iris, cells at the pupillary rim of that margin transform from epithelial citizens into muscle workers, while their neighbors continue building pigment. This odd parentage is not just trivia for examinations, though it is certainly that. It is the first clue to a pattern we will complete in a few minutes: the iris does its contracting with tissue borrowed from the nervous system's own embryonic layer, unlike every other mover attached to the eye.

Host: So the wires come next. Whose nerves tell this purse string to tighten, and how do they even reach it, buried inside the iris?

Guide: The sphincter answers to the parasympathetic arm of the autonomic nervous system, so let me lay that system out once, because we will use it twice today. Autonomic means the involuntary control network for organs, and it has two arms that mostly oppose each other. The parasympathetic arm handles quiet, conserving, housekeeping tasks. The sympathetic arm handles urgency and arousal. Now the route, and it is a beautiful piece of anatomy. The command to constrict begins in a cluster of cell bodies in the midbrain called the Edinger-Westphal nucleus. From there the fibers travel out along the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve, riding in its inferior division, and they leave that nerve in its branch to the inferior oblique, one of the muscles that turns the eye. Then comes the relay: those fibers peel off as the motor root of the ciliary ganglion, a pinhead-sized switchboard tucked deep in the orbit, between the optic nerve and the lateral rectus. A ganglion, remember, is a relay station where the first fiber hands the message to a second fiber, and parasympathetic traffic to the eyeball always changes drivers here. The new fibers stream forward in the short ciliary nerves, pierce the back of the iris, and arrive at the sphincter. One sharing arrangement worth noting: the same relay and the same short ciliary nerves also feed the focusing muscle inside the ciliary body. Series 1 told that focusing story in the episode Accommodation, and the muscle's own anatomy is reserved for episode eight, Ciliary Muscle.

Host: Now the payoff question. When that signal lands and the purse string tightens, what physically happens to the iris, and how fast?

Guide: What happens is miosis, the proper word for pupillary constriction. Mechanically it is lovely to watch in your imagination. The ring shortens, the circumference of the opening decreases, and the hole narrows. The iris tissue caught between the shrinking margin and the fixed root cannot simply vanish, so it buckles into radial folds, tiny pleats running outward like the creases of a drawn curtain closing. Speed matters clinically, so here are honest numbers: constriction to a bright light begins within about a quarter to half a second and is essentially complete within a second or two. The sphincter is the stronger and faster of the two iris muscles, and when both receive conflicting orders, the ring wins in bright conditions. The same muscle is recruited a second way, without any light at all, whenever the eyes turn inward to look at something close. That near answer, constriction together with convergence and focusing, is one coordinated triad, and Series 1 walked through its geometry in the episode Convergence. Muscle anatomy is what makes the third leg of that triad possible: one shared relay sends a single message that lands on the sphincter and the focusing muscle at the same time.

Host: On to the opponent then. Where does the dilator pupillae hide, and what is it actually made of?

Guide: Here is the strangest muscle in the eye, because strictly speaking it is not a separate muscle at all. Behind the stroma, the iris carries two thin curtains of epithelium, two stacked sheets of pigment-rich cells, arranged apex to apex, their pointed inner surfaces facing each other like two hands pressed palm to palm. The front sheet is the one that matters for us, because its cells are modified into myoepithelial cells, and those cells ARE the dilator pupillae. Each one has an epithelial body, an anchoring part parked inside the sheet, and from that body a muscular arm, a slender contractile process, slides forward into the deepest stroma. Every arm is oriented radially, like spokes of a wheel, running from the iris root at the angle toward the pupil. So picture it: a sheet one cell thick whose thousands of muscular arms grip the stroma from behind. When the arms shorten, they drag the pupillary margin outward toward the root, pulling the curtain open. That is mydriasis, dilation, the opposite movement produced by an anatomically opposite design. And notice what this position predicts: the dilator can only pull as far as the margin's attachment to the epithelium allows, which is why extreme dilation gathers the iris into thick folds near its root.

Host: Stop there, because you used a word that deserves its own moment. Myoepithelial. Unpack it. What is a cell that is half epithelium and half muscle doing in the eye?

Guide: A myoepithelial cell is a genuine hybrid, and the eye owns one of the body's rare flocks of them. The cell body remains a proper epithelial cell: it sits in its sheet, it is heavily pigmented, it holds onto its neighbors and keeps the barrier intact. But the anterior face of the cell has grown contractile machinery, actin and myosin, the standard protein pair of muscle contraction, packed into those muscular arms we just described. The closest cousins in the whole body are the myoepithelial cells wrapped around sweat glands, which squeeze droplets toward the skin surface. And here is the lineage punchline: the dilator, like the sphincter, comes from neuroectoderm, that outer embryonic sheet. So both iris muscles are ectodermal, which is nearly unheard of anywhere else. Compare the eye's other movers. The extraocular muscles, the ones that rotate the globe, are mesodermal, and their full anatomy comes in Season 9. The ciliary muscle is built from neural crest mesenchyme, yet another embryonic stream, as episode eight will tell. The iris alone carries muscle born from the nervous system's own layer, and examiners have loved asking about it for exactly that reason.

Host: Now the wiring of that spoke fan. Whose nerves drive the dilator, and what route do they take to reach it?

Guide: The dilator answers to the sympathetic arm, and this is the classic three-neuron chain every optometrist should be able to draw from memory. Neuron one lives in the hypothalamus, deep in the brain, a control center for drives, temperature, and arousal. Its fiber runs down through the brainstem and the neck of the spinal cord to reach the ciliospinal center of Budge, a column of cells in the upper chest region of the cord, segments labeled C8 to T2. Neuron two starts there. Its fiber exits the cord and climbs back up the sympathetic chain, the necklace of ganglia running alongside the spine, to the superior cervical ganglion, the big switchboard at the top of the neck beside the carotid artery. Neuron three starts there. Its fiber wraps onto the internal carotid artery, rides it into the head, slips through the ciliary ganglion without changing drivers, and runs forward in the long ciliary nerves to the dilator. You have met that road before: Season 4, episode seven, Corneal Nerves, where the same long ciliary nerves carried the cornea's sensation. Same highway, different cargo. Notice the elegant symmetry with the sphincter: short ciliary nerves deliver parasympathetic traffic to the constrictor, long ciliary nerves deliver sympathetic traffic to the dilator. Two roads, two names of nerves, one aperture between them.

Host: So what happens when that sympathetic road is cut somewhere along its very long journey? I have heard the name Horner syndrome attached to exactly this.

Guide: Horner syndrome is the sympathetic chain interrupted, and the anatomy predicts every finding before you ever see it. Cut the road anywhere from the hypothalamus down to the nerve endings, and the dilator loses its drive. The sphincter, still healthy and still holding its background tone, is suddenly unopposed, so the pupil constricts and stays small: that is the miosis of Horner syndrome. The same sympathetic highway feeds Muller's muscle, the small smooth muscle in the upper lid that provides the last couple of millimetres of lift above the main levator, so its paralysis adds a mild droop, the ptosis. We met that lid machinery back in Season 1, episode eight, Eyelid Muscles. And if the interruption sits low enough, below the branches that leave for the face, you can even add absence of sweating, anhidrosis, on the same side. The chain is long, passing from brain through neck to chest, which is why the possible culprits range from a tumor at the apex of the lung pressing the chain low in the neck, to injury along the carotid artery higher up. Where the other signs cluster tells you the level. No mystery, no memorized list: three anatomical consequences of one severed road, and a clinician who sees a small pupil with a droopy lid is literally reading this three-neuron map off the patient's face.

Host: Put the two muscles side by side for me now. One ring, one fan. How does this geometry actually produce the whole range of pupillary movement?

Guide: Think of them as opponents built into the same curtain. The sphincter is the purse string: it shortens the ring and closes the hole. The dilator is the spoke fan: it pulls the pupillary edge outward and opens the hole. Neither muscle needs to be powerful, because they are fighting over an opening a few millimetres across, but the arrangement gives exquisite, graded control. And there is an asymmetry worth owning. Constriction is the active, brisk, dominant movement, while dilation is partly passive. When the sphincter relaxes, the elastic stroma of the iris, which had been compressed into those radial folds, simply recoils, and the pupil reopens on its own, with the dilator's arms adding active pull on top. That is why the pupil snaps shut within a second of a bright light and then reopens gradually, drifting back over several seconds, never in a snap. Watch a patient come out of a bright penlight and you can see the fan and the elasticity sharing the work. Two geometries, two speeds, one aperture: every pupillary movement you will ever observe is some blend of that purse string and that fan.

Host: I want to add one more everyday piece: tone. What are these muscles doing when nothing special is happening, when a patient just sits quietly in your chair?

Guide: They are never idle, and this is the quiet key to the whole system. At every moment both arms of the autonomic system are firing at some background level, and the pupil size you see is the running balance of the two pulls. In ordinary room light, parasympathetic tone to the sphincter holds the aperture at its resting size while the dilator idles softly against it. Dim the room, and sympathetic output rises, the dilator takes the argument, and the hole widens. Scare the patient, hurt them, flood the bloodstream with adrenaline, and the sympathetic arm surges and the pupil blooms wide. And it runs the other way in sleep, when parasympathetic tone dominates and pupils settle small. It is worth saying plainly: the aperture you are watching is a live readout of two nerve tones, second by second. What those sizes should be at each age, and what counts as abnormal, is precisely the story of the next episode, so we leave the numbers there. Today's job is the mechanism, not the menu.

Host: Can you actually see these muscles working? If a student is at the slit lamp tomorrow, what does the movement look like, live?

Guide: More than you might expect, and knowing the layers tells your eye where to look. During brisk constriction, watch the pupillary margin move centripetally and look for the radial folds the compressed stroma throws into, the pleats of the drawn curtain. During dilation, watch the margin retreat toward the root and the iris surface flatten as those folds smooth out, the fan pulling the cloth taut. The speed difference we described is directly visible: closure is a blink, reopening is a drift, and timing that drift is an old clinical skill precisely because it reflects sphincter relaxation, elastic recoil, and dilator pull all at once. The landmarks from episode four help you anchor all of this: the collarette sits out there like a suture line, and you can judge how far the margin has retreated toward it, or toward the root beyond it. Even the crypts, those little gaps in the anterior border layer, move with the fabric. The point for tomorrow's clinic is simple: pupillary movement is not a black box with a number inside. It is two layers of tissue, in known positions, doing visible things you can now name.

Host: Now the reflex every optometrist has performed thousands of times. You swing a light into one eye and both pupils constrict. What anatomy makes that consensual trick possible?

Guide: The trick is bilateral wiring above the muscles. Trace it briefly. Light into one eye produces nerve traffic that leaves that eye in the optic nerve and climbs toward the midbrain, arriving at the pretectal area, a small processing station at the midbrain's roof. There the pathway splits and sends signals to BOTH Edinger-Westphal nuclei, left and right, not just to the one serving the lit eye. From each nucleus, the efferent route is the one we walked earlier: third nerve, inferior division, ciliary ganglion, short ciliary nerves, sphincter. So the lit eye constricts, the direct response, and the fellow eye constricts with it, the consensual response, because both switchboards received the same alarm. That symmetry is diagnostically precious, because comparing the direct and consensual responses tells you whether trouble sits in front of the midbrain or behind it, but the full reflex arc, afferent limb to efferent limb with every branch, is reserved for Season 10, episode seven, Pupillary Pathway. The muscle side of the story is today's. And for the testing choreography itself, Series 1 showed you the whole examination in the episode Pupil Examination. Now when you perform it, you can watch two named muscles answering behind the light.

Host: Let us talk about the drops, because every optometrist who dilates a patient to view the fundus is pharmacologically reaching into exactly this anatomy.

Guide: And the anatomy tells you exactly what each drop does, because each muscle carries its own chemical docking sites, receptors, tuned to its own nerve messenger. The sphincter runs on muscarinic receptors, the docks for the parasympathetic messenger acetylcholine. The dilator runs on alpha adrenergic receptors, the docks for the sympathetic messenger family, the adrenaline family. So take tropicamide, the standard dilating drop: it blocks the sphincter's muscarinic docks, the purse string slackens, and the aperture opens wide for your view of the fundus. Take phenylephrine: it stimulates the dilator's alpha docks directly, and the fan pulls the margin outward. Take pilocarpine, the opposite direction: it fires the sphincter's muscarinic docks, and the pupil constricts and holds, useful when you want that purse string keeping the peripheral iris pulled away from trouble. Atropine does what tropicamide does but for days, which is why it is reserved for specific jobs. One safety note comes straight from our own season: wide dilation gathers iris tissue toward its root, and in an eye whose angle is already narrow, that crowd can compromise the drainage machinery we mapped in episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle, the trabecular meshwork and the canal of Schlemm. The anatomy warns you before the pressure ever rises.

Host: One more clinical picture before we close the loop. You called the ciliary ganglion a relay. What happens when the relay itself is damaged?

Guide: Then you meet the tonic pupil, the classic Adie pupil. Damage at or just behind the ciliary ganglion, among those postganglionic fibers, knocks out the sphincter's parasympathetic supply in a patchy way, and the anatomy shows on examination. The pupil is large, because the unopposed dilator and elastic recoil now rule the aperture. It reacts poorly to light, and where it reacts at all, the movement is segmental: you see little worm-like contractions along the pupillary margin, patches of sphincter still connected to their nerves firing while their denervated neighbors stay still. Ask the patient to look at something near, and the response is oddly better, because near effort recruits whatever surviving fibers remain, but it is sluggish and slow, with a slow tonic redilation afterward. Every one of those signs is a direct consequence of where the lesion sits on today's map. The pupil becomes a living lesson in what the ciliary ganglion does, by showing you life without it. And the third nerve itself, if injured along its course, carries the same message in reverse: a wide pupil with a droopy lid and a turned eye, a story Season 10, episode nine, Cranial Nerves in Ocular Function will own.

Host: Guide, give me the recap. If a listener walks into clinic tomorrow and wants to carry today's anatomy with them, what is the shape of it?

Guide: Carry it as one curtain, two opponents, two roads. The curtain: the iris, whose stroma, anterior border layer, crypts, and collarette we mapped in episode four, Iris Anatomy. The opponents: first the sphincter pupillae, a circular band of smooth muscle about one millimetre wide in the deep stroma at the pupillary margin, born from neuroectoderm like its partner, joined into one contracting ring, and driven by parasympathetic fibers from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, along the third nerve, through the ciliary ganglion, out in the short ciliary nerves. Second the dilator pupillae, not a discrete muscle but a sheet of myoepithelial cells in the front epithelial layer, radial arms gripping the stroma from the root toward the pupil, driven by sympathetic fibers along the three-neuron chain: hypothalamus, ciliospinal center of Budge at C8 to T2, superior cervical ganglion, then along the internal carotid artery and out in the long ciliary nerves. The geometry: a purse string and a spoke fan, constriction active and brisk, dilation partly passive and slower, the resting aperture a running balance of two live tones. And the payoffs: Horner syndrome as the cut sympathetic road, the tonic pupil as the failed relay, and the dilating drops as receptor geography you can now name. That is the complete machine.
