# Description: Behind the colored iris hides a ring of tissue that makes the fluid filling the front of the eye and anchors the vascular coat of the globe. In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we map the gross anatomy of the ciliary body: the pleated pars plicata with its roughly seventy ciliary processes, the smooth pars plana, the width numbers worth memorizing, and the uveal root attachment that ties iris, ciliary body and choroid into one continuous coat. Expect real millimetres, plain-language explanations, and the anatomical reasons behind familiar clinical signs. Made for optometry students and practicing optometrists who like their anatomy taught clearly and for the ear.

Host: Let us start with the name, because it has always struck me as odd. Ciliary, as in cilia, as in eyelashes. Is there something lash-like about this structure? And while you are untangling that, tell me precisely where it sits inside the eye.

Guide: The name is a small fossil of an old observation. Cilium is Latin for eyelid or eyelash, and the early anatomists who studied this structure saw a front edge fringed with tiny ridges, like a fine row of lashes, and the name stuck. Now the location. The ciliary body is the middle third of the uveal tract, and I should unpack that word, because it is the key to the whole season. The uveal tract is the middle vascular coat of the eye, a continuous sleeve of richly blood-supplied tissue lying between the tough outer coat and the inner neural coat. It has three stations: the iris in front, the ciliary body in the middle, and the choroid behind. Our structure is the connective piece. It sits inside the globe just behind the limbus, the border zone where the clear cornea hands over to the white sclera, which we mapped at the start of season four in Corneal Location and Dimensions. Anteriorly it begins at the root of the iris and at a little ridge called the scleral spur. Posteriorly it ends at a jagged boundary called the ora serrata, where it hands over to the choroid. So picture it: a ring, tucked behind the iris, pressed against the inside of the wall.

Host: A ring. So there is one in each eye, and it runs the whole way around with no buckle and no beginning. Give me the dimensions. How wide is this collar?

Guide: One per eye, a complete annulus, no start and no finish. If you could lift the iris away and look from the front, you would see a ruffled collar surrounding the lens. As for width, textbooks put the whole ciliary body at about six millimetres, measured on the outer aspect from the region of the scleral spur back to the ora serrata. And it is not perfectly even, which is a detail worth keeping. It is a little narrower on the nasal side, close to five point nine millimetres, and wider on the temporal side, about six point seven. Why should a clinician care about a millimetre here or there? Because these millimetres are landmarks. When a retinal surgeon measures backward from the limbus, the first six millimetres of that journey are ciliary body territory, and the exact widths tell them where the quiet flat zone ends and other structures begin.

Host: Here is a question I have wanted to ask all season. We keep mapping structures that live behind the iris. Can we actually see any of this in a living eye, or is the ciliary body forever hidden from us at the chair?

Guide: Mostly hidden, with one proud exception. The front slope of the ciliary body, the part that forms the back wall of the angle, is directly visible with gonioscopy, the lens-and-mirror examination of the angle we learned in episode three. That pale band just behind the scleral spur is called, with no mystery at all, the ciliary body band, and it is today's structure announcing itself. Beyond that, the pleated territory behind the iris is not directly visible in the intact living eye; we meet it indirectly, through ultrasound imaging of the anterior segment, which draws the ring and its processes beautifully, or when a surgeon looks inside. So for a clinician, the ciliary body is mostly an inferred structure: known by its band in the angle, by the fluid it makes, and by what happens to the eye when it misbehaves.

Host: You keep saying ring, and I keep hearing flat numbers, but a ring is a three-dimensional thing. If we cut a slice, one radial wedge, the way you would cut a piece from a cake, what shape would we actually see?

Guide: That slice is called a meridional section, a cut along a radius of the ring, from the center of the eye outward, exactly like a cake wedge. And the answer is one of the most useful pictures in anterior segment anatomy: the ciliary body is a triangle. The base of the triangle faces forward, toward the angle of the anterior chamber. The outer side lies against the inside of the sclera, and between the two runs the supraciliary space, a potential space, which means in the living eye it is normally collapsed to a cleft thinner than a hair, bridged by delicate strands of tissue, but it can open into a real space if fluid or blood collects there. The inner side of the triangle faces the lens and the vitreous. And the apex points backward, tapering away toward the ora serrata. The wedge shape makes sense once you think about it: the uveal coat is broad and anchored up front, and becomes a thin membrane behind, so the middle third simply tapers between the two. And notice something else. The front slope of this triangle is the back wall of the angle we walked in episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle. I am not retreading that ground, just pointing: the band we saw there is today's structure seen from the front.

Host: Good, the triangle is in my head now. You said the width divides into two parts, and I have a feeling the names are going to be Latin geography again. Give me the two regions.

Guide: Two regions, both Latin, both plain once translated. The front strip, about two millimetres wide, is the pars plicata, which simply means the pleated part. Older texts call it the corona ciliaris, the ciliary crown, because its fringed edge reaches forward around the lens. The back strip, about four millimetres wide, is the pars plana, the flat part, also called the orbiculus ciliaris, the little ring. Two plus four makes our six. The pleated front strip carries the folds that give the whole structure its name. The flat back strip is quieter, smoother, and, as we will see, clinically important in a completely different way.

Host: Then let us spend a minute on the pleats themselves, the ciliary processes. What are they, how many are there, and why does the body bother folding this tissue at all?

Guide: The ciliary processes are about seventy major ridges, seventy to eighty in most eyes, each roughly two millimetres long, running radially like the pleats of a pleated skirt, and in the valleys between them sit smaller intermediate processes, so the whole surface is a ruffle. Each process is a tiny structure with a clear recipe. A core of loose stroma packed with capillaries whose walls carry little windows, fenestrated, leaky by design. And around that core, a sleeve of two epithelial layers. Why fold it at all? For the same reason the small intestine is lined with villi: folds multiply surface area. And area here is not decoration, because this ruffled sleeve is the waterworks of the eye. The inner epithelial layer actively secretes aqueous humor, the clear fluid we have been following all season. Born on these folds, it fills the posterior chamber we mapped in episode two, flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber of episode one, and leaves through the angle we walked in episode three. I am summarizing that journey on purpose; each leg already has its own episode. One more spatial note: the lens equator hovers just in front of the ruffle, so the maze of little gaps between the processes, the zonules and the lens is, in large part, the posterior chamber itself.

Host: You said the epithelium secretes that fluid, and I remember from earlier episodes that the aqueous is oddly protein-free, almost like distilled water compared with blood. Where does that refining actually happen?

Guide: At the epithelium, and specifically at the seams between its cells. The inner, non-pigmented layer of the ciliary epithelium is sealed by tight junctions, molecular gaskets that run all the way around each cell's margin and force everything entering the aqueous to pass through the cells themselves, under their control, rather than leaking between them. Gasket logic: decide what passes, refuse the rest. Anatomists call this the blood-aqueous barrier, and this is its principal seat. The windowed capillaries let fluid out easily, but the refined final product is made at the epithelium, so the aqueous stays nearly protein-free and crystal clear. The outer, pigmented layer, the one loaded with dark pigment, sits behind its partner as support and continuation, and remember from episode four that this double sheet keeps riding forward onto the iris. And here is the anatomy behind a familiar chair-side sign. When inflammation strikes the iris or the ciliary body, those gaskets break down, protein pours through, and the beam of the slit lamp lights it up as flare. The sign you record is a barrier failing.

Host: Now the quiet partner, the pars plana. Four millimetres of flat territory at the back. What is it actually for?

Guide: Three jobs, all worth knowing. First, it is a highway. Many of the zonular fibers, the fine strands that will sling the lens, take their origin from the inner surface of the pars plana and from the valleys between the processes, then run forward to the lens equator. The full story of those fibers belongs to season six, Lens and Accommodation Anatomy, so I will leave the signpost there. Second, it is an anchorage for the vitreous. The gel is welded to the wall in a band a few millimetres wide straddling the ora serrata, reaching about two millimetres forward onto the pars plana and a few millimetres back onto the peripheral retina. That vitreous base is the strongest vitreous attachment in the eye, which is why peripheral retinal tears cluster around the ora, where the gel tugs hardest, and why trauma that shakes the gel tends to hurt exactly that frontier. Third, and this surprises students, it is a surgical doorway. Because the pars plana lies safely behind the lens and still in front of the retina, instruments enter the vitreous cavity through it, classically about three and a half to four millimetres behind the limbus in an eye that still carries its natural lens. Behind the lens, ahead of the retina: that is the corridor.

Host: Before we leave the flat part, you said something in passing that I want to make explicit. The retina continues forward onto the ciliary body? I thought the retina stopped at the back of the eye.

Guide: The light-catching neural retina stops, but its epithelium does not. At the ora serrata the retina's two epithelial sheets carry on forward over both parts of the ciliary body, and histologists call this forward continuation the pars ciliaris retinae, the ciliary part of the retina. There are two layers, and the order matters. An outer pigmented layer rests against the stroma. An inner non-pigmented layer faces the aqueous, and that is the secreting layer we just discussed. Follow them backward and the pigmented layer becomes the retinal pigment epithelium, while the non-pigmented layer becomes the neural retina. Follow them forward and they ride onto the iris, as we heard in episode four. Why does this matter clinically? Because a retinal detachment is the neural retina peeling away from its pigment layer. Over the ciliary body the two sheets stay welded to each other, so fluid creeping under the retina does not simply flood forward around the entire ring. The seam holds, and that seam is today's structure.

Host: Anchoring, then. We have a vascular ring with fluid-filled spaces on either side, pressed lightly against the wall. What actually holds it in place? And I have heard the phrase uveal root used like a landmark. Where does that fit?

Guide: Two answers, front and back, and they are beautifully different. At the front, the root of the iris. The iris does not float; it springs from the anterior surface of the ciliary body, and that junction is what anatomists call the uveal root. It is the thinnest, most fragile attachment anywhere in the anterior uvea, which is why a blunt blow, a squash ball, a champagne cork, a fist, can tear the iris away from it. The injury has its own name, iridodialysis, and it looks exactly like what it is: a second dark gap at the iris periphery where the fabric ripped off its mooring. Now the ciliary body itself. It is anchored firmly to the sclera in essentially one place, at the scleral spur, that ridge at the back of the angle we used as a landmark in episode three. Everywhere else it is only loosely tied to the sclera by fine strands crossing the supraciliary space. Strong at the spur, loose everywhere behind. That arrangement explains a striking clinical picture: when the pressure inside the eye falls very low, after surgery or with severe inflammation, the uveal coat can balloon away from the wall as a ciliochoroidal detachment, while the spur anchor holds the front of the coat in place.

Host: And at the back? When the ciliary body hands over to the choroid at the ora serrata, is there a seam, a border, a door frame, or does one simply melt into the other?

Guide: No border in substance; the seam is continuous, only jagged in outline. The name ora serrata means saw edge, and the serrations are real: little forward-pointing teeth, called dentate processes, alternating with rounded bays, and the teeth are typically more pronounced on the nasal half of the eye. Functionally, though, there is no break. Vessels run through, fibers run through, the epithelial sheets run through. The uveal tract is one coat, and the ciliary body is its middle panel. In fact, this season is built around that continuity: episode ten, Complete Uveal Tract, will stitch iris to ciliary body to choroid into the single coat they truly are, and follow the blood from one end to the other. Today we are simply mapping the middle panel carefully enough that the capstone will feel inevitable.

Host: Speaking of blood, let us follow the pipes. Where does all this vascular tissue get its supply, and where does the blood leave?

Guide: The supply is elegantly redundant. The ophthalmic artery sends two long posterior ciliary arteries, one nasal and one temporal, which run forward between the sclera and the uvea like two travelers hugging the wall. Near the front of the ciliary body they are joined by the anterior ciliary arteries, branches that ride in with the four rectus muscles; those muscles get a full season of their own later, so for now just see them as arterial couriers. All of these vessels anastomose, join hands, to form a complete ring called the major arterial circle of the iris, lying in the ciliary body near the iris root. Picture it: a ring within our ring. From this circle, branches feed the ciliary processes, the waterworks we described, and supply the iris, whose plumbing we have already covered in episodes four and five. The processes are generous recipients, receiving blood flow far out of proportion to their small mass, which makes sense for a tissue whose business is moving fluid. The venous return is equally simple in outline: venules collect from the processes and the ciliary body and drain backward into the vortex veins, four great veins that pierce the sclera on their way out. The choroid shares that drainage, so when we reach episode nine, Choroid, the plumbing will feel familiar.

Host: And the wiring? You have mentioned nerves twice now, and each time you have stopped yourself mid-sentence.

Guide: Because the full wiring diagram belongs to next week, but here is the honest outline. The ciliary body is richly innervated. The short ciliary nerves, tiny branches coming forward from the ciliary ganglion, a small relay station behind the eye, carry parasympathetic fibers whose job is to drive the ciliary muscle; that is the headline of episode eight and I will not steal it. The long and short ciliary nerves also carry sensory fibers, which run back through the nasociliary nerve to the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, the great sensory nerve of the face we already traced in season four when we mapped the corneal nerves. Sympathetic fibers travel along the vessels, as they do throughout the uvea. Why should an optometrist care about the sensory wiring? Because this is deeply innervated vascular tissue. When it inflames, a condition anatomists call cyclitis, the pain is deep, boring, and felt radiating across the brow, quite different from the gritty surface pain of the cornea. The anatomy explains the symptom.

Host: Let us bring all of this to the chair. An optometrist sees patients, not diagrams. What are the moments in practice where knowing today's map changes what you notice, or how you describe it?

Guide: Four moments, at least. First, gonioscopy. Recognizing the ciliary body band behind the scleral spur, and noticing when it looks wrong, is part of angle assessment; and a blunt blow that tears the ciliary body along its internal fault lines, an injury called angle recession, changes exactly that view. Those fault lines only make sense with the muscle map, which is why I am deliberately deferring the picture to next week. Second, naming inflammation. Anterior uveitis is named by anatomy: iritis when the iris leads, cyclitis when the ciliary body leads, iridocyclitis when both are lit up together. When you record cells and flare in the anterior chamber, you are describing this ring inflamed. Third, pressure. The aqueous is manufactured here, so a ciliary body shut down by inflammation or surgical trauma leaves the eye abnormally soft, a state called hypotony; and a cleft torn between the ciliary body and the scleral spur, a cyclodialysis, can drain fluid abnormally fast for the same reason. Fourth, trauma geography. The injuries land where the anatomy is weakest: the uveal root for the iris, the loose supraciliary plane for the coat itself. Anatomy is the map of where trouble prefers to happen.

Host: Walk me through the whole thing one more time, slowly, the way you would want a student to recite it before an exam.

Guide: Gladly. One ring, the middle third of the uveal tract, running from the root of the iris back to the ora serrata, about six millimetres wide, a touch wider on the temporal side. Slice it radially and it is a triangle: base at the angle, outer side against the sclera across the supraciliary space, inner side facing the lens and vitreous, apex pointing back to the saw edge of the ora. Two parts: a front pleated strip, the pars plicata, about two millimetres, carrying roughly seventy vascular processes wrapped in a double epithelium; that is where aqueous is born and where the blood-aqueous barrier sits. And a back flat strip, the pars plana, about four millimetres: zonule highway, anchorage of the vitreous base, and the surgical doorway about three and a half to four millimetres behind the limbus. Anchored to the wall only at the scleral spur, loose elsewhere, continuous behind with the choroid under a jagged outline. Fed by the long posterior and anterior ciliary arteries joining in the major arterial circle of the iris; drained backward through the four vortex veins; wired by the long and short ciliary nerves. That is the napkin.
