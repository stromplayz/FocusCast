# Description: Deep inside the front of the eye sits a ring of smooth muscle that every optometrist depends on daily. In this episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks opens the ciliary body to reveal its three fiber groups - longitudinal, radial, and circular - and traces their attachments from the scleral spur back to the ora serrata. Learn how the parasympathetic pathway through the ciliary ganglion and the short ciliary nerves drives this muscle, and how the zonules of Zinn carry its tension inward to the lens. A clear anatomy refresher for optometry students, practitioners, and anyone who loves the machinery of the eye.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, the follow-up series for listeners who know what the eye does and now want to know exactly how it is built. We are deep in season five, Anterior Chamber and Uveal Tract, and this is episode eight. Last episode, number seven, we mapped the ciliary body itself: the ridged pars plicata with its crown of ciliary processes, the flat pars plana behind it, and the uveal root where the parts of the vascular middle coat meet. Today we step inside that body to meet the engine buried in its front half, the ciliary muscle. We will name its three fiber groups, trace their anchors, follow its nerves and vessels, and finish with the cables that carry its pull to the lens. One promise before we begin: the full mechanics of focusing belong to season six. Today is pure muscle anatomy.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to our regulars. Let me place the muscle before we dissect it. The ciliary body, as we heard last episode, is the middle ring of the uveal tract, wedged between the iris in front and the choroid behind. From outside in: a loose laminated tissue called the supraciliary lamella, lying against the inner surface of the sclera; then the muscle itself; then the vascular stroma; and finally, facing the posterior chamber, the two epithelial layers and their glassy basement membrane. The muscle is the single largest component of the whole ciliary body, a collar of muscular tissue filling most of its front portion. It is smooth muscle, unstriated and involuntary, arranged as a ring, or annulus, encircling the eye just behind the root of the iris. Slice the front of the eye in half, like a cut through the pole of an onion, and the muscle appears as a compact triangular mass tucked against the wall.

Host: You said smooth muscle, and that word carries more weight than students give it. We all know skeletal muscle from the biceps, muscle we command at will. What actually changes when the muscle is smooth rather than striped?

Guide: Quite a lot changes, and it all matters clinically. Smooth muscle cells are spindle shaped, fatter in the middle with a single nucleus, and they lack the striped repeating machinery that lets skeletal muscle twitch quickly. What they trade in speed they gain in endurance. A smooth muscle can hold a steady contraction, a sustained tone, for a very long time without fatigue, which is exactly what an eye needs from a muscle that holds a quiet grip on the focusing system all day. It works under autonomic control, meaning you cannot command it by deciding to; the autonomic nervous system, the wiring that runs your heartbeat and your pupils, runs this muscle too. You met its cousins in episode five, Iris Muscles, where the sphincter pupillae and dilator pupillae told the same story of involuntary contractile tissue. The ciliary muscle is that family's largest member inside the eye.

Host: Now give us the cast of characters. The classic teaching is that this one muscle comes in three parts, described by the direction its fibers run. Name them and tell us where each sits.

Guide: The three parts are the longitudinal fibers, the radial fibers, and the circular fibers, usually listed from outside inward. Picture those triangles from the cut surface again, because each part forms one triangle. The outermost triangle, lying flat against the sclera, is the longitudinal part. The innermost triangle, folded into a ring facing the lens, is the circular part. Between them, running diagonally like the bracing struts between two walls, is the radial part. And they are not three separate muscles stitched together. The fibers curve continuously from one group into the next, so anatomists describe one muscle with three orientations, not three muscles. That continuity is why a contraction involves the whole structure, though each fiber group transfers its pull to its own set of anchor points. We will take them one at a time, from the surface inward.

Host: Start with the outermost group then, the longitudinal fibers. What do they look like, and where do they run?

Guide: The longitudinal fibers, also called meridional fibers, are the outermost layer, a thin flat sheet of muscle lying parallel to the inner surface of the sclera, like a strip of tape run front to back along the inside of the eye wall. Meridional simply means running like the lines of longitude on a globe, from front to back toward the pole. Each fiber begins in front at the scleral spur, which you should picture as a small inward-projecting wedge of sclera at the back edge of the angle, the recess where iris meets cornea. We mapped that angle thoroughly back in episode three, Anterior Chamber Angle, and the spur was its firm landmark. From the spur the fibers run backward for nearly the whole width of the ciliary body, about six millimeters, gradually tapering, and they end in fine tendons that fan into the suprachoroidea, the loose tissue plane between muscle and sclera, stopping near the ora serrata, the serrated frontier where the ciliary body hands over to the retina. This part carries its own name, Bruecke's muscle.

Host: You mentioned the spur and the angle together, and I can feel a connection coming. When these front-to-back fibers shorten, what do they actually tug on?

Guide: When the longitudinal fibers contract, they shorten and draw their anterior attachment, the scleral spur, backward toward their posterior ends. That pull tautens the delicate sheets of the trabecular meshwork, the spongy filtration tissue at the angle that the aqueous humor must seep through to leave the eye, because fine tendinous slips of this muscle reach forward into the meshwork and even toward the region of Schwalbe's line, the ridge where Descemet's membrane, the cornea's inner layer, ends. Tauten those sheets and the spaces between them open, so fluid drains more easily. It is a tidy anatomical handshake between the muscle of focusing and the drainage structures of episode three, and the anatomical reason certain drops that make this muscle work harder also lower the pressure inside the eye. I will leave the full drainage story in that earlier episode; today's point is simpler: this muscle is anchored to the eye's plumbing, not just to its optics.

Host: On to the middle layer. The radial fibers sound like the forgotten sibling, sandwiched between two more famous parts. Describe them for us.

Guide: The radial fibers, sometimes called oblique fibers, form the intermediate layer. They begin around the same anterior territory, near the spur and the front of the muscle, but instead of running straight back or straight around, they fan outward and backward diagonally, interweaving with the longitudinal fibers outside them and the circular fibers inside them. Think of the diagonal bracing in a timber fence, or the webbing that ties the outer and inner layers of a jacket: they couple the two principal layers and spread force between them. In a meridional section they appear as small triangular fascicles stepping from the outer triangle to the inner one. They are the least distinct of the three groups, and some older textbooks quietly fold them into the other two, but most modern descriptions keep them as a real intermediate stratum. Longitudinal, radial, circular: say that sequence, outside in, a few times today and it will never leave you.

Host: And now the innermost group, the one that gives the whole muscle its reputation. The circular fibers.

Guide: The circular fibers, also called annular fibers, are the innermost part, and here the muscle's orientation turns ninety degrees. These cells run around the eye, encircling the inner aspect of the front of the ciliary body, right behind the root of the iris and alongside the valleys between the ciliary processes. In cross section they form a compact band like a sphincter, a closing ring, and in the triangular cut surface they make up the small inner triangle whose apex points backward. This part has its own eponym too, Muller's annular muscle, and it sits closer to the lens equator than any other part of the muscle. Two anatomical facts about it are worth keeping. First, when it contracts, it narrows the diameter of that muscular ring, drawing the muscle's bulk inward toward the lens; what that does to zonular tension we will build to in a few minutes, and what it does to focusing is season six's story. Second, this part is not present in the newborn eye in any useful amount. It grows through early childhood, a reminder that even anatomy you assume is finished at birth is still under construction.

Host: You said the circular part is still under construction in childhood. Where does this muscle come from in the first place, and when does it reach its adult form?

Guide: The ciliary muscle differentiates from the same frontier tissue that builds much of the front of the eye: loose embryonic connective tissue derived largely from neural crest cells, the cell family that also constructs the cornea's inner layers and the angle's drainage structures. In the fetal eye the region is crowded with this undifferentiated mesenchyme, and muscle fibers begin to appear within it in the middle months of pregnancy, oriented first in the longitudinal direction. After birth, the front of the eye keeps remodeling: the angle structures mature, the chamber in front of the lens deepens, a story told with its numbers back in episode one, Anterior Chamber Anatomy, and the muscle grows with it. The circular part is the late developer, becoming recognizable through the early childhood years, and the whole apparatus approaches its adult geometry during the school years. That slow maturation is one more reason pediatric examination rhythms differ from adult ones: this anatomy is a moving target in a young patient, settling into its adult pattern only gradually.

Host: Let us put numbers on this ring, because this series lives on real dimensions. How big is the ciliary muscle, and how does it vary around the eye?

Guide: Use the ciliary body as your frame, because the muscle fills most of its anterior portion. The whole ciliary body ring is wider on the temporal side than the nasal side: roughly six millimeters of radial width temporally and about five nasally, with most sources giving a total range of about four and a half to six and a half millimeters. Of that width, the front two millimeters or so are the pars plicata, the ridged zone carrying the ciliary processes, and the flatter pars plana stretches nearly four millimeters behind it. The muscle occupies essentially all of the front of that ring and sends its longitudinal component sweeping back toward the ora serrata. So picture a collar of smooth muscle, thickest in front near the spur, thinning as it sweeps backward, completely encircling the eye behind the iris root. In a cut surface you see the three triangles: outer, middle, inner. That shape is exactly why the older literature describes the ciliary muscle as three interlocking triangles, an image that has survived every modern imaging technique and still earns marks in examinations.

Host: Zoom to the microscope now. What is this collar actually made of, cell by cell?

Guide: Under the microscope you see smooth muscle cells gathered into bundles called fascicles, each bundle wrapped in a sheath of connective tissue, with the whole muscle embedded in a stroma that is unusually rich in elastic fibers and in pigment-bearing cells called melanocytes. That pigmentation is why the ciliary body looks brown-gray in section. Between the fascicles run small vessels and nerve twigs we will trace shortly, and woven through stroma and tendons is that elastic network. The muscle cells anchor to one another and to the surrounding connective tissue through tiny junctions, so the force of every cell is funneled into the tendons we mapped earlier. The elastic fibers matter more than students expect, because they hold the answer to a question the muscle's design forces us to ask. If this muscle pulls, what pulls it back?

Host: That is exactly my next question. Skeletal muscles come in pairs, one to bend and one to straighten. Where is the ciliary muscle's opponent?

Guide: There is none, and that is the elegant part. No second muscle inside the eye unwinds it. Relaxation happens passively, through elastic recoil. The elastic fibers woven through the muscle's stroma, its tendons, and the suprachoroidea behind it are stretched like a drawn elastic band whenever the muscle shortens. When the nerve signal stops, the muscle cells let go, and the stored elastic energy drags the muscle back toward its resting position, re-tightening the zonular cables as it goes. So the design is one active element, the muscle, paired with one passive element, the elastic network. Keep that pairing in mind, because in a few minutes it becomes the key to how tension is delivered to the lens, and taken off it, without a single opposing muscle in sight.

Host: One more design question before the geography. In daily life, where does this muscle sit between its two extremes? Is it ever completely off?

Guide: It carries a low resting tone most of the time, a quiet baseline of partial activity from the autonomic system, much like the muscles that hold your posture. In an eye gazing at distance the muscle is close to relaxed, but never truly switched off, and in some people, especially the young, that baseline sits noticeably above zero, one reason an eye can lean toward near focus even when it is meant to be at rest. The moment that baseline matters most clinically is under a cycloplegic drop: with the receptors blocked, even the tone disappears, and what remains is the anatomy in its true passive state, elastic tension and taut zonules with no muscle in the way. What that state looks like optically is season six's territory. For now, file the idea that this muscle's default is a gentle readiness, not silence.

Host: Now walk me around the muscle geographically. What does it touch on every side? This series has taught me that relations explain complications.

Guide: Gladly, because the neighbors are a map of clinical possibilities. On its outer side lie the supraciliary lamella and the sclera; the potential gap between muscle and sclera is the supraciliary space, a quiet compartment where fluid can collect in disease. On its inner side, the muscle is covered by the vascular stroma and the two epithelial layers, and beyond them lies the posterior chamber, the small triangular space we mapped in episode two, Posterior Chamber Anatomy. In front, the muscle's territory runs up to the root of the iris and the bases of the ciliary processes, and the major arterial circle of the iris loops through this region, tucked between muscle and stroma. Behind, the longitudinal fibers and their tendons melt into the suprachoroidea along the choroid. And in the angle sit the trabecular meshwork and the canal of Schlemm, the eye's main drainage canal, from episode three. Every structure this muscle can tug on, it touches.

Host: Feeding time then. Where does the blood come from?

Guide: From a remarkable piece of plumbing called the major arterial circle of the iris, the circulus arteriosus iridis major. It is a complete ring of vessels lying in the ciliary body near the root of the iris, formed where the two long posterior ciliary arteries, arriving from behind on either side of the eye, anastomose, meaning they join and exchange flow, with branches of the anterior ciliary arteries, which arrive with the muscles that move the eyeball. From this circular highway, small branches radiate inward and outward, and the ciliary muscle is one of the chief customers, fed generously because a working muscle demands blood. On the exit side, veins gather the spent blood and carry it backward, ultimately leaving the eye through the vortex veins, usually four of them, which pierce the sclera behind the equator. The fuller story of how blood routes through the whole uveal tract is the capstone of this season, episode ten, Complete Uveal Tract, so I will hold the wider map for then.

Host: Now the wiring, which for this muscle is the headline. I remember from episode five, Iris Muscles, that the iris sphincter was driven by parasympathetic fibers. Is the ciliary muscle wired the same way?

Guide: Yes, and the pathway is worth walking slowly, because it appears in examinations constantly. The command starts in the midbrain, in a cluster of cells called the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, part of the parasympathetic division of the autonomic nervous system. From there the fibers travel out along the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve, the same nerve that carries most of the eye's movements. They branch off toward a tiny relay station sitting behind the eye in the orbit, the ciliary ganglion. A ganglion is simply a junction box outside the brain where one nerve cell hands the signal to the next; the fibers arriving are called preganglionic, and the fibers leaving are postganglionic. Those postganglionic fibers stream out in the short ciliary nerves, six to ten slender nerves that pierce the sclera in a ring around the optic nerve, then run forward through the suprachoroidal space to reach the ciliary muscle and the iris sphincter. At the muscle, the chemical messenger is acetylcholine, landing on muscarinic receptors, the receptor family that the drop atropine is famous for blocking. That is the whole circuit, midbrain to muscle, and every cycloplegic drop you will ever instill acts somewhere along it.

Host: Cycloplegic is a word worth unpacking right there, but first finish the wiring. Does anything else reach this muscle?

Guide: Two more fiber families arrive, though both play supporting roles. Sympathetic fibers, born in the superior cervical ganglion in the neck, ride up along the arteries into the orbit and reach the front of the eye mainly by way of the long ciliary nerves, branches of the nasociliary nerve from the first division of the trigeminal. Their clearest job is vasomotor, adjusting the tone of the blood vessels the muscle depends on, though the textbooks treat the parasympathetic supply as the true motor. The third family is sensory. Pain fibers from the ciliary body run back through the ciliary nerves to the nasociliary nerve, which explains why inflammation here produces that deep, boring ache that surges with every beat of the pulse. And here is where cycloplegics earn their name: cyclo means ciliary, plegia means paralysis. Drops such as atropine and cyclopentolate block those receptors and put the muscle to rest, which is why they widen the pupil, quiet an inflamed, aching ciliary body, and let a child's true resting refraction be measured, while drugs such as pilocarpine drive the muscle harder instead.

Host: Let me test my memory on the anchors before we move to the lens. Sum up the attachment map of this muscle, front to back.

Guide: A tidy summary, front to back. In front, the muscle grips the scleral spur, and beyond the spur it sends fine tendinous slips into the trabecular meshwork and up toward Schwalbe's line and the back of Descemet's membrane. Behind, the longitudinal fibers taper into posterior tendons that enter the suprachoroidea and fade out near the ora serrata, gripping the choroid along the way. Around it, connective tissue ties the muscle to the ciliary processes and to the stroma, and the elastic fibers weave it into that passive recoil system. Notice the asymmetry, because it is the whole point. The anterior anchor sits in sclera, one of the toughest, least elastic tissues in the body. The posterior anchor sits in the choroid, soft and elastic. So the muscle is strung between a rigid wall in front and a yielding membrane behind, like a hammock between one fixed post and one springy post. Everything it ever does begins from that arrangement of anchors.

Host: Which brings us to the part of today's lane I have been waiting for: the zonules. Between that muscular ring and the lens sits a system of fine fibers. Describe the zonular apparatus as anatomy, and save the optics.

Guide: The zonular apparatus, the zonule of Zinn, or by its older name the suspensory ligament of the lens, is a system of thousands of hair-fine fibers slinging the lens within the ring of the ciliary body. Each fiber is a bundle of microfibrils built from a protein called fibrillin, rope-like strands with a slight, useful stretch. They arise in two main territories: posterior fibers from the flat inner surface of the pars plana and from the valleys between the ciliary processes, and anterior fibers from the sides of the processes near their bases. They sweep inward and converge on the lens, inserting on its capsule in a band that straddles the equator, roughly a millimeter and a half in front of it and about a millimeter behind. Close to the capsule they split into a fine plexus, so the pull spreads evenly around the lens equator rather than tugging at points. Two anatomical truths to carry. The fibers cross the posterior chamber, so they live in the very space we drew in episode two. And they are passive: there is no muscle inside the zonule. They are cables, nothing more, and their tension is set entirely by the diameter of the ring they spring from.

Host: So the muscle and the zonules form a single mechanical unit: a ring that can change its diameter, and cables whose stretch depends on that diameter. Before we hand the mechanics to season six, give me the analogy that makes it stick.

Guide: Picture a winch, a set of guy ropes, and a tent pole. The ciliary muscle is the winch, mounted on the wall of the eye. The zonules are the guy ropes, running inward from the winch's rim to the lens at the center. When the muscle's circular part tightens the ring, the rim moves inward and the ropes slacken; when the muscle relaxes and the elastic system recoils, the rim settles back and the ropes pull taut on the lens. Tension reaches the lens only through those cables, and the lens, as you learned in Series 1's episode on Accommodation, changes its focusing power when tension on its capsule changes. The whole story of how the lens responds belongs to season six, Lens and Accommodation Anatomy, which opens with an episode called Lens Location and Relationships. What you own after today is the winch itself: three fiber groups, a rigid front anchor, an elastic rear anchor, and cables that make the coupling. Anatomy first, mechanics later: that is the discipline of this series.

Host: Here is a question patients effectively ask us in the chair without knowing it. Presbyopia. In Series 1's episode called Presbyopia we heard how arms get too short sometime in the forties. Anatomy day: is that the muscle wearing out?

Guide: That is the beautiful surprise: in presbyopia the muscle is largely innocent. Studies of the aged ciliary muscle show it can still contract; the machinery still answers the wiring. What stiffens is upstream: the lens and its capsule, which thicken and harden across the decades, so the same zonular slackening buys less change in lens shape. The muscle strains against an increasingly unyielding object. A small anatomical footnote, for honesty. With age the muscle does lose some volume, connective tissue increases between the fascicles, and the circular part in particular can thin, so the system is not untouched. But the primary failure is the lens, not the nerve, not the ganglion, and not the muscle's wiring. Hold that thought for the next time you explain presbyopia to a patient: their eye keeps trying, the material it pulls on has changed.

Host: One last clinical portrait before takeaways. What happens when this muscle will not let go? Patients describe eyes that ache after a long day of near work.

Guide: You are describing ciliary spasm, and the anatomy explains every feature of it. The muscle holds a sustained contraction beyond what the moment needs, often after hours of near effort, sometimes stirred up by medications that stimulate those receptors. The ring stays tightened, the zonules stay slack, and the eye is locked leaning toward near focus, so distance vision blurs until the muscle releases. Because a held contraction in smooth muscle still aches, the patient feels a deep, tired soreness, the sensory wiring we traced to the nasociliary nerve complaining loudly. Clinically, the escape hatch is the anatomy itself: a cycloplegic drop blocks the receptor, the muscle lets go, and the spasm breaks. The same logic is why strong cycloplegia is used to measure a child's resting refraction, taking the muscle out of the game for a while. The optical consequences live elsewhere in our library; today, notice only that every step of the story ran through anatomy we built in this episode.

Host: Then let us collect the takeaways. If a listener keeps only three things from this episode, what should they be?

Guide: Three things. First, the architecture: one smooth muscle with three orientations, longitudinal fibers running from the scleral spur back about six millimeters to the ora serrata, radial fibers bracing diagonally between the layers, and circular fibers making an inner ring beside the iris root, all continuous, all anchored to a rigid spur in front and elastic choroid behind. Second, the wiring and plumbing: parasympathetic drive from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus along the third cranial nerve, relayed in the ciliary ganglion, delivered by the six to ten short ciliary nerves, with the major arterial circle of the iris feeding the tissue and pain fibers reporting back through the nasociliary nerve. Third, the coupling: passive zonular cables rising from the pars plana and the valleys between the processes, slinging the lens equator, with no muscle of their own, so the ring's diameter sets their tension. Muscle, anchors, cables. If you can redraw those three triangles and label the anchors, you own this episode.
