# Description: Where does seeing actually begin in the brain? In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we reach the final station of the visual pathway: the striate cortex folded around the calcarine sulcus on the medial surface of the occipital lobe. Explore the line of Gennari, the layered input machinery, the point-to-point retinotopic map, and the striking principle of cortical magnification that hands the central ten degrees of vision more than half of the primary visual cortex. Then take a brief guided look beyond area one, toward the colour and motion areas of the extrastriate belt. Built for optometry students and practitioners who want anatomy they can lean on in the examining chair.

Host: Welcome back, old friend. Six episodes ago we set out from the back of the eye, and we have been travelling the great highway of sight ever since. We crossed at the chiasm, swept around the midbrain in the optic tract, relayed through the lateral geniculate body, and fanned out across the white matter of the hemisphere in the optic radiations. Today we finally arrive at the address every one of those fibres has been carrying toward: the visual cortex, tucked into the occipital lobe at the very back of the brain. This is Focus Cast by focuslinks, and this is episode six of our season on neuroanatomy for optometrists. Here is the plan for the hour. We will find the striate cortex and the calcarine sulcus it wraps around, learn why anatomists have given this one patch of grey matter so many names, walk the point-to-point map of the visual field across its surface, and then spend real time on the most surprising idea in the whole season, cortical magnification. Before we close, we will take one brief look beyond the primary area. To guide us, my patient teacher returns. Take us to the back of the brain.

Guide: Gladly. Let me set the frame before we open the doors. Everything we have studied so far this season has been transport. Nerve fibres, crossings, relays, fans of white matter. Beautiful, but still only delivery. The visual cortex is the first place in the entire pathway where those deliveries are actually unloaded, sorted, and worked on, and it is built anatomically like no other stretch of cortex. It carries a signature you can see with the naked eye in a fresh brain. It holds a distorted but exact map of the visual world drawn across its folds. And it spends its territory unevenly, favouring the centre of your vision with an extravagance that still startles students the first time they hear the numbers. Each of those three facts has a name, a location, and a clinical consequence, and that is exactly the order in which we will take them.

Host: Start with the location then. When you say the visual cortex sits in the occipital lobe, paint the picture for the listener who has never held a brain in both hands.

Guide: Picture the cerebrum from the side first, then rotate it so you are looking at the inner face of one hemisphere, the flat medial surface that faces its twin across the great longitudinal fissure. The occipital lobe is the rearmost lobe of the hemisphere, resting above the tentorium cerebelli, the tent-like sheet of dura that shelters the cerebellum underneath. On that medial surface, running roughly horizontally, you will find a deep and prominent groove called the calcarine sulcus. It begins close to the occipital pole, the very back tip of the hemisphere, and runs forward onto the medial surface, angling gently upward as it goes, passing beneath the splenium, the thick rear cushion of the corpus callosum. The name comes from calcar, the Latin word for spur, because this sulcus is pressed so deep into the hemisphere that it pushes up a little spur-shaped ridge into the medial wall of the occipital horn of the lateral ventricle. Anatomists call that ridge the calcar avis. Above the sulcus lies a triangular wedge of cortex called the cuneus, hemmed in between the parieto-occipital sulcus in front and the calcarine sulcus below. Below the sulcus lies the lingual gyrus, named for its tongue-like curl. And the walls of the calcarine sulcus, both banks of it, belong almost entirely to one structure: the primary visual cortex.

Host: Wait, one moment. You said the banks of the sulcus. So this cortex is not just lying on the surface where we could point at it. It is folded away inside a groove?

Guide: Almost all of it is, and that is worth pausing on, because it explains why so many students have never actually seen it. The greater part of the primary visual cortex is buried in the walls and depths of the calcarine sulcus, on the medial wall of the hemisphere, hidden from view unless you part the two hemispheres and look at the inner face. Like all cortex it is a sheet, only a couple of millimetres thick, but the sulcus folds that sheet back and forth on itself, packing a great deal of surface into a small volume, exactly the way a crumpled poster fits into a tube. The one exception to the hiding is at the occipital pole, where the cortex representing the very centre of vision spills over the back of the hemisphere and shows a little of itself on the lateral surface. Keep that exception in mind. It will matter when we talk about the map, and again when we talk about stroke.

Host: Now the names. V one, area seventeen, striate cortex, primary visual area. Why does one patch of cortex need a passport with four aliases?

Guide: Each name is a window from a different science, and you need all of them, because textbooks, lecturers, and papers use them interchangeably. Primary visual cortex is the functional name: it is the first cortical station to receive visual input, which is what primary means here. V one is the modern neuroscience label, simply the first in the numbered series of visual areas. Area seventeen comes from Korbinian Brodmann, the German anatomist who, in the early years of the twentieth century, divided the entire cerebral cortex into fifty-two numbered regions based on how their cells were stacked and stained, a method called cytoarchitecture. His numbering is still the common currency of cortical anatomy. And striate, my personal favourite, is the anatomist's name, and it points at something you can genuinely see. Francesco Gennari, an Italian medical student, noticed it in seventeen seventy six. Cutting through unstained brain tissue of this region, he spotted a pale stripe running within the grey matter, visible to the naked eye without any microscope or dye. That stripe became known as the line of Gennari, and any cortex that contains it is called striate, which simply means striped. No other area of the cortex carries it.

Host: A stripe you can see in fresh tissue with no microscope at all. What is that stripe actually made of?

Guide: Myelinated nerve fibres, axons wrapped in their fatty pale insulation, which glimmer in a fresh cut. To place it properly, I need to say a word about cortical architecture in general. The cerebral cortex, wherever you sample it, is organised into six horizontal layers, numbered one through six from the surface downward, each with its own cell types and its own business in the circuit. The primary visual cortex is an extreme and glorious version of this plan. It is classed as koniocortex, from a Greek word meaning dust, because it is densely packed with tiny granule cells. Its layer four, the great receiving layer, is enormous, and it is subdivided into sublayers four A, four B, and four C. The line of Gennari is the dense band of myelinated fibres lying in layer four B, an arrangement that older texts call the external band of Baillarger. It marks the zone where incoming fibres fan out sideways and where a huge amount of local processing is wired. The neighbouring visual areas, Brodmann area eighteen and area nineteen, lack this stripe, which is exactly why they are called extrastriate, beyond the stripe. Hold that pairing in your pocket: striate inside, extrastriate outside. It will map neatly onto our story at the end.

Host: So this is where the optic radiations finally plug in. Trace the arrival for me. The fibres we followed last time, where exactly do they land?

Guide: They land overwhelmingly in layer four C, the deepest subdivision of that great input layer, and the way they land is one of the elegant details of neuroanatomy. Back in episode four, the Lateral Geniculate Body, we saw that the relay has six layers: two magnocellular layers of large cells carrying fast information about motion and contrast, four parvocellular layers of small cells carrying fine detail and colour information, and the tiny koniocellular layers interleaved between them. Those streams stay segregated as they travel through the radiations, the journey we mapped in episode five, the Optic Radiations, and they remain segregated right up to the cortical doorstep. Magnocellular axons terminate chiefly in the upper half of layer four C, called four C alpha. Parvocellular axons terminate in the lower half, four C beta. The koniocellular stream reaches a little higher, into small clusters of cells within layers two and three. And once the signal is in, the cortex distributes it like a mail room. Layers two and three send their outputs forward into the extrastriate areas. Layer four B feeds the motion-processing region beyond. Layer five projects downward toward the superior colliculus and the brainstem. And layer six sends a massive feedback projection all the way back to the lateral geniculate body it came from. Arrival, sorting, and reply, layer by layer.

Host: Now the part I have been waiting for since you said the word map. Walk me across the surface. Where does the visual world actually lie on this sulcus?

Guide: This is the heart of the episode, so we will go slowly and build it rule by rule. The primary visual cortex is retinotopic, which means it preserves a point-to-point correspondence with the retina, and therefore with the visual field. Every patch of cortex corresponds to a specific location in the visual world, and the neighbourhood relationships are preserved, so a map of the field can be drawn onto the sulcus. Four rules describe the whole arrangement. Rule one: each hemisphere represents the opposite half of the visual field, the consequence of the crossing we studied back in episode two, the Optic Chiasm, where the nasal fibres decussated. Rule two: central vision lies behind and peripheral vision lies in front. The macula maps to the occipital pole, spilling onto the visible part of the hemisphere, while the increasingly peripheral parts of the field map progressively further forward, deeper along the calcarine sulcus. Rule three: the map is inverted, just as the retinal image is. The upper bank of the sulcus, the cuneus, represents the lower quadrant of the opposite field, and the lower bank, the lingual gyrus, represents the upper quadrant. Rule four: at the very front of the striate area, at the anterior tip along the sulcus, sits the representation of the far temporal periphery, the monocular crescent, that thin sliver of field that each eye sees alone, beyond where the nose blocks the partner eye. So the entire half of the visual world is laid out along one sulcus, pole to tip, inverted and orderly.

Host: I can hear the clinic knocking already. But say it out loud for us. Why should an optometrist who stares at visual field printouts all week care about a map on the medial wall of the occipital lobe?

Guide: Because that map is the decoder for every homonymous field defect you will ever chart. When you run a visual field test, and we learned the practical craft of that back in Series 1, in the episode called Basic Visual Field Testing, you are probing this cortical map from the outside in. Because the cortex preserves order so faithfully, a lesion here produces a defect that respects the vertical midline, and matches between the two eyes, which is what homonymous means: the same half of the field lost in both eyes, the same side, the same shape. The quadrant of the loss tells you the bank and the depth. A lesion in the upper bank steals the lower field quadrant. A lesion that strikes the pole attacks central vision first. A small infarct at the extreme anterior tip knocks out only the monocular crescent of the opposite eye, one of the most localising patterns in the whole of perimetry. The map is not trivia. It is the reason a field printout can point you to a postal address inside the brain, and the reason you, sitting in primary care, can tell a neurologist precisely where to look.

Host: One structural question before we turn clinical. There are two of these cortices, one in each hemisphere. Do they work in isolation, or do they know about each other?

Guide: They know about each other, and the connection is anatomical and specific. The two striate areas trade fibres through the splenium of the corpus callosum, the thick posterior bulb of the great commissure we mentioned earlier. And the trade is not random. The callosal fibres link the cortical representations that sit close to the vertical midline of the field, on either side of it, so that the two halves of the world, split at the midline by the chiasm, can be stitched into one seamless scene. It is one more example of a theme running through this whole season: every anatomical arrangement in the visual pathway is there to keep the world unified while it is being processed in parallel.

Host: Let us turn to blood supply then, because strokes are exactly how most of us meet this cortex in real life.

Guide: The striate cortex is supplied chiefly by the posterior cerebral artery, through its calcarine branch, which runs forward along the very sulcus it waters. But here is the pearl with real explanatory power. At the occipital pole, the territory of the posterior cerebral artery meets the farthest reach of the middle cerebral artery, and in many brains the tip of the pole receives an overlapping supply from both territories. Now lay that fact next to the map, because the pole is the macula. When the posterior cerebral artery occludes and the striate cortex infarcts, the patient wakes with a homonymous hemianopia, a loss of the opposite half of the field in both eyes, and yet the central few degrees often survive, because the pole is being fed by collaterals from the middle cerebral territory. That is macular sparing, and it is not a paradox, it is plumbing. The same logic scales up: when both posterior cerebral arteries fail and both occipital lobes infarct, the patient becomes cortically blind, with eyes that are healthy and pupils that react, and in a subset of these patients comes the strange companion, Anton syndrome, in which the cortically blind person genuinely does not know they cannot see. The anatomy locates the lesion, and the syndrome reminds us why: this cortex is the end of the line, and with no station beyond it, there is nothing left to report the failure.

Host: And now the idea I have been circling toward all evening, the one you promised would surprise me. Cortical magnification. The phrase sounds abstract. The reality, I suspect, is anything but.

Guide: Cortical magnification is the name for the fact that this cortex does not give the visual field a fair map. It gives the centre an enormous, deliberate excess. Here are the numbers, and I want you to let them land. The central ten degrees of the visual field, the territory served by the macula, occupies somewhere between fifty and sixty percent of the entire primary visual cortex. More than half of the cortical sheet, for ten degrees out of the whole panorama. And the gradient within that is steep. Near the fovea, a single degree of the visual field commands roughly five millimetres of cortical surface. Out at ten degrees from fixation, the same single degree claims only about one millimetre. So the cortex is not scaled to the world; it is scaled to the business end of the world. We met the macula itself back in Series 1, in the episode called The Macula and Central Vision, where we learned it is the region of sharpest, most detailed sight. Now you can restate that fact anatomically, and it stops being a description and becomes an explanation: central sight is sharpest because the brain has granted it half of the processing territory. The map is drawn by importance, not by area.

Host: Why would the brain build such an unfair map? What is the logic of spending half the budget on the centre?

Guide: The logic follows the hardware of the retina itself, and that takes us back to an episode of this series we can be proud of: Season 7, episode nine, Fovea and Foveola, where we saw cone photoreceptors packed to their anatomical maximum and the inner retinal layers pushed bodily aside to let light reach them. Wherever the retina invests in receptor density, the cortex invests in processing space. Cone density, ganglion cell density, relay space in the geniculate, and cortical territory are all scaled together, from fovea to periphery, one continuous economy. Cortical magnification is simply the cortical end of a pipeline that has been magnified from the very first synapse. And that gives you two instincts you can carry into the chair. First, a small cortical lesion at the pole is a large functional catastrophe, because a few millimetres of cortex there carry the reading zone of the entire visual world. Second, it explains why patients with hemianopias can often keep reading when the sparing reaches the pole. The cortex spends where the patient lives.

Host: Zoom in with me one more level, from the map down to the wiring itself. Inside all that territory, what are the neurons actually doing with the image?

Guide: Two organising principles define the microstructure, and both were worked out in the landmark experiments of David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel in the twentieth century, work that later earned a Nobel Prize. The first principle is ocular dominance. Within layer four C, the inputs from the two eyes remain segregated into alternating narrow stripes, each roughly half a millimetre wide, one stripe answering mostly to the left eye, the next mostly to the right, alternating across the whole map. This is the first place in the entire pathway where the two eyes' images occupy the same sheet of tissue, and notice that even here they arrive interleaved but still separate, like two printing plates laid side by side. The merging into single vision is a story for later processing. The second principle is orientation. As you record from cells above layer four, neurons respond preferentially to lines of a particular tilt, and cells stacked in a little column all share the same preference, while neighbouring columns prefer neighbouring angles, arranged across the surface in beautiful pinwheel patterns. A block of cortex about a millimetre across, called a hypercolumn, contains the complete set of orientations for both eyes, for one small point in the field. Hubel and Wiesel named the earliest of these neurons simple cells, responders to bars and edges at a precise location and tilt, and the ones beyond them complex cells, which respond to that same orientation anywhere within their patch. Functionally then, the striate cortex takes the intact image delivered by the pathway and shatters it into oriented fragments, each tagged by location, by eye of origin, and by tilt. It is a deconstruction plant. Everything we will ever recognise as form is assembled downstream from its output.

Host: And downstream is where you promised a brief look. Beyond area one, what is waiting?

Guide: Only a sketch today, because each of these regions deserves an hour of its own, and our lane today is the striate cortex. Immediately surrounding it lies the extrastriate belt we met when we discussed the line of Gennari: Brodmann area eighteen, the second visual area, and beyond it area nineteen and a chain of further specialised regions. From these, two great streams flow out of the occipital lobe. The ventral stream runs forward and downward into the temporal lobe, dominated by areas such as area V four, and it carries the analysis of form and colour; it answers the question, what am I looking at. The dorsal stream runs upward into the parietal lobe, dominated by the middle temporal area, often called area V five, which is specialised for motion; it answers the question, where is it, and where is it going. Form and colour toward the temple, motion and position toward the crown. Clinical neurology confirms the split: rare lesions of the ventral occipitotemporal region can leave a patient unable to name colours, and rare dorsal lesions can leave motion itself invisible. I offer this tour the way a map offers a coastline: enough to navigate by, not enough to claim you have visited.

Host: Before the recap, one honest question. Some of this map, these columns, these streams, how much of it do we actually know, and how do we know it?

Guide: A fair challenge, and worth answering, because it builds trust in everything else. The classical layer of knowledge comes from the dissection and staining methods we have already met: Brodmann mapping the cortex by cytoarchitecture, Gennari's stripe visible to the eye, and the lesion work of neurologists and neuro-ophthalmologists, who for over a century matched field defects in living patients to damage found after death, and slowly drew the map we have been walking. The modern layer comes from functional imaging and from microelectrode recordings in the laboratory. Functional magnetic resonance imaging, which watches blood flow rise in busy cortex, can let a person watch a flickering target sweep across a field of view while the scanner traces exactly which millimetres of calcarine cortex light up, and it has confirmed the classical map in living brains, including the magnification of the centre. Hubel and Wiesel's recordings gave us the columns. So when I quote you a number today, it rests on more than one kind of evidence, from more than one century.

Host: Let me make sure I can carry all of this out of the room. Give me the episode in one pass, the way you would want a student to recite it the night before an exam.

Guide: Happily, and in short breaths. Location: the primary visual cortex wraps the calcarine sulcus on the medial surface of the occipital lobe, the cuneus above, the lingual gyrus below, the sulcus so deep it raises the calcar avis into the occipital horn of the lateral ventricle. Names: V one, Brodmann area seventeen, striate cortex, the last named for the line of Gennari, the naked-eye stripe of myelinated fibres in layer four B of a six-layered koniocortex. Wiring: the magnocellular and parvocellular streams from the lateral geniculate body arrive separately, in layer four C alpha and layer four C beta, and the layers dispatch outputs forward to the extrastriate belt, downward to the colliculus, and backward to the geniculate. Map: each hemisphere holds the opposite hemifield, the macula at the pole, the periphery forward along the sulcus, the upper bank for the lower field, the lower bank for the upper field, and the monocular crescent at the anterior tip. Magnification: the central ten degrees take fifty to sixty percent of the cortex, about five millimetres per degree at the fovea falling to one at ten degrees. Microstructure: ocular dominance stripes about half a millimetre wide, orientation columns gathered into hypercolumns about a millimetre across. Supply: the posterior cerebral artery through its calcarine branch, with a middle cerebral border zone at the pole that spares the macula in stroke. And beyond: area eighteen, and the ventral and dorsal streams. That is the striate cortex, complete.

Host: A full pass, and I felt the whole sulcus settle into place. Before we part, leave our listener with one thought for tomorrow morning in the examining chair.

Guide: Remember that this entire season has been one unbroken cable running from the photoreceptors to this one sulcus, and tomorrow, when a field printout shows a defect that respects the vertical midline, you will no longer be merely reading a pattern. You will be reading a coordinate. A loss that is dense and central, think pole and cortex. A loss that respects a quadrant, think bank, or radiation, and remember which bank goes with which quadrant. A thin crescent-shaped loss in one eye alone, think the anterior tip of the calcarine sulcus. Anatomy converts a mystery into a locality, and a locality converts into the right referral at the right speed, which is the whole reason an optometrist learns the brain at this level of detail. The eye gathers, but the cortex is where the delivery ends, and it deserves to be known as exactly that: a real place, with a real address, drawn on a sulcus you can now name.
