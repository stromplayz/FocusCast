# Description: Six of the twelve cranial nerve pairs keep your eyes working, and this episode lines them up in one anatomical roster. Focus Cast by focuslinks unpacks what the optic, oculomotor, trochlear, trigeminal, abducens and facial nerves each carry for ocular function: the million-plus fibres of sight, the movers of the globe, the relay for pupil and focus, the protective sensation of the cornea, and the muscles that close the lids. Hear how every nerve travels through the cavernous sinus and the superior orbital fissure, and why those routes explain the clinical signs. A synthesis episode for optometry students, practitioners and curious listeners, drawing together seasons of ocular anatomy in one walk.

Host: Welcome back, and welcome if this is your first hour with us. You have arrived at an interesting moment. For eight episodes now, this tenth season of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists has walked the wiring of vision one pathway at a time: the optic nerve, the chiasm, the tract, the relay, the radiations, the cortex, and then the two reflex loops, the pupil and the near response. Today we stop walking pathways and stand back to look at the whole switchboard. Six of the twelve pairs of cranial nerves touch ocular function, and we are going to gather them onto one roster and ask a single question of each: what exactly does this nerve carry for the eye? Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and to my guide through the anatomy, thank you for being here. Where shall we plant the flag before the climb?

Guide: With pleasure, and let me set the frame first, because this episode is a bird's-eye view by design. The body has twelve pairs of cranial nerves, and six of the twelve touch ocular function: the second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth and seventh. Across earlier seasons we met their work piece by piece, the lid muscles, the lacrimal gland, the corneal nerves, the extraocular muscles, and in the last two episodes the pupillary and accommodation pathways. Today we line up the nerves themselves and ask, for each one, what cargo it actually carries for the eye, and by which route. When a topic belongs elsewhere, we will name the episode that owns it and move on. This is a synthesis map, not a re-teaching. The roster is short, the routes are crowded, and by the end of the hour the whole switchboard should have labels on every switch.

Host: Cargo is a good word, because it makes me picture freight rather than the nerves themselves. Before we open the manifest, remind us of the categories. When an anatomist says a nerve carries something, what kinds of cargo are actually riding through the head?

Guide: Four categories cover almost everything we will meet today. First, somatic motor fibres, which drive skeletal muscle under voluntary control, the movers of the globe and the muscles of the lids. Second, parasympathetic fibres, the visceral motor system, which always involve a relay: preganglionic fibres run out from the brainstem to a ganglion, synapse there, and postganglionic fibres continue on to smooth muscle, in our case the iris and the ciliary body. Third, sensory fibres, from the ordinary touch of the cornea and the lids to the special sense of sight itself. And fourth, a stowaway class: sympathetic fibres, which are not cranial nerve cargo at all, since they climb up from the neck along the carotid artery, but which hitch rides on cranial branches to reach the dilator pupillae and the vessels. Keep those four bins in mind, and every nerve below will sort itself neatly.

Host: Then let us take them in numerical order, starting at nerve two. We ended episode one of this season with it, so treat us to the compressed version. What does the optic nerve carry, and what kind of structure is it really?

Guide: Nerve two first, because it is the odd one from the very first word. We call it the optic nerve, but strictly it is not a peripheral nerve at all; it is a stretched-out piece of the central nervous system, a white matter tract dressed up as a nerve. Back in Series 1, the episode The Optic Nerve and Visual Information told us what this cable tells the brain; today we ask what it is made of. Its fibres are the axons of the retinal ganglion cells, roughly one point two million of them in each nerve, converging onto the optic disc, which you know as a pale-yellow disc about one and a half millimetres across, and leaving the eye through the lamina cribrosa, the sievelike plate in the sclera. From there the nerve runs about fifty millimetres in four segments: roughly one millimetre inside the eye, about twenty-five millimetres in the orbit, around nine millimetres inside the optic canal, and the remainder intracranial before the chiasm. And because it is brain tissue, it is wrapped in the three meninges with a sleeve of cerebrospinal fluid around it, a fact that matters enormously, as you will hear in a moment.

Host: Wait, I want to slow down on that phrase, a piece of the brain dressed as a nerve. Why does the optic nerve count as central tissue, and does that change anything about what else it carries besides the picture?

Guide: Two reasons, one structural and one functional. Structurally, the myelin behind the lamina cribrosa is made by oligodendrocytes, the same glial cells that insulate brain fibres, not by Schwann cells as in ordinary nerves, and central tissue does not regenerate the way a cut peripheral nerve can. Functionally, the optic nerve does not only carry pictures. As those million fibres stream toward the chiasm, a small bundle branches off before the relay, toward the pretectal area, a small patch of midbrain that hands the light signal onward, and that branch is the afferent limb of the pupillary light reflex, the sensation to which the pupil responds. We walked that arc completely in episode seven, the Pupillary Pathway; today the point is simply that the light-reflex signal rides in nerve two along with sight itself. When you covered one eye and watched the other pupil in the Series 1 episode Pupil Examination, you were testing the integrity of exactly this cargo.

Host: From the smallest freight we move to the largest haul. Nerve three, the oculomotor. Paint the route first, and then tell us precisely what is aboard.

Guide: Nerve three is the freight train of the roster, and its somatic motor cargo serves five muscles in the orbit. Four of them are movers of the globe: the medial rectus, the superior rectus, the inferior rectus, and the inferior oblique, which leaves only the lateral rectus and the superior oblique to other nerves. The fifth is the levator palpebrae superioris, the lid lifter. The fibres begin in the oculomotor nuclear complex in the midbrain, at the level of the superior colliculus, and emerge from the brainstem in the interpeduncular fossa, the notch between the two cerebral peduncles. The nerve then runs forward beneath the posterior cerebral artery and above the superior cerebellar artery, pierces the dura, climbs into the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, that blood-filled crossroads at the skull base through which the carotid artery and several nerves pass, and enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure, the main doorway between skull and orbit. There it splits into a superior and an inferior division: the superior division supplies the superior rectus and the levator, and the inferior division supplies the medial rectus, the inferior rectus, and the inferior oblique.

Host: Now the part of nerve three every optometrist leans on daily. You said the pupil and the focusing muscle take their orders through the third nerve. Walk us through that cargo, station by station.

Guide: Alongside those motor fibres, the third nerve carries the parasympathetic wiring for focus and for the pupil, and it is worth naming each station because we have met them all before. Preganglionic fibres arise in the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, the small visceral column beside the main oculomotor nucleus, and travel down in the inferior division. Near the orbital apex, a twig called the motor or parasympathetic root leaves the nerve for the ciliary ganglion, a relay barely two millimetres across, sitting lateral to the optic nerve toward the back of the orbit. There the preganglionic fibres synapse, and the postganglionic fibres leave in six to ten short ciliary nerves, which run forward around the optic nerve and pierce the sclera to reach the sphincter pupillae, the circular muscle that constricts the pupil, and the ciliary muscle, whose contraction slackens the zonules so the lens rounds up for near focus. We walked the light-reflex wiring in episode seven and the near-response wiring in episode eight, the Accommodation Pathway; the anatomy to file today is that all of that cargo physically rides inside nerve three.

Host: Here is where I always get lost clinically. Students learn that some third nerve palsies involve the pupil and some do not. What in the anatomy decides that?

Guide: One more anatomical fact decides it: within the third nerve, the parasympathetic fibres travel close to the surface, while the motor fibres sit more centrally. Now picture the nerve's route beneath the posterior cerebral artery and above the superior cerebellar artery, right beside the great arterial circle at the base of the brain. If a small bulge on an artery wall, an aneurysm, slowly swells there, it presses on the nerve from outside, and the surface fibres suffer first: the pupil blows wide and stops reacting while eye movement may still be nearly normal. A compressive palsy announces itself with a dilated, unreactive pupil, a drooping lid because the levator has lost its supply, and an eye resting down and out because four of its six movers are silent. An ischaemic palsy, where the nerve's own small vessels fail, tends to hit the deep motor core and spare the pupil. Ever since the Series 1 episode Pupil Examination taught you to treat the pupil as a vital sign, the anatomy has been backing you up: the pupil lies on the surface of the wire.

Host: Onward to nerve four, then, the trochlear. I confess this is the one students whisper about before exams. What makes it so unusual?

Guide: Nerve four is the miniature of the family. By fibre count it is the smallest cranial nerve, roughly three thousand four hundred axons, and its cargo is a single muscle, the superior oblique. Its nucleus sits in the midbrain at the level of the inferior colliculus, and then the anatomy turns strange in two ways that every viva examiner loves. First, the fibres curve backward around the cerebral aqueduct and cross completely, decussating in a thin sheet called the superior medullary velum, so the nerve emerges from the dorsal surface of the brainstem, the only cranial nerve to leave from the back, and every fibre in it serves the superior oblique of the opposite eye. Second, that dorsal exit commits it to the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve, about seventy-five millimetres, winding around the midbrain, through the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus below the third nerve, into the orbit through the superior orbital fissure outside the annulus of Zinn, the fibrous ring at the orbital apex from which the rectus muscles arise, and then medially above the levator to reach the superior oblique on its upper border. The nerve is named for the trochlea, the little pulley through which that muscle's tendon turns, the pulley we studied in the Season 9 episode Superior Oblique.

Host: Two oddities, then: the dorsal exit and the complete crossing. Why would the system route its smallest muscle through a full decussation, and what does that long course cost the patient?

Guide: The crossing is not an accident; it follows the muscle's geometry. The superior oblique works as a pulley muscle: it intorts the eye, turning the twelve o'clock pole toward the nose, and it helps pull the eye down when the eye is already turned inward, and its wiring simply leaves the midbrain on the far side to match. The clinical price is the long, slender, tethered course: because the nerve wraps over the edge of the midbrain and travels so far in the water-jacket space that surrounds the brainstem, a knock to the head can stretch it, which is why a trochlear palsy after trauma is a classic, and why the patient complains of vertical double vision that is worst looking down, such as on a staircase. One muscle, three thousand four hundred fibres, and a whole pattern of double vision hangs on them.

Host: And now the great sensory trunk. Nerve five has three divisions, so keep us honest: which parts of it actually matter inside the orbit?

Guide: Nerve five, the trigeminal, is the giant: the largest cranial nerve, with three great sensory divisions and a small motor root. From the side of the pons emerge two roots, a massive sensory root and a slender motor root. The sensory root expands into the trigeminal ganglion, sometimes called the semilunar or Gasserian ganglion, sitting in Meckel's cave, a pocket of dura near the pointed summit of the petrous temporal bone. From that ganglion three divisions fan out: the ophthalmic, the maxillary, and the mandibular; the first two are purely sensory, and the motor root joins only the third, to supply the muscles of chewing. For ocular function the ophthalmic division, the first division of nerve five, is the star. It runs forward in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, below the fourth nerve, and divides into three branches as it reaches the superior orbital fissure: the lacrimal, the smallest, which runs along the upper border of the lateral rectus to the lacrimal gland; the frontal, the largest, which splits into the supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves for the forehead, scalp and upper lid; and the nasociliary, the deepest, which concerns us most.

Host: Take the nasociliary slowly, because I suspect this single branch is the reason optometrists care about nerve five at all. What does it carry, and to where?

Guide: The nasociliary is the branch that actually enters the muscle cone. It passes through the annulus of Zinn between the two divisions of the third nerve, crosses above the optic nerve from lateral to medial, and along the way distributes its cargo with remarkable precision. It gives the sensory root to the ciliary ganglion, and note the word sensory, so the ganglion is a three-root station: motor from nerve three, sensory from nerve five, sympathetic from the carotid plexus. It gives two or three long ciliary nerves, which run straight to the eyeball, carrying sensation from the cornea, iris and ciliary body, plus postganglionic sympathetic fibres headed for the dilator pupillae, the radial muscle that widens the pupil. It gives the posterior and anterior ethmoidal nerves into the ethmoid bone, the anterior one continuing as the external nasal nerve to the tip of the nose, and it finishes as the infratrochlear nerve for the medial canthus, the side of the nose, and the region of the lacrimal sac. Remember from the Season 4 episode Corneal Nerves that the cornea is the most densely innervated surface in the body; every one of those subbasal fibres ultimately funnels into this one branch of nerve five.

Host: Which brings us to a reflex everyone performs a dozen times a day. How do nerves five and seven cooperate in the blink, in strictly anatomical terms?

Guide: Handsomely, because the blink reflex is the handshake of nerves five and seven. The arc runs like this: the cornea is touched, and the impulse travels in the long ciliary nerves, through the nasociliary and the ophthalmic division, into the main sensory nucleus and the spinal trigeminal nucleus of nerve five in the pons and medulla. Interneurons then project onward to the facial motor nuclei on both sides, and the efferent signal leaves in the facial nerves, reaching the orbicularis oculi through its temporal and zygomatic branches. The result is a bilateral blink: the stimulated eye closes, and so does the other. Anatomy explains the consent: the crossing interneurons spread the signal to both seventh nuclei at once. So when a patient blinks both eyes as you approach with a drop, you have just watched nerve five reporting and nerve seven executing, a two-nerve arc you can test in a single second.

Host: Before we leave nerve five, one loose end. The maxillary division runs under the orbit. Does it do anything for the eye, or is it purely cheek territory?

Guide: It does, in the most roundabout way imaginable, and it concerns tears rather than touch. The maxillary division leaves the skull through the foramen rotundum into the pterygopalatine fossa, and one of its branches, the zygomatic nerve, slips back into the orbit through the inferior orbital fissure. Running in the floor of the orbit, the zygomatic gives off the zygomaticotemporal branch, and that little twig carries the secretomotor parasympathetic fibres that left the pterygopalatine ganglion, up to a communicating branch that hands them to the lacrimal nerve. So the wiring of tears is a relay race: parasympathetic fibres, having arrived at the pterygopalatine ganglion, jump to the maxillary division, ride the zygomaticotemporal into the orbit, and are delivered to the lacrimal gland in the company of the lacrimal branch of the ophthalmic nerve. Back in the Season 2 episode Lacrimal Gland Anatomy we mapped the gland itself, its fossa and its ducts; today you finally see whose wires feed it.

Host: Nerve six, then, the abducens. One muscle, one job. But I have a feeling the journey to deliver that job is the real story.

Guide: Nerve six is the straight shooter with the cruel commute. Its cargo is one muscle, the lateral rectus, the only true abductor of the eye. The nucleus lies in the lower pons, buried just beneath a bump on the floor of the fourth ventricle called the facial colliculus, a bump created by the facial nerve looping over the abducens nucleus, a relation worth remembering because it couples two nerves we discuss today. The fibres emerge at the junction of pons and medulla and then begin the longest subarachnoid climb of the ocular motor nerves: up the surface of the clivus, the bony slope between the brainstem and the pituitary, then sharply over the crest of the petrous temporal bone, squeezing beneath the petrosphenoidal ligament through a small channel called Dorello's canal, before entering the cavernous sinus. And here is its signature: the third nerve, the fourth and the ophthalmic division travel in the wall of that sinus, but the sixth runs free inside it, right alongside the internal carotid artery. It then enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure inside the annulus of Zinn and runs forward in the muscle cone to the lateral rectus, whose insertion and nerve entry we measured in the Season 9 episode Lateral Rectus.

Host: You called its commute cruel, and I want the clinical translation. Why do optometrists and neurologists both keep a sixth nerve palsy high on their watch list?

Guide: Because its anatomy sets three classic traps. First, raised intracranial pressure, from any cause, can drag the nerve downward along the clivus and pull it tight over the sharp petrous apex, strangling the fibres, which is why a sixth nerve palsy can be a false localising sign, blaming the pons when the real problem is pressure somewhere else entirely. Second, the middle ear and the mastoid air cells sit just across the bone from the petrous apex, so infection eroding that bone can catch the nerve on its way past. Third, its free run inside the cavernous sinus puts it beside the carotid artery, exposed to any disease of that vessel. The result on the eye is pure geometry: without the abductor, the eye drifts inward, an esotropia that is worst at distance, and the patient turns the head toward the weak side to keep the world single. Back in Series 1, the Ocular Motility episode taught you to watch the eyes follow a target through the six cardinal directions; now you know the individual cables you were really testing.

Host: Last on the roster, nerve seven, the facial. This one always surprises people, because it seems to be about smiling, not about eyes. What is it carrying for us?

Guide: Nerve seven is the lid closer and the tear supplier, and it is a mixed nerve with three streams of cargo: motor to the muscles of facial expression, parasympathetic secretomotor fibres, and a trickle of taste and small sensation. The motor fibres arise in the facial motor nucleus of the pons, loop backward over the abducens nucleus, creating that facial colliculus we just met, and emerge beside nerve six at the pontomedullary junction along with a second small root, the nervus intermedius, which carries the sensory and parasympathetic streams. All three roots pass together into the internal acoustic meatus, and then the nerve enters the facial canal, a bony tunnel about thirty millimetres long, the longest bony canal any nerve travels through. At its first bend sits the geniculate ganglion, and right there the greater petrosal nerve peels off, carrying the parasympathetic fibres for tears, which were born in the superior salivatory nucleus: it joins the deep petrosal nerve, the sympathetic strand from the carotid plexus, and the combined nerve of the pterygoid canal runs to the pterygopalatine ganglion, where we found these same fibres boarding the maxillary division a few minutes ago. The main trunk continues down the canal, exits through the stylomastoid foramen, and fans into five branches, of which the temporal and zygomatic branches reach the orbicularis oculi, with the temporal branch also lifting the frontalis and the brow.

Host: So compare the two lid catastrophes for me directly. If a third nerve is damaged, versus a seventh nerve, what does the patient's eye actually look like at rest, and why?

Guide: Perfectly opposite, and the contrast is worth memorising because it comes straight from the anatomy. A third nerve lesion removes the levator palpebrae superioris, so the lid drops, a ptosis, but the orbicularis oculi is intact, so the cornea remains covered and protected; the problem is an eye that cannot open and cannot move, not an eye that dries. A seventh nerve lesion is the mirror image: the orbicularis oculi is paralysed, so the eye cannot close, a gap called lagophthalmos; the lower lid falls away from the globe, tears pool and spill over the edge, and the exposed cornea dries into exposure keratopathy. Same lid, opposite disaster: one nerve cannot lift the curtain, the other cannot lower it. This is the wiring side of the lid machinery we assembled in the Season 1 episode Eyelid Muscles, the levator opening and the orbicularis closing, and it is why every facial palsy demands corneal protection from day one.

Host: Before the takeaways, give us the two things this whole roster shares: the corridors. And then, if you can, the nerve-by-nerve summary in one breath each.

Guide: Two corridors, then the roster. Corridor one is the cavernous sinus, that crowded venous crossroads at the skull base beside the pituitary: running up its lateral wall, from above down, the third nerve, the fourth, then the ophthalmic division of the fifth, with the sixth alone inside the sinus hugging the carotid. Corridor two is the superior orbital fissure, the great doorway between the sinus and the orbit, which we inventoried opening by opening in the Season 1 episode Orbital Openings and Communications. Through it pass both divisions of the third nerve, the fourth, the sixth, and all three branches of the ophthalmic nerve; and at its entrance the annulus of Zinn sorts the traffic: both divisions of the third, the nasociliary and the sixth pass inside the ring, into the muscle cone, while the fourth, the frontal and the lacrimal slip over the top, outside it. And the roster in one breath. Nerve two carries sight and the light-reflex signal. Nerve three carries four movers, the lid lifter, and the parasympathetic cargo for pupil and focus. Nerve four carries one crossed intorter. Nerve five carries the protective sensation of the eye and the afferent blink, and ferries the tear fibres by way of its maxillary division. Nerve six carries the abductor. Nerve seven carries the closer and the tear wiring. Six nerves, and every blink, glance, tear and focused image rides on them.
