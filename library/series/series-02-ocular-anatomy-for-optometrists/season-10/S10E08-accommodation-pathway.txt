# Description: Focus Cast by focuslinks traces the near response, the wiring that lets two eyes swing together from a distant sign to a page in the hand. This episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists follows retinal blur from the visual cortex down through the internal capsule, the Edinger-Westphal nucleus and the oculomotor nerve, then through the ciliary ganglion and the short ciliary nerves to the ciliary muscle, while convergence and pupillary miosis travel on the same command. Learn how the accommodation pathway differs from the light reflex, why light-near dissociation occurs, and what the near triad looks like at the consulting chair. Made for optometry students, practitioners, and anyone who loves the wiring behind the eyes.

Host: Here is a small experiment you can do right now, wherever you are listening. Hold one hand up at arm's length, look past it at the far wall, and then switch your gaze down to the lines of your own palm. In that instant, three separate systems in each eye answered the same call at the same moment. The lens inside each eye changed shape to bring the palm into focus. The two eyes turned inward toward each other. And the pupils drew down smaller. Nobody commanded any of it. It simply happened, smoothly and together, because you decided to look near. Three reflexes, one instant, no rehearsal. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series together. We are deep in Season 10, Neuroanatomy for Optometrists, and this is episode eight. Last week we walked the pupillary light pathway from retina to sphincter. Today we stay in the same neighbourhood of the midbrain but change the question, from how light drives the pupil to how nearness recruits the whole eye at once.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back. The trio you just performed has a name, the near triad, sometimes called the near response. It has three limbs. Accommodation, which is the eye adding plus focusing power by rounding up its lens. Convergence, which is the two eyes turning inward so the near object lands on the central pit of each retina at the same time. And miosis, which is constriction of the pupils. Three limbs, one event, and keep those three words close, because the whole episode hangs on them. Our job today is the wiring, the actual nerve pathway that connects the decision to look near to those three muscular outcomes. We will travel from the cortex at the back of the brain, down through the internal capsule, into the midbrain, out along the third cranial nerve, through the ciliary ganglion, and into the ciliary muscle and the iris. One boundary note before we set off. The moving parts themselves, the lens capsule, the zonules, the geometry of the ciliary muscle, we already assembled in Season 6, episode nine, the Anatomical Basis of Accommodation. So today the machinery is a finished engine on the bench, and we are installing the electrics.

Host: Let us start the story at its true beginning, with the trigger. What actually sets the near triad in motion? Is it seeing blur, or is it knowing that the page is close?

Guide: The primary trigger is blur. Back in Series 1 there was an episode called, simply, Accommodation, and it gave us the concept. Today we lift the lid on the mechanism, and the mechanism starts with the image. When you shift fixation from a distant sign to a page, the eye is still focused for distance, so the retinal image of the letters arrives smeared. That smear has a name, defocus, and here is the remarkable part. The visual system does not merely notice that the image is blurred. It estimates how blurred the image is, and in which direction the focus has missed, too far in front of the retina or too far behind it, and it computes the exact plus power needed to cancel the error. That computed correction is the instruction the rest of today's pathway will carry. Blur, in other words, is not noise to the visual system. It is data. Secondary cues help the estimate along. The doubling of the image, which we call disparity, tells the eyes how far to turn in, and the simple knowledge that the target is near sharpens the whole response. And all of it runs beneath awareness and quickly, the entire loop closing in well under a second.

Host: Now the blur report has to travel from the retina into the brain. Does it take the same road the light signal took last week?

Guide: No, and this is the single most examinable fork in the whole episode. Back in episode seven, the Pupillary Pathway, we watched the light signal leave the main visual highway early. Before it ever reached the great relay station of the thalamus, fibres peeled away toward the pretectal area of the midbrain, and the light reflex was completed there, entirely below the level of conscious vision. That is a subcortical reflex. Accommodation is built differently. The blur signal rides the full visual highway all the way to the top. Out along the optic nerve, partially reshuffled at the chiasm, through the optic tract, into the lateral geniculate body, which we mapped in episode four, the Lateral Geniculate Body, then across the optic radiations of episode five, the Optic Radiations, and finally into the visual cortex itself, the striate area folded along the calcarine sulcus, our destination in episode six, the Visual Cortex. Same corridor at the start, different exit, and the exit is everything. Hold that contrast firmly. The light reflex is answered in the midbrain. The near response is answered in the cortex.

Host: So the brain literally has to perceive the blur before it can correct it? The cortex is a working part of this reflex?

Guide: That is the cleanest way to say it, and it produces a lovely piece of evidence you can quote in any tutorial. You cannot trick the light reflex with a picture. A photograph of the sun will not constrict a pupil in a darkened room, because the pretectal system measures real luminance arriving at the retina, and nothing else. But a picture can pull accommodation. Show the eye an image that simulates defocus, and the system responds to the computed blur itself, not to the object in front of the nose. The stimulus is the optics, not the content. And notice something practical that follows from this. The blur does not have to be legible. Letters too smeared to read still drive the response, because what the cortex estimates is the magnitude and direction of the smear, not the meaning of the words. The striate cortex, area seventeen in the classic numbering, builds the picture. The belt of association cortex around it, areas eighteen and nineteen, reads the blur out of that picture and hands the correction downward.

Host: From the cortex at the very back of the head, then. Where do those wires go next?

Guide: They descend. Fibres leave the occipital visual areas, sweep forward through the deep white matter of the hemispheres, and pass down through a great funnel of projection fibres called the internal capsule, the main cable route between cortex and brainstem. From there the pathway reaches the dorsal midbrain, the level of the superior colliculus, home of the oculomotor nuclear complex. I should be honest about one thing. This descending stretch is not drawn as one crisp ribbon in an atlas, the way the radiations are. It is a supranuclear route, meaning control fibres travelling from higher centres down to the motor nuclei, and it is organised diffusely, from the occipital regions and their neighbouring association areas at once. That fuzziness is itself a lesson. The near response is a broadcast, not a telegram. Whatever the exact fibre sheet, the command arrives bilaterally, informing motor nuclei on both sides of the midbrain, because the near response is a two-eyed affair. One decision, delivered to both sides. We will see what that buys in a few minutes.

Host: The destination, then, is the same little nucleus we met last week. The Edinger-Westphal nucleus?

Guide: The very same, now seen from a new angle. The Edinger-Westphal nucleus is the accessory parasympathetic nucleus of the oculomotor complex, a slim column of small preganglionic parasympathetic cells lying alongside the main motor nucleus of the third nerve, in the dorsal midbrain at the level of the superior colliculus. Last week it played the pupil's motor, dispatching the fibres that constrict the sphincter pupillae for the light reflex. This week we watch it play a second role, dispatching the fibres that will drive the ciliary muscle. The parent complex it belongs to also houses the motor pools for the other muscles the third nerve serves, but those lie outside today's story, except one. In the same complex sit the subnuclei that supply the medial rectus muscles, which is the anatomical address of convergence. So the control room for the near triad is one compact neighbourhood, the oculomotor complex, with the Edinger-Westphal columns at its centre. One switchboard, three output lines. Let us walk each line in turn.

Host: Before we open the switchboard, a small detour for the curious. The name Edinger-Westphal. Who were they?

Guide: Two nineteenth century German investigators. Ludwig Edinger, an anatomist working in Frankfurt, and Carl Westphal, a neurologist working in Berlin, each described this small-celled accessory nucleus beside the third nerve motor pool in 1885, in separate publications appearing in the same year. The eponym stuck to both of them, which is why the name carries a hyphen. I mention the history for a practical reason. When a nucleus earns a proper name of its own, it means clinicians kept finding that it could fail on its own, and a century of pupil clinics has proven exactly that with this one. It is worth knowing whose territory you are entering.

Host: Line one, then. Accommodation. Walk me along the wire, from the nucleus all the way to the ciliary muscle.

Guide: Preganglionic fibres leave the Edinger-Westphal nucleus and gather into the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve, as it organises itself within the midbrain. Inside the nerve they ride the inferior division, and they leave that division in the branch destined for the inferior oblique muscle. That branch carries the motor root to the ciliary ganglion, the relay station about two millimetres across that sits in the muscle cone between the optic nerve and the lateral rectus, and that we studied closely last week. Only parasympathetic fibres actually synapse in that ganglion, so the parasympathetic passengers are the only ones who change trains here. The preganglionic fibres find their cells, and postganglionic fibres stream out in the short ciliary nerves, some eight to ten of them, which pierce the sclera in a ring around the optic nerve and run forward in the suprachoroidal space, the thin plane between choroid and sclera, until they reach the ciliary muscle. Last week the same corridor was delivering fibres to the sphincter pupillae. This week the address is the ciliary muscle, and here is the telling number. In the counts usually quoted, the fibres earmarked for the ciliary muscle outnumber those bound for the sphincter by roughly thirty to one.

Host: Thirty to one. So this parasympathetic line is, first and foremost, the lens line. And what happens when the message finally lands?

Guide: The message lands chemically. The postganglionic fibres release acetylcholine, and the ciliary muscle answers through muscarinic receptors. Keep in mind that the ciliary muscle is smooth muscle, involuntary, quite unlike the striped muscles that aim the eye. When it contracts, the mechanical story takes over, and that story we built in Season 6, episode nine. In one sentence, contraction of the muscle ring, with its longitudinal, radial and circular portions we named back then, slackens the zonular tension at the lens equator, the elastic capsule rounds the lens up, and the eye gains plus power. Today, note only the wiring facts around that machinery. Contraction is cholinergic and muscarinic, which is why a drop of pilocarpine, which mimics acetylcholine at those receptors, forces the accommodation side of the system to fire, and why cyclopentolate or atropine, which block those same receptors, abolish accommodation for the duration of their action. Topical, painless, and completely reversible once the drug wears off. That is the entire logic of a cycloplegic refraction. The wiring above remains perfectly intact. The receiver has simply been unplugged at the muscle.

Host: One practical follow-up while we are on pharmacology. Pilocarpine, the mimic you just mentioned. Why does that drop famously leave patients aching above the eye?

Guide: Because of where the receptors live and how many of them there are. A miotic drop does not trickle politely to one destination. It floods the anterior chamber and reaches a ciliary muscle that is stuffed with muscarinic receptors, recall our thirty to one traffic weighting, and the muscle contracts hard and stays contracted. That sustained cramp of a focusing muscle is called ciliary spasm, and patients feel it as a deep ache around the brow and inside the eye, often with the focus temporarily pulled toward near. The ache is pharmacology confirming the anatomy. The lens line is the busiest platform on this parasympathetic railway.

Host: Line two. Convergence. What is different about the way that output is wired?

Guide: Almost everything. Convergence is delivered by the medial rectus subnuclei of the oculomotor complex, and their output is somatic motor, the striped-muscle kind. These fibres need no peripheral ganglion and no chemical relay outside the brain. No ganglion, no detour, no delay. They leave the midbrain with the third nerve, travel in its inferior division, and land directly on each medial rectus. And crucially, the descending cortical command fires both subnuclei together, so the two medial recti contract as a yoked pair. The classical texts also describe a dedicated convergence centre near the third nerve complex, sometimes called Perlia's nucleus. Treat that name gently, because its identity in the human brainstem has been argued over for a century. What is not argued over is the output. The medial recti are driven, bilaterally and in step, by the same complex that drives the lens, which is exactly why the two responses lock together in the first place.

Host: Why must they lock together, though? In principle, each eye could turn in on its own schedule. Why is it one command?

Guide: Because of the fovea bargain. Single vision depends on the image of the near object falling on the fovea of each eye at the same time, and that demands an inward turn scaled precisely to the viewing distance. Do the arithmetic with me. Average pupils sit about six and a half centimetres apart. Hold a page a third of a metre away, and keeping both foveas on that page demands close to twenty prism dioptres of total convergence. Hold it twice as far away, and the demand halves. There is no slack anywhere in that calculation, so the drive that sets lens power and the drive that sets eye position have to come from the same source and travel together. You can feel that precision yourself. The turn that keeps a single image at arm's length would leave you with doubling at reading distance, and yet the system holds single vision all day long, to within a prism dioptre or two. The coupling even has measured numbers. Each dioptre of accommodation normally carries about three to five prism dioptres of convergence along with it, the ratio students learn as the accommodative convergence to accommodation ratio, and a mirror coupling runs in the other direction, so that strong convergence itself pulls a little extra accommodation. Series 1 opened that door in its episode called Convergence, so we will not walk down it again today. Where these couplings run unusually strong or unusually weak, that is where clinical binocular vision trouble lives. But keep today's wiring point. One command, three hands, and the hands cannot fall out of step.

Host: Which leaves line three, miosis. That one, at least, we have half traced already.

Guide: We have, and it is the shortest retelling of the three. The same preganglionic fibres from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, the same relay in the ciliary ganglion, the same short ciliary nerves, and at the far end a second destination, the sphincter pupillae of the iris. In the near response the pupils constrict gently and slowly compared with the brisk pinch of a light flash, and the amount is modest, a small tightening rather than a squeeze. Notice also that this limb shares last week's anatomy wholesale. We are simply watching a corridor we already know join a different ensemble. The purpose, though, is optical, and it is worth saying aloud. A smaller pupil is a smaller aperture, and a smaller aperture increases the eye's depth of focus and trims the blur circle produced by any residual aberration. It is the eye stopping down like a camera so that the newly sharpened focus holds across a slightly deeper range of distances. So the triad is not three accidents travelling together. It is accommodation pulling the page into focus, convergence doubling the image onto two foveas, and miosis tightening the aperture so the gain survives the small tremors of everyday viewing.

Host: One more wiring question before we go clinical. Is the near response built per eye, or does it run for both eyes at once?

Guide: Both eyes at once, always, and the anatomy we have laid out already explains it. The command originates at a supranuclear level, which simply means the control comes from higher centres rather than from any single eye's final motor nerve. Cover one eye and ask the patient to read near with the other. Both eyes still accommodate, because once the blur report reaches the cortex it drives both Edinger-Westphal nuclei. Both pupils still constrict, the consensual behaviour we watched in the light reflex last week. And both eyes turn in, because the medial rectus subnuclei are fired together by design. One broadcast, many receivers. In the consulting chair this shows up as the simplest thing in the world. The covered eye converges with its partner, its pupil constricts, and its lens changes power, all unseen and all completely real. That is why you can assess the whole triad even while one eye sits behind an occluder, a fact every routine quietly relies on.

Host: Now the clinical payoff, because there is a famous finding waiting here. Pupils that will not react to light but still constrict for near. How does today's anatomy produce that split?

Guide: The finding is called light-near dissociation, and our two-door map explains it in one picture. Remember that there are two afferent doors into the same efferent hall. The light reflex enters through the pretectal door, subcortical. The near response enters through the cortical door, by way of the lateral geniculate body and the visual cortex. Once inside, both share the same hallway downstairs, the Edinger-Westphal nucleus and its efferent line. Damage the pretectal door and the hall stands untouched, so the light reflex dies while the near response survives. That is the classic Argyll Robertson pupil, classically associated with neurosyphilis and quoted in every pupil lecture ever given, the pupil that accommodates but does not react. The pupils in that classic form are small and irregular, both of them, another fingerprint of midbrain disease. Damage the dorsal midbrain more broadly, as in Parinaud syndrome, and you get large pupils with the same dissociation, often alongside convergence-retraction nystagmus and retraction of the upper lids, because so much of the near-response neighbourhood sits right there. And this is precisely why the pupil routine from Series 1, the Pupil Examination episode, has you swing the patient from a distance target in to a near target. You are not being fussy. You are testing the second door.

Host: And when the fault sits further down the line, after the midbrain, on the efferent wires themselves? What does the near response look like then?

Guide: Then both doors open onto a damaged hallway, and the picture changes character. The best example is the tonic pupil, Adie's pupil, where disease strikes the ciliary ganglion and the short ciliary nerves. Since every efferent fibre for both light and near shares that final corridor, both responses suffer. But recall our ratio of thirty to one. Far more fibres serve the ciliary muscle than the sphincter, so the near response, riding a much larger population of fibres, is the one that limps back first and best. The result is the behaviour the name promises. A pupil that barely moves to light, responds slowly and incompletely to a sustained near effort, and then redilates lazily over long minutes when the effort stops. The eye itself looks quiet and sees normally, which is why the pupil's behaviour at the lamp does the diagnosing. One clinical sign, explained by a fibre ratio we learned in the middle of this very episode. And as for what happens when the third nerve itself is compressed or starved along its longer course, and how the pupil tells those stories apart, that is next week's roll call, so I will leave that door ajar rather than walk through it.

Host: Before the takeaways, walk me through the whole circuit once, end to end, as one unbroken line.

Guide: Gladly. A near page in the hand. Retinal blur is detected, and its magnitude and direction are computed. Notice how the near response borrows the same first-class visual mail route that reading itself uses. The report leaves in the ganglion cell axons of the retina, travels out the optic nerve, is partially reshuffled at the chiasm, sweeps through the optic tract, and synapses in the lateral geniculate body. The radiations carry it to the striate cortex along the calcarine sulcus, area seventeen. The surrounding association cortex, areas eighteen and nineteen, reads the blur out of the picture. Descending fibres pass forward through the white matter and the internal capsule to the dorsal midbrain, to the oculomotor complex. There the command splits three ways. Preganglionic parasympathetic fibres from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus ride the inferior division of the third nerve, leave in the branch to the inferior oblique, synapse in the ciliary ganglion, and continue in the short ciliary nerves, the great bulk of them to the ciliary muscle and a small remainder to the sphincter pupillae. Somatic fibres from the medial rectus subnuclei run in that same inferior division straight to each medial rectus. The lens rounds up, the eyes converge, the pupils constrict. One command, three hands, and the page snaps into focus.

Host: Beautiful. Now the takeaways. If a listener remembers only three things from this episode, what should they be?

Guide: Three things. First, the near triad is one cortical command with three limbs. Accommodation, convergence, and miosis, triggered by computed retinal blur and dispatched together, which is why they never fall out of step with each other. Second, there are two afferent doors into the pupil's efferent hall. The pretectal door for light, subcortical, and the cortical door for near, through the lateral geniculate body and the visual cortex. Every case of light-near dissociation you will ever meet is that double-door anatomy showing itself, from the Argyll Robertson pupil to the dorsal midbrain syndromes. Third, the efferent corridor is shared, and its traffic is weighted. One parasympathetic line from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus through the ciliary ganglion and the short ciliary nerves serves both the ciliary muscle and the sphincter, at roughly thirty to one in the muscle's favour. And one grace note to file beside Season 6, episode ten, Lens Anatomy and Presbyopia. In presbyopia the wiring we traced today remains perfectly intact. It is the machinery at the far end that stiffens. If you want to lock the circuit in, draw it once from memory tonight. Cortex, internal capsule, midbrain, third nerve, ciliary ganglion, short ciliary nerves, three destinations. Then redraw it with the light reflex beside it, and mark the two doors.
