# Description: Where does the visual message go after the chiasm? In this neuroanatomy episode, Focus Cast by focuslinks follows the optic tract as it sweeps around the midbrain carrying the opposite half of the visual world from both eyes. We unpack the fibre order inside the tract, the vessels that travel beside it, and the classic signs of a tract lesion, from incongruous homonymous hemianopia to bow-tie pallor of the optic disc. A clear, exam-ready walk for optometry students and practising optometrists.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library where one topic at a time gets the room it deserves. You are listening to Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series, and we are three episodes deep into season ten, Neuroanatomy for Optometrists, the season where we finally leave the eye itself and follow the visual message into the brain. Two episodes ago, in episode one, Optic Nerve, we watched the cable form, more than a million fine axons leaving each eye through a hole in the skull. Last time, in episode two, Optic Chiasm, we stood at the great switchyard beneath the brain and watched a little more than half of those fibres cross to the opposite side. Today we follow the sorted bundles onward, along a sweeping, flattened ribbon called the optic tract. By the end of the hour you will know exactly where it runs, how the map of the world is arranged inside it, what each tract carries, and why damage here produces one of the most examinable patterns in all of neuro-ophthalmology. To guide us, as always, my calm and patient teacher. Welcome.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome, every listener. The optic tract is the second great segment of the visual pathway, the stretch that begins where the chiasm ends. Picture two flat ribbons of nerve fibres, one on each side of the brain. Each begins at the outer back corner of the chiasm, sweeps backward and outward around the upper part of the midbrain like a road skirting a hill, and ends at a small relay station buried deep in the hemispheres, the lateral geniculate body. One idea should sit at the centre of everything today, and it is beautifully simple. Each optic tract carries the opposite half of the visual world. The left tract carries the right half of the field of view of both eyes, and the right tract carries the left half. Everything else we do this hour, the course, the fibre order inside the ribbon, the blood supply, the clinical signs, is that single idea seen from different angles. Let us take the angles one at a time.

Host: Before we leave the chiasm behind, give us a one breath refresher of the switchyard. You mapped it all last time, so just enough to set up today, and then we push on into new ground.

Guide: Gladly, and with credit where it belongs: the full crossing map belongs to episode two, Optic Chiasm, so I will compress it to three facts we need today. Fact one, going all the way back to our season opener, episode one, Optic Nerve, and further still to a Series 1 episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information: each optic nerve is a bundle of roughly one point two million axons, the output threads of the retinal ganglion cells, the nerve cells whose bodies sit in the innermost cellular layer of the retina. Fact two: the retina is divided by an imaginary vertical line through the middle of the fovea. Fibres from the nasal half, the side toward the nose, cross at the chiasm to the opposite side of the brain. Fibres from the temporal half, the side toward the temple, stay on their own side. Fact three, the numbers: the nasal half of each retina carries slightly more fibres than the temporal half, so a little over half the total crosses, about fifty-three percent, leaving about forty-seven percent uncrossed. Past the chiasm, the crossed stream of one eye and the uncrossed stream of the other merge into a single tract, and that merged stream is our subject.

Host: There it is, and I will confess, nasal and temporal always tangle my feet the moment I try to chain them into a full pathway. Walk me slowly through why the left tract ends up carrying the right side of the world, because heard that way it sounds like a paradox.

Guide: No paradox, only geometry, and it is worth walking every step, because generations of students lose easy marks here. First, remember from Series 1, in an episode called From Light to Vision, that the eye works like a small camera, so the image it casts on the retina is upside down and back to front. Light from the temporal field, the outer half of each eye's view, falls on the nasal retina. Light from the nasal field falls on the temporal retina. Second, apply the crossing. Take the right half of the world. The right eye sees that half with its temporal field, and the image lands on the right eye's nasal retina, whose fibres cross at the chiasm and arrive on the left side of the brain. The left eye sees the same right half of the world with its nasal field, and that image lands on the left eye's temporal retina, whose fibres never cross, so they also remain on the left side. The left tract therefore holds the right eye's crossed fibres and the left eye's uncrossed fibres, and both sets speak for the right half of space. The shortcut rule is worth memorising word for word: each tract speaks for the far side of the world.

Host: So the chiasm is where the two eyes' matching halves are united, and the tract is the first road they travel down together. Is that the right way to picture it?

Guide: It is the right way, with one refinement worth the detour. The tract is indeed the first single track that carries one half of the world from both eyes at once, point matched against point. But the two eyes' fibres do not blend into a uniform mixture here. Inside the tract, the crossed fibres and the uncrossed fibres still run as separate sheets, interleaved like the pages of two different books pressed together, so you can still read which fibres came from which eye. True mixing, layer by layer, only happens at the relay station ahead, and that mixing is exactly where we will open the story next time. That separation also plants a seed we will harvest later: when a tract is injured, the two eyes can lose different amounts of the same half of the world, and reading that inequality is a genuine clinical skill. For now, hold the picture of two tidy ribbons in which the eye of origin is still readable.

Host: Now the walk itself. Start me at the chiasm and take me along the tract, landmark by landmark, the way you would draw it on a board.

Guide: The tract leaves the chiasm at its two outer back corners, the posterolateral angles, and immediately bends backward and outward. Near its start, each tract is separated from its partner by the tuber cinereum, a pale bridge of hypothalamus, the part of the brain that forms the floor of the third ventricle, with the pituitary stalk hanging down just behind and between them. So the chiasm and the two tracts together draw a ring around the front of the underside of the brain, and the pituitary gland sits snugly inside that ring. Each tract then slides backward beneath the brain, floating in the cerebrospinal fluid of the suprasellar cistern, which simply means the expanded pool of fluid above the sella, the little cradle of bone holding the pituitary. That word cistern will return, because the tract spends its whole life bathing in fluid, and this is one reason why slow-growing tumours can squeeze the pathway for years before the patient notices anything at all.

Host: And then comes the bend our season map promised, the swing around the midbrain. Why must the tract go around, rather than straight back through the brain?

Guide: Because of where the journey ends. The relay station, the lateral geniculate body, sits on the outer back corner of the thalamus, the great egg shaped switching hub near the centre of the brain, perched just above the midbrain. Between the chiasm and that corner stand the cerebral peduncles, the two thick pillars of motor and sensory traffic that form the front surface of the midbrain, the narrow top segment of the brainstem. The tract cannot tunnel through those pillars, so it skims around them, hugging the outer edge of each peduncle as it wraps the upper midbrain, gliding in a narrow pool of fluid there called the ambient cistern. Just outside the ribbon, the uncus, the little hook at the front inner edge of the temporal lobe, and the parahippocampal gyrus behind it overhang the tract and press close to it. Keep that tight packing in mind. Radiologists describe this neighbourhood as one of the most crowded addresses in the head, because in a few centimetres the tract shares space with the brainstem, the temporal lobe, the fluid cisterns and the great arteries of the circle of Willis. A swollen temporal lobe, or a vessel ballooning at the base of the skull, has the tract well within reach, which is precisely why tract signs so often arrive together with signs of pressure.

Host: You have hinted at vessels twice without naming them. Which arteries travel with the tract?

Guide: The companion vessel is the anterior choroidal artery, a slender branch of the internal carotid artery, the great vessel of the skull base that supplies a large share of the front of the brain. It arises near the back end of the carotid and runs backward right alongside the optic tract, like a wire stapled to the ribbon, before diving inward through a slit between the temporal lobe and the midbrain to supply deep structures, including part of the tract itself. Near the start of the tract, the posterior communicating artery, another branch of the carotid, also sends small branches to it. Two practical notes follow from that geography. First, these arteries are favourite sites for aneurysms, balloon-like weakenings of the vessel wall, and an enlarging aneurysm there can press directly on the tract and mimic a tumour. Second, a stroke of the anterior choroidal artery can starve the tract in isolation, giving field loss with a completely healthy-looking eye, which is exactly the kind of case that lands, puzzled, in a chair like yours.

Host: So the tract arrives, finally, at the relay. What happens at the finish line?

Guide: At the finish, the tract splits into two roots. The large one, the lateral root, carries the great majority of the fibres, about ninety in every hundred, into the lateral geniculate body, where the relay to the cortex of the brain will happen. The smaller one, the medial root, peels away just before the geniculate and runs in a slender stalk called the brachium of the superior colliculus, brachium being simply the old word for arm. Its fibres skip the relay and head for two reflex centres. One is the pretectal area, a small patch of grey matter at the front of the midbrain roof that re-tunes the pupil when brightness changes. The other is the superior colliculus, a paired bump on the midbrain roof that helps steer rapid orienting movements of the eyes and head. For completeness, a few clock-setting fibres had already slipped away back at the chiasm, heading for the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the tiny pacemaker that keeps the body's daily rhythm aligned with daylight. Notice that these reflex fibres travel inside the tract almost to its end, and hold onto that fact, because in a few minutes it will explain a sign you can watch at the slit lamp. The geniculate itself is the whole subject of our next episode, so today we let it wait at the end of the ribbon.

Host: Now the part I have been waiting for, the wiring diagram inside the ribbon. If the tract is a cable carrying a map of the world, how exactly are the fibres arranged inside it?

Guide: The organising principle has a name, retinotopy, which means that neighbouring points on the retina remain neighbours in the pathway, all the way along, station after station. The tract preserves that point-to-point order with great fidelity. Everywhere else in the body, long cables are content to mix their traffic; the visual system refuses, because its whole job is to keep the address of every point of light. In cross-section the tract is roughly round, and the classic textbook picture gives it four surfaces, dorsal on top, ventral underneath, medial toward the midline, lateral toward the outside. The arrangement, and examiners love it, is this. The macular fibres, the contingent serving the central seeing spot, run in the dorsal part of the tract. Fibres from the upper peripheral retina, which look at the lower field, occupy the ventromedial region, low and toward the middle. Fibres from the lower peripheral retina, which look at the upper field, occupy the ventrolateral region, low and toward the outside. Say it three times aloud and it belongs to you: macula dorsal, upper ventromedial, lower ventrolateral. Notice the inversion built into it, the retina's map folded into the ribbon with the same logic the eye itself uses.

Host: Macula dorsal, upper ventromedial, lower ventrolateral. Filed away. But a cable that wraps around a hill must twist as it goes. Does the map rotate along the way?

Guide: It does, and this is the elegant part of the story. As the tract sweeps around the peduncle, the whole bundle rotates, a slow corkscrew motion, so that by the time the fibres reach the lateral geniculate body they arrive in a new order, an order that sets up the famous layered map inside the relay. Students sometimes ask how a bundle can rotate without losing its order, and the honest answer is that the fibres are never tangled; they are systematically re-sorted, the way a shuffled deck is still a deck if you know the rule. Nothing is ever scrambled. The pathway only re-shelves, and every station from retina to cortex keeps the map readable, which is why the field defects of each station have their own tidy geometry. Precisely how the shelves are stacked inside the geniculate, and how the crossed and uncrossed sheets finally interleave there, is the opening story of episode four. For today, keep the clinically useful takeaway: anywhere along the tract, a small lesion damages a coherent patch of the field, never a random scatter, because the map never stops being a map.

Host: Speaking of the macula, that dorsal contingent deserves a pause. Back in season seven, episode eight, Macula, we saw how anatomically privileged the central spot is. How much of the cable does it claim here?

Guide: An outsized share, and the reason is pure anatomy we have already met. The macula is a tiny patch of retina, but its ganglion cells are packed densely and each sends its own fine, individually wired axon, so central vision claims a disproportionately large slice of the total fibre population, and it keeps that slice at every station of the pathway. In the tract, that macular contingent runs as a thick dorsal wedge and stays wedded to the map all the way to the cortex. One consequence matters enormously in the chair. When a tract is damaged, the field loss usually cuts through the centre as well, splitting the macula in two, a pattern called a macular splitting hemianopia. Why lesions further back sometimes spare the centre instead is a puzzle we deliberately park until episode six, when the visual cortex finally takes the stage. For the tract, assume the split. So a patient can read the chart line by line with both eyes open, and a quiet hemianopia can still be sitting there unnoticed, which is why careful field testing earns its place in every full examination.

Host: Now the chair-side payoff, the reason this hour earns its keep. Suppose something goes wrong along this ribbon. What does optic tract damage actually look like to an optometrist?

Guide: The signature is a homonymous hemianopia, and both words deserve unpacking. Hemianopia means half-blindness, the loss of one half of the visual field. Homonymous means the same half in both eyes, so the loss occupies one side of space, left or right, in both eyes together, and it respects the vertical midline, often with a sharp, almost knife-edged step as the patient reads across the chart. The side rule we built early in the episode does all the work: damage the left tract, and the right field of both eyes goes dark. The tract also adds its own texture to the pattern. Because its fibres are still tightly bundled and still sorted by eye, the defects in the two eyes are usually unequal in size, depth and density, a quality called incongruity, and incongruity is the classic fingerprint of a lesion this far forward in the pathway. If Series 1 gave you the vocabulary, in the episode called Visual Field, this is where that vocabulary starts paying rent in the consulting room.

Host: Now, the pupils. What do they tell us about the tract?

Guide: The pupils give the tract away, and this is genuinely elegant anatomy. Recall from the finish line discussion that roughly one tract fibre in ten is not destined for conscious sight at all. Those are the reflex fibres bound for the pretectal area, and they travel inside the tract until the very last moment, leaving only just before the geniculate. Two consequences follow. First, the relative afferent pupillary defect, the finding in which one pupil responds more weakly when the light swings to it in the swinging flashlight test. A tract lesion destroys more afferent fibres of the eye opposite to the tract, because crossed fibres outnumber uncrossed ones, about fifty-three percent against forty-seven, so the defect appears in the eye on the same side as the field loss, contralateral to the damaged tract. Second, the old bedside sign called Wernicke's hemianopic pupil: with a very narrow beam aimed at the blind half of each retina, the pupil barely stirs, while the same beam on the seeing half pulls a brisk response, because the pupillomotor fibres still ride inside the damaged ribbon. The full reflex arc, from pretectal neurons to the sphincter muscle of the iris, is episode seven's territory, and we will not steal it.

Host: And the optic disc? Weeks or months after such an injury, what does the nerve head show?

Guide: The disc writes the history of which fibres died, and it follows straight from the disc anatomy we built in season seven, episode ten, Optic Disc. Fibres from the peripheral temporal retina arch above and below the disc to enter at its upper and lower poles, while the straight-running fibres of the nasal retina enter along the horizontal nasal margin, and the dense papillomacular bundle, carrying the macula's output, enters the temporal side. Because the vertical dividing line of the hemianopia runs right through the macula, a tract lesion damages the nasal retinal fibres and half of the papillomacular bundle of the eye opposite the tract, so that disc pales in two horizontal bands, nasal and temporal, with the poles spared, a pattern literally named bow-tie or band atrophy. The eye on the tract's own side has lost temporal retinal fibres spread through all quadrants, and its disc pales more diffusely. So the pair you may meet, months later, is a bow-tie in one eye and general pallor in the other, a combination that points squarely backward to a hemianopia the patient may never have noticed, because walking around with half a world missing is remarkably easy to ignore.

Host: The discs are practically a case history written on the nerve head. Now the localisation ladder. In the chair, how do I tell a tract lesion apart from something further back at the cortex?

Guide: Run the ladder, rung by rung, and the anatomy will answer. Rung one, the field pattern: an incongruous hemianopia, with the two eyes' defects disagreeing, leans forward toward the tract, while a highly congruous one, matching almost perfectly, leans back toward the cortex, whose hour is episode six. Rung two, the pupils: a relative afferent defect and a demonstrable hemianopic pupil response point to the tract, because the reflex fibres are still aboard; cortex lesions leave the pupils silent, since those passengers left the bus long before. Rung three, the discs: a bow-tie in one eye with diffuse pallor in the other nearly settles the argument. Both disc signs take weeks to appear, though, because the injured axons must first die back and the disc must lose its living fibre bulk, so early cases are judged on fields and pupils. Rung four, the setting: tract lesions come with pressure from below and beside, a pituitary tumour growing up and sideways past the chiasm, a craniopharyngioma in a child or young adult, an aneurysm of the carotid or posterior communicating artery, demyelinating disease, or an anterior choroidal stroke. And when the pattern is mapped, remember what Series 1 urged in an episode called When to Refer: recognise the edge of your role, explain calmly, and send the patient for brain imaging without losing time.

Host: A beautifully practical ladder. Now let us compress the whole hour. Give me the tract in one paragraph I can rehearse on the commute.

Guide: Here it is. Two flat ribbons leave the posterolateral corners of the chiasm, each carrying the opposite half of the visual world from both eyes: left tract, right field, right tract, left field. Each sweeps backward and outward beneath the brain, hugs the outer edge of the cerebral peduncle of the midbrain, glides beneath the uncus of the temporal lobe in the ambient cistern, and ends at the lateral geniculate body on the thalamus, where about ninety percent of its fibres enter the relay while the rest peel away for the pupillary and orienting reflexes. Inside, the map is preserved with the macula dorsal, the upper peripheral retina ventromedial, the lower peripheral retina ventrolateral, and the whole bundle twisting slowly on its way. The anterior choroidal artery runs alongside like a companion wire, and the posterior communicating artery helps feed the start. Damage produces a contralateral, usually incongruous, macular splitting homonymous hemianopia, a relative afferent pupillary defect and bow-tie pallor in the eye opposite the lesion, and diffuse pallor in the eye on its own side.

Host: And one pearl, something to carry into Monday morning's clinic?

Guide: Here is the pearl I would choose. The field chart and the tract describe each other, and the vertical midline is the anchor that joins them. Because retinotopy never breaks, you can predict the bundle from the defect and the defect from the bundle: any field loss occupying the same half of space in both eyes, stopping cleanly at the vertical midline, means the lesion lies behind the chiasm, and the tract is the first station in line. Then think about the patient in front of you. People with these defects drift past furniture on one side, lose their place reading halfway across a line of print, and blame their glasses, because their central acuity is often perfect and nobody thinks to test the half of the world they cannot report. The optometrist who charts fields patiently is frequently the first person in the whole healthcare system to detect the quiet mass, the ballooning vessel, or the small stroke hiding behind a complaint that sounded like a need for new spectacles.
