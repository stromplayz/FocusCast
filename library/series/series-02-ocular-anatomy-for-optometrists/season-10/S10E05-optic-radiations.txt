Host: Ready. Let us start with the name itself, because I have heard three different names for this thing already. Optic radiations. Geniculocalcarine tract. Which one is right, and what is each word doing?

Guide: All three names point at the same bundle of fibres, and each name teaches something. Optic radiations is the oldest and the most visual. Radiation comes from the Latin radius, a ray or a spoke, and if you could see this structure you would understand instantly. The fibres leave the lateral geniculate body as a compact little fan and then swing open across the white matter of the hemisphere, like the ribs of a fan opening, or the spokes of a wheel seen edge on. The second name, geniculocalcarine tract, is the itinerary. Geniculo, from the geniculate body where the fibres begin. Calcarine, from the calcarine cortex where they end. It is literally a street address, start to finish. Now, whose fibres are these? They are the axons of the relay cells inside the lateral geniculate body. Remember the chain we built back in season seven, in the episode Retinal Layers: a photoreceptor hands to a bipolar cell, the bipolar cell hands to a ganglion cell, and the ganglion cell's axon is what we called the optic nerve. That axon ends in the geniculate body, and the relay cell on the other side of that synapse sends its own axon onward. Neuroanatomists call that the third-order neuron of the visual pathway. On each side, on the order of a million axons make this journey, carrying everything one half of the visual world has to say.

Host: A million axons, and every one of them has to reach the very back of the head. Before we follow them, tell me about the obstacle course. What does the brain put in their way?

Guide: The obstacle is beautiful, and it is the reason this episode exists. Deep inside each hemisphere sits the lateral ventricle, one of the brain's fluid-filled chambers, a long curved cavity of cerebrospinal fluid. And the destination, the visual cortex, lies folded on the inner face of the occipital lobe, immediately behind and beside the back end of that cavity. So a fibre leaving the geniculate body cannot simply march straight across the hemisphere; it would dive straight into the ventricle. The solution is a sling. The fibres leave the geniculate body, pass through a sheet of white matter called the retrolenticular part of the internal capsule - the internal capsule being the great highway of fibres between the thalamus and the rest of the hemisphere, and retrolenticular meaning behind the lentiform nucleus, the lens-shaped mass of deep grey matter. Then the fan spreads and wraps itself around the outer wall of the ventricle, arching around the front tip of its lower horn and around its back horn. Textbooks traditionally describe three bundles inside the fan. An anterior bundle that swings forward into the temporal lobe - that is Meyer's loop. A large posterior bundle that sweeps back through the parietal lobe. And a central mass between them that runs almost straight backward. In a cross-section of the brain, all of these lie stacked as one compact strip along the wall of the ventricle, a strip anatomists call the sagittal stratum.

Host: Let me make sure I hold the picture. The fibres are not wandering for fun. The ventricle is in the way, and the fan drapes around it like a sling around a post?

Guide: Exactly like that. And there is a lovely consequence: the shape of the radiation mirrors the shape of the ventricle. Where the cavity reaches forward under the temporal lobe, the lowest fibres reach forward with it, hugging its roof, and that forward reach is precisely Meyer's loop. Where the cavity widens toward its back end, the fan wraps around that widening. Neuroanatomy often pretends to be arbitrary, but here the geometry is almost architectural. The water chamber bends the wires.

Host: Now the famous part. Meyer's loop. Every neuroanatomy course names it, and every field-defect lecture leans on it. Walk me through the loop itself. Where does it actually go?

Guide: Picture the lower horn of the lateral ventricle reaching forward like a finger under the temporal lobe, toward the pole of that lobe, the front tip, deep behind your temple. The anterior bundle of the radiation runs forward along the roof of that horn, rounds its front tip, and then turns sharply back toward the occipital lobe. That forward-reaching arch is Meyer's loop, and it carries the fibres of the radiation deeper into the temporal lobe than any other part. How deep? The classic dissection studies put its front edge somewhere around three and a half centimetres from the temporal pole, and modern fibre-tracking scans, which trace these bundles in living patients, often find it reaching a little further, up toward five centimetres. So a working range of roughly three and a half to five centimetres from the temporal pole is what most textbooks now quote, with the honest admission that people vary. That variability is not trivia. It is the whole clinical story of this bundle, and we will come straight back to it. One more piece of anatomy before we leave the picture: the loop is not a separate little organ. It is simply the most anterior sweep of the same fan, the part of the sling that had to travel furthest around the water.

Host: Three and a half to five centimetres. Why would an optometrist care about a measurement inside the temporal lobe? We do not operate there.

Guide: Because someone else does, and because our charts catch the consequences. The temporal lobe is surgery country. Surgeons operate there for epilepsy that will not settle with medication, and for tumours, and one of the standard operations removes the front portion of the temporal lobe. Now think about what sits in that white matter: the forward sweep of the visual map itself. Cut it, and the patient does not lose an eye, or sharpness, or colour. They lose one corner of the visual world, silently, in both eyes at once. So the distance from the temporal pole to Meyer's loop is a safety margin, and neurosurgeons think about it before every resection. The classic numbers came from dissection; today many surgical teams map the loop in the individual patient with diffusion imaging before they operate, because the range between people is wide enough to matter. Here is the point for our side of the desk. When a patient walks out of a temporal lobe operation with a perfectly healthy retina and a brand new quadrant defect on the chart, the anatomy in this episode is the explanation. The eye was never touched. The map's wiring was.

Host: Say the consequence precisely, then. Which fibres ride the loop, and what exactly goes missing when the loop is cut?

Guide: The loop carries fibres from the lower half of the retina - after the chiasm, from both eyes, the lower retinal halves that watch the upper half of the visual field. And now the flip that Series 1 drilled: the retina inverts the world. The inferior retina looks at the superior field. So fibres from the lower retina, riding the temporal loop, report the upper field. Add the crossing, which we mapped in episode ninety-two, the Optic Chiasm: after the chiasm, each radiation carries the opposite half of the visual field of both eyes. Put those two facts together and you have the classic defect. One cut Meyer's loop takes the superior quadrant on the opposite side out of both eyes - a superior homonymous quadrantanopia. Homonymous, meaning the same region of the field is affected in the two eyes. Quadrantanopia, meaning a quarter of each field. Clinicians have a nickname for it that patients remember forever: pie in the sky. A slice lifted out of the upper field. And because the defect is homonymous, the patient often arrives in the optometry chair having noticed only that things seem to appear from one side, or that they keep clipping objects on one side. The eyes are innocent. The loop, on the far side of the brain, is the culprit.

Host: Now the other course. What is the parietal half of the fan doing while the loop is making its detour under the temporal lobe?

Guide: The posterior bundle sweeps backward through the white matter of the parietal lobe, the lobe above and behind the temporal lobe, arching around the back horn and the widened middle of the lateral ventricle. Its journey is more direct than the loop's, and its payload is the mirror image. The parietal bundle carries fibres from the superior half of the retina, and the superior retina watches the inferior visual field. So the pairing is worth committing to memory as a couple. Temporal lobe, lower retina, upper field. Parietal lobe, upper retina, lower field. A lesion in the parietal radiation - and the classic causes there are strokes and tumours - erases the inferior quadrant on the opposite side from both eyes: an inferior homonymous quadrantanopia. There is no charming nickname for this one, but there is a symmetry students love once they see it: pie in the sky for the temporal loop, and for the parietal bundle, a slice drops out of the floor of the field. And notice that both names of the defect describe where the patient loses sight, not where the damage sits. The anatomy is up in the parietal lobe; the hole is down in the field.

Host: And the middle of the fan? Between the loop below and the parietal bundle above, something must be running straight through.

Guide: That central mass is dominated by the macular fibres. The macula, the sharpest-seeing centre of the retina that we gave its own episode back in season seven, episode eight, sends a huge contingent of fibres into the radiation, and they occupy the middle of the fan and take the straightest, shortest course of all, running almost directly backward toward the occipital pole. They are the widest, busiest lanes in the fan, and they are why the centre of the visual world occupies so much of the pathway's width. Around them the peripheral fibres spread outward, upper retina above and lower retina below, exactly as we said.

Host: You keep saying map. Retinotopy. Say plainly what survives the journey and what does not. Is the picture in the fibres still a picture?

Guide: It is still a picture, and this is the deepest idea in the episode. Retinotopy means that neighbours in the retina remain neighbours in every structure along the pathway. Fibres that start side by side travel side by side and finish side by side. The radiation does not scramble the map; it stretches it. Think of a folding fan lying closed: every rib is in order, touching its neighbours. Open the fan and the ribs separate, but the order is untouched. That is the radiation. The upper retina's fibres ride the parietal bundle. The lower retina's fibres ride the temporal loop. The macula holds the middle and runs straight. We watched the same discipline all season long: episode ninety-three, the Optic Tract, showed fibre order inside the tract, and episode ninety-four showed the map preserved through the six layers of the geniculate body. The radiation simply continues the tradition across the longest gap of all. Series 1 made this idea clinical in the episode Visual Field, when we treated the field as a chart that can be read defect by defect. Today you can finally see why that chart works: every quadrant of the field has a dedicated cable, and the cables keep their neighbourhoods all the way to the back of the head.

Host: So if a cable is cut, the defect is predictable. Walk me through the localizing logic the way you would teach it to a third-year student.

Guide: Start with the shape of the hole. A quarter of the field, the same side in both eyes, points into the radiation, and the quadrant's height points to the bundle. Upper field gone: think temporal lobe, Meyer's loop. Lower field gone: think parietal lobe. If the whole half-field is gone in both eyes, a complete homonymous hemianopia, think of the entire radiation cut, which is what a large stroke in the middle cerebral artery territory can do, because the temporal and parietal stretches of the fan live largely in that artery's supply. The second clue is congruence, meaning how closely the defect matches between the two eyes. Radiation lesions are often incongruous: the missing slice in one eye and the missing slice in the other are similar but not identical, because the corresponding fibres of the two eyes, though neighbours since the chiasm, are not perfectly married until they reach the cortex. The rule of thumb: the further back along the pathway you go, the more congruous the defect becomes. The third clue, and this is the quiet gift of postgeniculate anatomy, is that the pupils are normal. The fibres that drive the pupillary light reflex leave the main pathway long before the radiation, heading to their own relay, and we will wire that reflex properly in episode seven, the Pupillary Pathway. Normal pupils beside a hemifield loss is itself a localizing sign, and it is one the optometrist is perfectly placed to collect.

Host: You mentioned supply twice, the middle cerebral artery. The radiation is long. Does it really change landlords along the way?

Guide: It does, and the landlords' names are worth knowing, because they tie this episode to stroke reports you will read for the rest of your career. The front of the journey, the geniculate body and the first stretch of fibres including the region of Meyer's loop, is supplied largely by the anterior choroidal artery, a slender branch of the internal carotid artery. The long middle stretches, the temporal and parietal courses, are fed by deep penetrating branches of the middle cerebral artery, the same artery whose territory we blamed for the big hemianopias. And the final approach to the occipital pole belongs to the posterior cerebral artery, whose calcarine branch will also feed the cortex we are heading toward. So the radiation begins in the carotid circulation and ends in the circulation fed from the back of the neck, and somewhere in the parietal white matter the supply hands over. The clinical echo is neat. A tiny stroke in the anterior choroidal artery can produce a homonymous field defect that looks exactly like the loop lesions we described, and a posterior cerebral stroke produces the classic complete hemianopia. The field defect rarely tells you the artery's name by itself, but it tells you the neighbourhood, and the neighbourhood tells the radiologist where to look.

Host: Then let us finish the journey. The fibres have rounded the ventricle. Describe the last stretch and the arrival.

Guide: Toward the occipital lobe the bundles reconverge into that compact sheet along the wall of the ventricle, and then, one by one, the fibres leave the white matter and end in grey matter folded on the medial surface of the occipital lobe - the surface you would see if you sliced the brain down the middle between the hemispheres. There lies a horizontal sulcus called the calcarine sulcus, and the visual cortex spreads above and below it. That is the calcarine cortex, our destination. The order of arrival preserves the map one last time. The parietal bundle, carrying the superior retina and the lower field, terminates above the calcarine sulcus, in a wedge-shaped fold called the cuneus. The temporal bundle, carrying the inferior retina and the upper field, terminates below it, in a fold called the lingual gyrus, named for its tongue-like shape. And the macular fibres, remember, hold the centre of the fan and run all the way out to the occipital pole itself, so the centre of the visual world arrives at the very back of the brain. That is as far as we go today. What the cortex does with the arriving signal - how its own layers are built, and why some parts of the map are given colossal amounts of it - that story belongs to our next episode, and I will not steal a word of it.

Host: This is the moment to make it practical. A patient sits in my chair. Their fields show one quadrant gone, both eyes, upper field, left side. Retina looks normal, discs look normal. Talk me through what this episode just did for me.

Guide: It just handed you a differential that lives inside the skull. First, the side: a left-sided field loss in both eyes means the right hemisphere's pathway, because beyond the chiasm each side of the brain represents the opposite side of the world. Second, the height: an upper-field quadrant implicates the lower retina's bundle, the temporal loop, on the right side. Third, the pupils: if they are brisk and equal, the lesion sits behind the geniculate body, in radiation country. Your optic discs can look entirely normal, because the damage is two lifetimes of neurons downstream of the eye. That is the trap and the triumph of this anatomy: the fundus is innocent, the field is guilty, and the lesion is in the temporal lobe. From there the job is the one we built in Series 1, the pillar about management and referral. Document the defect, confirm it repeats, and route the patient to the right specialist quickly, because in a temporal lobe the causes include tumours, and time matters. In Series 1 we met the Patient With Sudden Vision Change, and this is exactly the anatomy that turns that complaint into an urgent story rather than a glasses problem.

Host: One last skeptic's question before we let you go. Is this fan-and-loop picture real, or is it a textbook cartoon that falls apart in a living brain?

Guide: It is real enough to operate around, and modern imaging has made it personal. Diffusion tractography - a scanning method that follows the direction of water movement along fibre bundles - can trace the radiation and even sketch Meyer's loop in an individual patient before temporal lobe surgery, and those scans confirmed the classic courses while adding honest nuance. The three bundles are really a continuum, one smooth fan whose front edge dips furthest forward, not three separate cables with labels tied on. The distances vary more between people than the classic numbers admitted, which is exactly why surgical teams now image rather than assume. But the retinotopic order - upper retina through the parietal lobe, lower retina through the temporal lobe, macula in the middle - is not a cartoon. It is confirmed, and it is the reason the field defects we described are so cleanly shaped. Anatomy that survives a century of dissection and a decade of living-brain imaging is anatomy you can trust.
