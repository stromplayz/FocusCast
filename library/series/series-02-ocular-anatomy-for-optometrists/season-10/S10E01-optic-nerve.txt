# Description: Season ten of Focus Cast by focuslinks opens the final season, Neuroanatomy for Optometrists, and starts at the true beginning of the visual pathway. Meet the optic nerve through its fibre composition: about one point two million axons born in retinal ganglion cells, gathered across the retina as a transparent veil and insulated into brain white matter behind the eye. Across nine seasons we built the eye piece by piece: the orbit and its walls, the lacrimal system, the conjunctiva and sclera, the cornea, the anterior chamber and uveal tract, the lens, the vitreous and posterior segment, the retina and choroid, and the muscles that move the globe. Those parts do nothing for vision until their signals travel, and that travel is our subject now. This season we follow the visual message from the retina all the way to the cortex, and then we wire up the pupil, the near response, and the cranial nerves that serve the eye. Today, in episode one, we begin with the optic nerve itself. Not the nerve as a physical specimen, because season eight already built that carefully, but the nerve as the opening stretch of a pathway. Here is our route: we will find the fibres at their true birthplace in the retinal ganglion cells, count them, sort them into their functional families, learn why this nerve is actually a piece of brain, watch the fibres keep their retinal map as they gather, and end at the doorway of the chiasm with a first look at the partial crossing ahead. Let us walk in.

Host: Then let us start at the very beginning, because today is all about fibre composition, and I want to meet each fibre where it is born. When we say the visual pathway begins at the optic nerve, where exactly is that beginning? Point to it for me.

Guide: The true start is a cell body, and I can name the cell precisely. Every fibre of the optic nerve is the axon of a retinal ganglion cell. Back in Series 1, an episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information introduced this cable as the messenger that carries sight to the brain, and that picture was the right one for the foundations. Today we open the messenger up and ask what it is actually made of. The ganglion cell is the retina's output neuron, the only nerve cell in the retina whose axon leaves the eye. Its body sits in the ganglion cell layer, the strip of cell bodies nearest the vitreous; episode 67, Retinal Layers, mapped that neighbourhood for us. From each body, short branching dendrites reach into the inner plexiform layer just below, where they collect finished signals from bipolar cells and amacrine cells. So the ganglion cell catches no light of its own; it gathers the retina's computation and converts it into a train of electrical impulses. One small honesty note: not every body in that layer is a ganglion cell, because scattered displaced amacrine cells live there too, but those contribute no axons to the nerve. And the population is small. A human retina holds roughly one point two million ganglion cells, and careful counting studies put healthy eyes anywhere from about seven hundred thousand to one point seven million. Each cell contributes exactly one axon, so the composition of the optic nerve in one sentence is this: about one point two million axons, one per ganglion cell, nothing more.

Host: That one sentence hides a startling piece of arithmetic, and I want to slow down on it. The retina seasons told us the eye holds something like a hundred million photoreceptors. If only about a million cables leave the eye, what happens to all the rest of the signal?

Guide: You have put your finger on one of the most instructive numbers in the whole eye. Episode 74, Photoreceptor Distribution, gave us the census: roughly one hundred and twenty million rods and about six million cones in each retina. Count the ganglion cells and you find about one point two million. So across the retina as a whole, roughly one hundred photoreceptors feed into every output fibre. That funnel is called convergence, and it is not waste; it is design. Pooling many rods beneath one ganglion cell lets their faint whispers add together, and that summation is why you can glimpse a single candle on a dark hillside. Sensitivity is bought by merging. But merging blurs position, so the retina spends its wiring budget carefully. In the fovea the design flips: a single cone can connect through a single midget bipolar cell to a single midget ganglion cell, a private line with almost no sharing. That one-to-one wiring in the centre is the anatomical price of reading the smallest letters, and it is why the macula, which episode 68 drew for us, claims a wildly disproportionate share of the nerve's fibres. So when you picture the one point two million axons, picture a budget statement of the retina's priorities, with central vision richly over-funded and the far periphery running on shared lines.

Host: Now let me follow one of those axons physically on its way out. Say a ganglion cell in the upper outer quadrant of the retina has just fired. Walk me along its fibre, from the moment it leaves the cell body to the moment it exits the eye.

Guide: From the cell body the axon bends and runs sideways, parallel to the retinal surface, in the innermost stratum of the retina, a translucent veil called the nerve fibre layer. It is astonishingly thin, a fraction of a millimetre at most, and yet every word the retina ever says to the brain travels through that sheet. The fibres curve inward toward a single exit, the optic disc, and season eight gave that doorway two full episodes: episode 77, Optic Nerve Head, and episode 78, Lamina Cribrosa, the sieve of collagen beams the axons must squeeze through. The disc carries no photoreceptors, which is the anatomical reason every eye owns a blind spot; you first met it in Series 1, in the episode called Visual Field. Two fibre facts matter enormously right here. First, inside the eye these axons are unmyelinated, running bare, without insulation, and that is deliberate: myelin is opaque and bulky, so bare axons keep the retina transparent and the image clean. Second, the fibres do not arrive at the disc as a shapeless crowd. They arrive in ordered bundles, and that order will matter for everything this season, so we will pin it down shortly.

Host: You said they arrive at the disc in ordered bundles, and I realize I have been picturing a sharp corner this whole time. The retina is a curved wall and the exit is a hole pointing backwards. Do all these fibres really make that turn?

Guide: They do, and the geometry is worth a minute. Every axon running across the nerve fibre layer must make roughly a right angle turn at the disc, pivoting out of the retinal plane and diving backwards through the small scleral canal, an opening only about one and a half to two millimetres across. Imagine a million thin wires lining a dome and funnelling through one small drain, and you have the picture. The fibres from the far periphery travel the longest arc and make the sharpest turn, while fibres near the disc arrive almost straight. Episode 77 described how they pile into that canal in layers, and episode 80, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, drew the choreography: arcuate bundles sweeping around above and below, the dense papillomacular bundle from the macula entering straight, and the seam between them called the nasal raphe. I will not redraw that map today, because it is already yours. But notice what this geometry buys us: a million delicate fibres exit through a hole smaller than a pea, and in a healthy eye not one of them is pinched or lost. That is anatomical engineering worth respecting.

Host: You said the axons run bare inside the eye, which means the insulation story must begin somewhere else. Where does myelin start, what exactly is it, and what does it do for the message these fibres carry?

Guide: The change happens just behind the eye, at the lamina cribrosa. Once the axons have squeezed through the sieve and left the globe, glial cells called oligodendrocytes begin wrapping them in myelin, a fatty insulation laid down in short segments with tiny naked gaps between them, called nodes of Ranvier. An electrical impulse cannot flow efficiently through fat, so instead of crawling it jumps from node to node, a leapfrog named saltatory conduction, and that leaping is many times faster than conduction along a bare fibre. Myelin is a large part of the reason the retinal message reaches the brain almost instantly instead of arriving late. So picture the finished composition: more than a million fine axons, bare as they cross the living retina, insulated behind the lamina, carrying their impulses onward in the dark. And most of these axons are among the finest in the entire nervous system, many of them only around a micron across, a fraction of the width of a single red blood cell. The thicker ones among them belong to a particular family with a particular job, and we will meet that family soon.

Host: Before we meet the families, I want to pick up one word you used. You said oligodendrocytes, and I noticed you did not say Schwann cells, the insulation makers we hear about for nerves in the arms and legs. Was that word choice deliberate?

Guide: Completely deliberate, and it is the deepest fact about this nerve. Schwann cells insulate peripheral nerves, the wires of the limbs and body wall. Oligodendrocytes belong to the central nervous system, the brain and spinal cord, and the optic nerve is insulated by oligodendrocytes because the optic nerve is not really a peripheral nerve at all. It is a tract of the brain, a stretched-out strand of white matter, parked in the orbit for convenience. Embryology makes this wonderfully vivid. The eye begins as a hollow outpouching of the forebrain called the optic vesicle, and the stalk connecting it to the brain, the optic stalk, is what elongates into the optic nerve. The retina facing the vitreous is literally a piece of brain, thinned and specialized to catch light, and the nerve is the stalk that never stopped being brain. That identity has consequences you already know from practice. Central tracts do not regrow after injury the way peripheral nerves can, which is why optic nerve damage tends to be permanent. And because the nerve is central, it carries the brain's own wrappings: episode 79, Optic Nerve Structure, walked us along its roughly fifty millimetre course in four segments and through its three meningeal sheaths, the coverings it shares with the brain itself. Structure was that season's gift. Function is ours.

Host: That is a strong frame for the season. Now to the families. When a million and more fibres bundle into this brain strand, are they all carrying the same kind of message, or are there different kinds of cable inside the bundle?

Guide: Different kinds, and this is where fibre composition becomes genuinely beautiful. Anatomists sort retinal ganglion cells into three great classes by the size of their bodies and dendritic trees. The largest family by far is the midget ganglion cell, named for its small body and tiny dendritic tree. These make up roughly four fifths of all ganglion cells, they dominate the macula, and in the fovea they are the private one-to-one line we met earlier. Their axons are thin, and they answer in a sustained, steady way, holding their signal for as long as the stimulus lasts. They are the anatomy of fine detail and of red against green colour, and their fibres travel to the middle four layers of the lateral geniculate body, the brain's great relay, where this stream is called parvocellular, from the Greek for small. The second family is the parasol ganglion cell, its dendritic tree spread wide like an umbrella, about one tenth of the population. Parasol cells pool from many photoreceptors, answer briefly and briskly to change rather than holding steady, and they are exquisitely tuned to motion, flicker and timing. Their axons are among the thickest in the nerve and conduct fastest, and they feed the two outer layers of the same relay, forming the magnocellular stream, magno meaning large. The third family, roughly the remaining tenth, is the small bistratified cell, bistratified meaning its dendrites branch in two separate tiers of the inner plexiform layer. These carry the blue against yellow story that begins in the short wavelength cones, and their fibres run in the gaps between the relay's main layers, forming the koniocellular stream, from the Greek for dust, because the cells are dust-small. Episode 94, Lateral Geniculate Body, owns the six-layer map of that relay, so today I stop at its doorstep.

Host: You slipped in a fourth kind almost under your breath, fibres that are not about pictures at all. I have heard that a pupil can still react even in an eye that can no longer see. Is that our missing family?

Guide: It is, and it is my favourite corner of this anatomy. Scattered among the million are a few thousand ganglion cells carrying their own light-sensitive pigment, called melanopsin, which lets them respond to light directly, without rods or cones. Anatomists call them intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, and they are a tiny fraction of the total, well under one percent. Their axons bypass the picture relay entirely. Some run to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the clock of the brain, setting the body's rhythms of day and night. Others run to the pretectal area, the pivot of the pupillary light reflex. Sit with what that means: the optic nerve is not only the cable of sight, it is also the body's principal daylight sensor cable, which is why an eye whose rods and cones are gone can still constrict its pupil to a bright torch. We will wire the whole pupillary loop in episode 97, Pupillary Pathway. For today, hold only the fibre fact: the composition includes a small, ancient, light-sensitive minority, and it watches the sky for us rather than painting pictures of it.

Host: Let us take all of this to the chair, because this is where it earns its keep. An optometrist looks into the back of an eye every working day. When we gaze at that disc, which of these fibres are we actually seeing, and what does the composition change about how we examine?

Guide: More than you might think. When we describe the nerve fibre layer with our lenses, or measure it with optical coherence tomography, we are photographing the very population we have just catalogued: the ganglion cell axons in their final stretch before the disc. That instrument reports their combined thickness in microns, and thinning there is thinning of the nerve's fibre population itself. In glaucoma, ganglion cell axons are precisely the tissue the disease removes, fibre by fibre, which is why that measurement has become a daily clinical number. The classes matter at the chair as well. Because parasol cells carry motion and small bistratified cells carry blue against yellow, early optic nerve disease can show itself in motion sensitivity and in blue-yellow discrimination before the smallest letters falter, since those letters lean on the enormous midget population. And because the fibres keep their retinal addresses, damage follows the map, a point we will make in a moment. I will leave the disease detail to the clinical texts. The takeaway today is compositional: the nerve is not one wire but a million wires of at least four kinds, and each kind keeps its own appointment deeper in the brain.

Host: You just said the fibres keep their retinal addresses, and that deserves pinning down. A million fibres converging on a hole two millimetres wide sounds like the perfect recipe for a tangle. Is the cable actually organized inside?

Guide: Beautifully organized, and the order has a name: retinotopy, the principle that fibres from neighbouring points of the retina remain neighbours inside the nerve. The optic nerve is not a knotted rope; it is a folded map. Peripheral fibres run in the periphery of the cable, and the macula sends a dense wedge that comes to occupy the central region of the nerve further back. Episode 80, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, drew the full geography, the arcuate bundles, the papillomacular bundle and the nasal raphe, so I will not redraw it. But keep the headline, because the entire season stands on it: inside the nerve, place is preserved. A patch of retina has an address, and its fibres carry that address like a postcode all the way to the brain. This is why a lost patch of fibres announces itself as a matching shape in the field, and it is why the crossing we are about to preview can be surgical in its precision. Nothing in this season will make sense without retinotopy, so if you keep one phrase from today, keep that one.

Host: Then let us walk the fibres to the end of the nerve, because that is where our season is heading. They leave the eye, run back through the orbit, and arrive at the chiasm. Give us the preview. What happens to our million fibres there?

Guide: Here is the preview, and it is one of the elegant facts in all of neuroanatomy. Not every fibre crosses to the other side of the brain. Only about fifty-three percent cross. The fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross to the opposite side, while the fibres from the temporal half stay on their own side and simply continue backwards. Why fifty-three percent? Because that is roughly the share of the ganglion cell population lying nasal to the fovea, so the crossing fraction simply mirrors the retina's own proportions, fibre for fibre. And why cross at all? Remember that the two eyes sit side by side and see overlapping but offset views of the world. The nasal half of your left retina and the temporal half of your right retina are both watching the same thing, the left half of the visual world. For the brain to hold one coherent picture of each half of the world, those two matching half-images must be gathered onto the same side, and the chiasm is the sorting hall where that gathering happens. Half the letters go through, half stay home, and each outgoing bundle carries a complete, matched half of the world drawn from both eyes. That is the preview. The full crossing map, the weave of the fibres, the neighbours above and below, and what pressure there does, all of that belongs to episode 92, Optic Chiasm.

Host: Let me test the logic, because it sounds like a deliberate design choice. Why not cross everything, or cross nothing? What exactly does the halfway split buy the visual system?

Guide: Cross nothing, and each hemisphere would receive the entire scene from one eye only, two complete but slightly different pictures that never sort themselves into a single world. Cross everything, and each hemisphere would still receive one eye's entire view, merely the other eye's. Either way the brain holds eye-shaped pictures instead of world-shaped ones. Split the fibres exactly as the anatomy does, nasal across and temporal straight on, and something remarkable appears: each hemisphere receives the same half of the world from both eyes, two views of one half-scene taken from two slightly different positions. Those two views, gathered side by side, are the raw material the brain uses for depth. So the partial crossing is not a compromise; it is the hinge on which binocular vision turns, and it is the anatomical foundation beneath every field pattern you will ever interpret. That is precisely why this season stops and lingers at the chiasm next, before building anything on top of it.

Host: One more composition question before we tour the season. We keep calling this the cable of sight. Does anything else actually ride in the optic nerve? Does it carry touch or pain fibres of its own, the way a nerve in the hand would?

Guide: No, and the answer is revealing. The optic nerve contains one cargo only: the axons of retinal ganglion cells, every single one headed from retina toward brain. There are no touch fibres, no pain fibres, no fibres of sensation running in either direction, which is one more way it differs from every true peripheral nerve. When the eye hurts, that pain reaches the brain by a different road entirely, the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve, which supplies the surface of the eye and the orbit. Even the deep ache behind the eye that accompanies an inflamed optic nerve is really the pain of the inflamed meningeal coverings around the nerve, which do carry trigeminal endings, and not of the visual fibres themselves. The composition is pure: sight out, and nothing else. We will take the full inventory of the cranial nerves that serve the eye in episode 99, Cranial Nerves in Ocular Function.
