# Description: Where does the optic tract finally end? In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we arrive at the lateral geniculate body, the thalamic relay that every visual signal must pass through on the road from eye to cortex. Explore the six layers and which eye feeds each one, the magnocellular and parvocellular streams, the retinotopic map with its huge macular territory, the blood supply that explains macular sparing, and the output fibres that become the optic radiations. A neuroanatomy episode for optometrists and eye care students, written for the ear.

Host: Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library where we take one topic at a time and give it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series, and we are deep in season ten, Neuroanatomy for Optometrists. For three episodes now we have been following the visual pathway switch by switch, the way engineers trace a cable through a building. In episode one, Optic Nerve, we walked the cable itself, the million-odd ganglion cell axons leaving each eye. In episode two, Optic Chiasm, we stood at the great crossing, where the nasal fibres of each eye swap sides. And in episode three, Optic Tract, we followed the bundled highway as it swept around the midbrain. Today that highway finally ends. The tract does not run on forever; it terminates in a small, knobbly, beautifully organized relay called the lateral geniculate body. Six layers, two eyes sorted onto different floors, a map of the visual world folded inside, and an output that grows into the next great bundle of the pathway. Guide, take us to this station and tell us what we are standing on.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to every listener who has walked this pathway with us so far. Let us start with the name, because the name is a picture. Geniculate comes from the Latin geniculum, a little knee, and it names a bend. The visual pathway makes a sharp bend here, around the side of the midbrain, and this small body sits right in the elbow of that bend, the way a sleeve covers an elbow. We say lateral geniculate body to distinguish it from its neighbour, the medial geniculate body, which sits nearby and is the relay for hearing. Same family name, opposite senses: lateral for seeing, medial for listening. Some books say lateral geniculate body and some say lateral geniculate nucleus, and you can treat those as the same thing. The word nucleus here simply means a compact cluster of nerve cell bodies working together, not the centre of a single cell. And this whole cluster belongs to a structure we have not visited before in this series, the thalamus, so let me place that first, because the address tells you a lot about the job.

Host: Yes, that word came up and I want it clear before we go deeper. Where exactly is the thalamus, what is it for, and how does our little relay fit into it?

Guide: The thalamus is a pair of large, egg-shaped masses of grey matter, one on each side of the brain, sitting almost exactly in the centre and forming most of the walls of the fluid-filled space between them, the third ventricle, and in most brains the two halves are even joined across the midline by a small bridge of tissue. Think of it as the brain's central relay station. Nearly every sensory signal on its way to the cortex, everything except smell, checks in at the thalamus first, is sorted, and is forwarded to the right cortical address. Each sense has its own desk in this switchboard. Our desk is the lateral geniculate body, and it sits at the back and outer corner of the thalamus, precisely at the far end of the optic tract. Just above and behind it bulges a large thalamic swelling with a lovely name, the pulvinar, Latin for a cushion, and our small body nestles under that cushion. Below and in front lies the midbrain, and a thin stalk called the superior brachium, literally the upper arm, ties the lateral geniculate body to a roof structure of the midbrain called the superior colliculus. The medial geniculate has its matching arm to the inferior colliculus for sound. Lateral for light, medial for music; that contrast is worth carrying with you.

Host: So the pathway bends, and the relay sits in the bend. What actually happens to the optic tract fibres when they arrive? Do they simply stop there?

Guide: They end, and that ending is the whole point. The lateral geniculate body is the first place beyond the eye where the visual signal crosses a synapse. Every fibre you have followed since the retina, through nerve, chiasm and tract, belongs to a retinal ganglion cell, and here those cells finally hand the message over to a new population of neurons. The great majority of tract fibres, classically quoted at around ninety percent, terminate in this relay; the remainder peel off earlier to serve reflex duties, and their story, the pupil among them, waits for episode seven, Pupillary Pathway. Way back in Series 1, in an episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information, we drew the visual pathway as a chain of stations running from eye to brain. This is the station where the eye's message is handed to brand-new couriers, the geniculate relay cells, neurons with their own cell bodies and their own axons, and those axons grow into the next section of the pathway. A relay in the truest sense: message received, new courier dispatched.

Host: Before we open up the floors, give me the physical picture. What does this relay look like, and how do the tract fibres actually meet it?

Guide: It is small, about the size and shape of a coffee bean, an ovoid, slightly flattened body bulging out of the thalamus at the corner we just described. The optic tract fibres do not slam into it head-on; they wrap around its surface like a hand closing over a fist and then dive inward, entering through a hollowed notch on its underside, a dimple called the hilum, the same word used for the little dent where a bean's stalk once sat. From that entry point the fibres spread around and between the layers before they make contact. Now cut across it, and you meet the famous number. Six layers, thin sheets of nerve cells stacked one atop another. Anatomists call each sheet a lamina, Latin for a thin plate or leaf, and the layers are numbered one to six starting from below, the underside where the fibres enter, and counting upward. Layer one is innermost, closest to the incoming fibres, and layer six is outermost on top. In the direction words of anatomy, ventral means toward the base and dorsal means toward the top. One more detail and the picture is complete: the whole six-layered stack is bent, curved around the entering fibres like a boomerang or a folded cap, so on a cut section the six laminae look like wavy pages pressed together rather than flat pancakes.

Host: Six layers, each a sheet of cells. But why six? Why does the brain bother to slice one relay into six separate floors instead of running one solid lump?

Guide: Because the two eyes must be kept separate, floor by floor. Here is the rule, and it is one of the most quoted facts in this whole season. Each layer receives fibres from one eye and one eye only. Layers one, four and six belong to the eye on the opposite side of the body, the contralateral eye, and layers two, three and five belong to the eye on the same side, the ipsilateral eye. Contralateral and ipsilateral are the two words to carry home: contra means opposite, ipse means same. Remember what the chiasm did back in episode two, Optic Chiasm: it sent the crossed nasal fibres of each eye into the opposite tract, so every optic tract already carried a mixture of both eyes. Now, at the geniculate, the mixture is unscrambled. The two eyes' streams are sorted into alternating floors of a six-storey sandwich, like two sets of guests placed on alternate decks of a ferry, never sharing a deck. Students remember it as one, four, six cross; two, three, five stay. Every floor is strictly monocular, one eye's view and nothing else.

Host: Let me check that I have it with a concrete case. The left lateral geniculate body. Which eye puts its fibres on which floors?

Guide: Take it slowly and it falls into place. The left geniculate serves the right half of the visual world, because the chiasm already swapped the nasal halves over. Into it come two streams. From the right eye come the fibres of the nasal retina, the half of that retina toward the nose, which crossed at the chiasm; those crossed fibres, arriving from the opposite eye, occupy layers one, four and six. From the left eye come the fibres of the temporal retina, the half toward the temple, which never crossed and stayed on their own side; those same-side fibres occupy layers two, three and five. So in the left geniculate, right eye fibres sit on one, four and six, left eye fibres sit on two, three and five, and both sets watch the same right half of the world. Mirror everything for the right geniculate, where the left eye's crossed nasal fibres take one, four and six, and the right eye's uncrossed temporal fibres take two, three and five. The layer numbers never change; only the eye labels swap. And each layer, remember, still holds a complete picture of that same half-field, seen entirely through one eye.

Host: So inside this relay the two eyes never actually exchange a word. No neuron here sees both eyes at once?

Guide: Correct, and it is worth underlining, because it is one of the pathway's neatest design decisions. You can search the lateral geniculate in vain for a binocular neuron, a cell driven by both eyes together. Each geniculate cell answers to one eye, one layer, one place in the field. The first neurons that truly combine the two eyes' signals live further along, in the visual cortex, and that awakening is episode six's story, Visual Cortex. But the layers are not stacked in random order, and this is the beautiful part. They are in register. Corresponding points in the six layers lie along radial lines through the stack, so a needle pushed straight through all six floors samples the same point of visual space six times, once for each eye's version of it. The geniculate does not mix the two pictures; it files them side by side, point for point, ready for someone else to compare.

Host: Now the vocabulary that every textbook throws at this structure: magnocellular and parvocellular. Two heavy words. Take them apart for us.

Guide: They are Latin arithmetic. Magnocellular: magnus, large, plus cellularis, of cells, so large-celled, and it names layers one and two, whose neurons are conspicuously big. Parvocellular: parvus, small, so small-celled, and it names layers three, four, five and six, four layers of conspicuously small neurons. So the six decks divide into two and four: two magnocellular floors below, four parvocellular floors above, which is why some texts simply call them the ventral and dorsal layers. And there is a third family hiding between the decks. In the thin zones separating the main layers sit tiny, pale cells packed so finely that early anatomists called them koniocellular, from the Greek konis, dust, as if a dusting of fine grit had settled between the sheets. Those zones are called interlaminar, which simply means between the laminae. So when you hear the three names, think big cells, small cells, and dust: on the bottom two floors, the middle four, and in between.

Host: And I suspect each of these cell families is fed by a matching family back in the retina. Which retinal cells feed which floors?

Guide: Exactly, and we prepared for this back in episode one, Optic Nerve, when we noted that the axons streaming out of the retina are not all cut from one cloth: some thick, some slender, some numerous and fine. The match is precise. The magnocellular layers are fed by the large parasol ganglion cells, named for their wide, umbrella-like dendritic trees, and their axons are among the thickest and fastest in the nerve. The parvocellular layers are fed by the midget ganglion cells, tiny, extremely numerous, and crowding the macula more densely than anywhere else. The koniocellular sheets are fed by the bistratified ganglion cells, whose branches rest in two separate levels of the retina, hence bi-stratified, two strata. Three streams, then, travelling side by side: the magnocellular stream carries motion and coarse form, fast and contrast-hungry but blind to fine detail; the parvocellular stream carries fine detail and red-green colour; the koniocellular stream carries the blue-yellow signals. The relay does not scramble these characters; it keeps the three currents separate as they pass through.

Host: Next stop, the phrase from every diagram caption: the retinotopic map. What does retinotopic actually assert about this place?

Guide: It asserts that geography survives the journey. Retinotopic means the map of the retina is preserved in the target: neighbouring ganglion cells send their axons to neighbouring geniculate cells, so the layout of the visual world is reproduced, point for point, inside the relay. And not once but six times. Each of the six layers contains its own complete, orderly map of the opposite half of the visual field, one map per floor, six maps of the same territory, each drawn through a different eye. Better still, because the layers are in register, the six maps align with one another: the same point in space occupies the same radial position in every layer, like six transparencies of one street map stacked in a pile, each printed from a different eye but with every street in the same place. Six separate maps, then, but one shared coordinate system, and that shared system is precisely what the later stages of the pathway will exploit. Neighbours in the world stay neighbours in the brain, from retina through geniculate, all the way to the cortex.

Host: Is that six-fold map drawn to scale? Does every patch of the visual field get equal space on the floor?

Guide: Not remotely, and the distortion is the message. The centre of the field is enormously overweighted. The macula, the bull's-eye of the retina that we explored in Series 1 in an episode called The Macula and Central Vision, claims a wildly disproportionate share of the geniculate's cells. Within each lamina, the macular territory sits toward the posterior end of the layer, the back end, while the ever more peripheral parts of the field occupy the anterior end, toward the front pole. The reason lives back in the retina itself: in season eight, episode four, Photoreceptor Distribution, we watched cone density collapse as you move away from the fovea, so the centre simply has far more fibres to send. The geniculate inherits that bias and preserves it, and the cortex then carries it much further; the cortex's own exaggeration, cortical magnification, belongs to episode six, Visual Cortex. But the principle starts here: central vision is given stage space out of all proportion to its area. A bull's-eye of retina becomes a wide boulevard of cells.

Host: One more orientation question, and then I promise we will make this clinical. Inside each layer, which way is up, which way is down, and which way is the ear?

Guide: Within each lamina the map runs like this. The upper retinal quadrants, which watch the lower half of the visual field, map onto the medial side of the layer, the side toward the midline of the brain. The lower retinal quadrants, which watch the upper field, map onto the lateral side, toward the ear. And we have already placed the macula posteriorly and the periphery anteriorly. So every layer is a folded map with the same compass: back for centre, front for edge, middle for the lower world, outside for the upper world. Hold onto the lateral versus medial split, because it pays off immediately in the output. The fibres leaving the lateral part of the geniculate swing far forward, into the temporal lobe, as a loop named after Meyer; the fibres from the medial part run back more directly, through parietal territory. The compass inside the geniculate predicts the geography of everything that comes next, and that geography is the whole of the next episode.

Host: You mentioned blood supply a moment ago, and I have been waiting for it, because anatomy this deep only earns its keep if it explains what we see in patients. Who feeds the geniculate?

Guide: Two arterial families split the relay roughly into the same halves as the map. The anterior choroidal artery, a slender branch of the internal carotid artery, supplies the lateral portion of the geniculate. The lateral posterior choroidal artery, a branch of the posterior cerebral artery, supplies the medial portion. Now place that against the map we just drew and notice the accident of geography: the macular territory, sitting posteriorly, lies near the border zone between the two supplies, the region anatomists call the watershed, where two irrigation systems meet and overlap. Both arteries are small, deep vessels, which is why geniculate infarcts are rare but memorable when they do happen. That double plumbing is protective. If one of the two arteries fails, the macular area often survives on the other line, and the patient keeps a working island of central vision inside an otherwise affected field. Central vision sparing after a deep lesion is not a mystery; it is the blood supply diagram speaking.

Host: Which brings the natural question. If a stroke or a tumour takes out the lateral geniculate body, what does the optometrist actually see in the visual field?

Guide: You see a homonymous hemianopia, meaning loss of the corresponding half-field in both eyes, the same side of the world gone for each eye, because each geniculate carries that half-field for both eyes at once. But two signatures hint that the lesion sits at the geniculate rather than further forward or further back. First, the defect is typically incongruous: the two eyes' blind areas do not match shape for shape, because the two eyes' maps sit in separate layers, and no single lesion strikes both identically. Second, the loss often has a wedge shape, a sector like a slice of pie rather than a clean vertical half. And the pupils behave perfectly normally, because the fibres serving the light reflex peeled away from the tract before it ever reached the relay; we build that reflex arc, pretectal nucleus and all, in episode seven, Pupillary Pathway. So the clinical fingerprint of the geniculate is an incongruous, wedge-shaped hemianopia with quiet, reactive pupils.

Host: Time to follow the outgoing mail. The relay cells have done their sorting; what actually leaves the geniculate, and by what road?

Guide: The relay cells send their axons bursting out of the lateral surface of the body, and that fan of new fibres is the next great bundle of the pathway, the optic radiations, sometimes called the geniculocalcarine fibres because they run from the geniculate to the calcarine cortex, the visual area at the back of the brain that lines a shelf called the calcarine sulcus. To leave the thalamus the fan squeezes through the retrolenticular part of the internal capsule. The internal capsule is the great funnel of white matter through which almost every fibre entering or leaving the cortex must pass, and retrolenticular means behind the lenticular nucleus, the lens-shaped mass of grey matter that the funnel skirts around. The radiations get their own full tour next time, which lands as episode ninety-five in the grand count of this hundred-episode series: the sweep into temporal and parietal lobes, the detour of Meyer's loop, and the arrival at the cortex. Today we stop at the exit gate, and we let the map we built, lateral fibres forward, medial fibres back, do the pointing.

Host: Here is a question that has been forming in my mind since we started. Is the geniculate anything more than a junction box? A place where wires change colour?

Guide: That assumption collapsed when anatomists counted the synapses. On a single geniculate relay cell, the incoming retinal fibre's terminals account for only about five to ten percent of the synapses actually landing on the cell. Who owns the rest? Two conversations. The cortex itself sends fibres back down to the geniculate, the corticogeniculate projection, and by most counts those returning fibres outnumber the ones we sent up. The brainstem adds its own inputs, tied to alertness and drowsiness, that change how eagerly the relay cells pass the mail along. So the picture reverses: at this station the retina is a whispering minority, and the geniculate is a gate, tuned moment by moment by attention and state, deciding how much of the visual world is worth forwarding. It is not a junction box; it is a filter with an opinion.

Host: Then let me ask the design question underneath all of it. Why does the brain keep the two eyes in separate layers at all? It could have mixed them from the very start.

Guide: Two reasons, one practical and one developmental. Practically, keeping each eye's version of the world intact and in register means the cortex can later compare the two pictures point for point, cell for cell, and extract the small differences between them that we experience as depth. Stereopsis, the sense of three dimensions, is born from exactly this kind of comparison, and we traced its perceptual side in Series 1 in the episode Depth Perception. The geniculate's register is the hardware that makes that comparison possible. Developmentally, the sorting is an achievement, not an accident. Axons arrive from the two eyes in overlapping waves and actively sort themselves by eye and by place, nudged along by molecular labels on their surfaces, the way luggage finds the right carousel. Give the system six labelled floors, and the sorting can complete cleanly. The same logic protects the three streams, magno, parvo and konio, so that motion, detail and colour arrive upstairs still distinguishable from one another.

Host: Walk me through the whole station once more, in one pass, so I can see all of it at once.

Guide: Here is the geniculate in one breath. It is the visual relay of the thalamus, a coffee-bean-sized body at the back and outer corner, tucked under the pulvinar, sitting in the bend that gives it its name, the little knee. Nearly all optic tract fibres end here, and this is the first synapse beyond the eye; new relay cells take the message onward. Six laminae, numbered one to six from below: two magnocellular floors fed by parasol cells, four parvocellular floors fed by midget cells, and sheets of koniocellular dust between them, fed by bistratified cells. Layers one, four and six belong to the opposite eye, layers two, three and five to the same eye, so every floor is monocular, yet all six stand in register, aligned point for point. Each layer holds its map of the opposite half-field: macula posterior and enlarged, periphery anterior, lower field medial, upper field lateral. The anterior choroidal artery feeds the lateral half, the lateral posterior choroidal artery the medial half, and their watershed shelters the macula. Lesions give incongruous, wedge-shaped hemianopias with normal pupils. And the output is the fan of relay axons through the retrolenticular internal capsule, becoming the optic radiations.

Host: That is a station worth the visit. Thank you. Before you go, point down the track for us. What waits at the next stop?

Guide: Next time we follow the fibres that today's relay cells have just grown, in an episode called Optic Radiations. We will trace the great fan as it spreads through the white matter of the hemisphere: part of it diving forward into the temporal lobe as Meyer's loop, carrying the upper field, and part of it running back through parietal territory, carrying the lower field. We will see why a temporal-lobe injury and a parietal-lobe injury carve opposite quadrants out of a patient's vision, and how the retinotopic map we assembled today survives the whole journey intact, all the way to the calcarine cortex.
