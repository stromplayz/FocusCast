# Description: At the base of the brain, the two optic nerves meet and deliberately swap half their fibres. In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we map the optic chiasm: why nasal retinal fibres cross while temporal fibres stay, where the macular fibres run through the central and posterior core, and the crucial neighbours above and below, from the third ventricle and the circle of Willis down to the diaphragma sellae and the pituitary gland. Pure anatomy and wiring for optometrists, with dimensions, blood supply, and the positional variants that change the geometry. Lesion patterns wait for a future clinical series; today is the crossroads itself.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Series 2, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, and we are deep in Season 10, Neuroanatomy for Optometrists, the season that walks the visual pathway station by station, from the retina all the way to the cortex. Last episode we started at the true beginning, the optic nerve. Today we reach the first great landmark beyond the eye, the place where the two nerves meet, shake hands, and deliberately trade half their cargo. It is called the optic chiasm, and it is one of the most elegant pieces of wiring in the entire body. To guide us into the crossroads, welcome back our anatomist. Where exactly have we landed?

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back. Today we stand at the junction box of the visual pathway. Here is the plan, so you can hold the map while we walk. First, where the chiasm sits and what it touches, including the neighbours above and the neighbours below. Second, its size and shape. Third, and this is the heart of the episode, the crossing map: which fibres cross, which stay, and where the macular fibres run. Fourth, its blood supply. And fifth, why an optometrist needs this geography at all. One promise before we begin. This season is anatomy and wiring only. The story of what happens when the chiasm is compressed, and what a patient sees or stops seeing, belongs to a future clinical series. Today, only structure and wiring.

Host: Let us set the scene for the ear first. I picture the chiasm sitting under the brain, roughly in the middle of the head, a little behind and above the eyes. Is that the right neighbourhood, and what is it actually resting on?

Guide: That is the right neighbourhood. The chiasm lies at the base of the brain, at the junction where the floor of the third ventricle meets its front wall, and it sits in the fluid filled suprasellar cistern, the space just above the pituitary fossa. Now comes a pearl that surprises students. The sphenoid bone below has a little ledge between the two optic canals called the chiasmatic groove, and you would expect the chiasm to rest in its namesake groove. It usually does not. In most people the chiasm lies above and behind that groove, hanging above the diaphragma sellae, a small dural shelf that roofs the pituitary fossa and has a central opening for the pituitary stalk. Between the underside of the chiasm and that shelf there is a variable gap, commonly a few millimetres and up to about ten, filled with cerebrospinal fluid. So the chiasm hangs like a suspended bridge above the gland, bathed in fluid, tethered at its four corners.

Host: A structure named for a groove it does not usually occupy. I love that. But you said in most people, which implies the address can shift. Where else can the chiasm sit?

Guide: Anatomists describe three positions, and the differences matter because they change which neighbour is closest. In roughly four out of five people the chiasm sits centrally, directly above the pituitary gland. In about ten percent of people it sits forward, over the tuberculum sellae, the small ridge at the front edge of the fossa, and that is called a prefixed chiasm. In a similar proportion it sits back, over the dorsum sellae, the plate of bone at the rear of the fossa, and that is a postfixed chiasm. Notice what each shift does to the geometry. A prefixed chiasm lies farther from the gland and closer to the front structures. A postfixed chiasm drifts back toward the stalk. The wiring never changes; only the address does. And later in your career, when you read a scan or a surgical report, that address matters enormously. Today we are laying the anatomical groundwork for exactly those moments.

Host: Before we climb or descend, give me the physical object itself. How big is this thing, and what shape am I picturing?

Guide: Picture a flattened, slightly tilted ribbon of nerve tissue. Standard textbook numbers: about twelve millimetres across, eight millimetres from front to back, and roughly four millimetres thick, so it is much wider than it is deep. The classic shape is a lopsided letter X, but the X is only the silhouette. At each anterolateral corner an optic nerve arrives, coming in from above and the side, angling down and inward. At each posterolateral corner an optic tract leaves, running back and outward around the midbrain. The crossing itself happens in the central body between those four corners. So the chiasm is not two whole cables crossing like crossed swords. It is a flattened exchange where only a selected half of each cable detaches, crosses the midline, and joins the other side. Keep that image, because this whole episode is about which half detaches, and where each part goes.

Host: Let me reset the cargo before we open the exchange. Last episode followed the fibres out of the eye. Remind us what arrives at those anterolateral corners, and how much of it there is.

Guide: Each corner receives one optic nerve carrying the axons of retinal ganglion cells, the output neurons of the retina. Back in Season 10, episode one, Optic Nerve, we counted the cargo: about one million two hundred thousand axons in each nerve, and we noted that those axons differ in calibre and destination. We also split each nerve into four segments back in Season 8, episode nine, Optic Nerve Structure: the intraocular segment at the disc, the long intraorbital segment, the squeezed intracanalicular segment inside the optic canal, which we first mapped in Season 1, episode four, Orbital Openings and Communications, and finally the intracranial segment. That last segment concerns us now, because it runs medially and slightly downward, so the two nerves converge toward the midline above the pituitary. They meet the chiasm at a shallow angle, and the exchange begins almost immediately. Every number we use today follows from that arrival.

Host: Now the question the whole episode turns on. Why do any fibres cross at all? Each eye has its own nerve. Why not simply run both nerves straight back to the brain and keep everything separate?

Guide: Because the brain does not want two eye cables; it wants two world halves. Remember from Series 1 how the optics invert the image. Light from the temporal side of the visual field, the outer half toward the ear, falls on the nasal half of the retina, the half toward the nose. Light from the nasal field falls on the temporal retina. So each retina is a map of the opposite half of the world. Now, the cortex behind us is organised so that one hemisphere handles one half of the visual scene. To make that possible, fibres from the nasal retina, which already carry the temporal field, must cross to the other side and join the temporal fibres of the opposite eye, which carry that eye's nasal field. The swap stitches the two matching halves together. Anatomists call this partial decussation, and when about half the fibres cross, hemidecussation. We followed the cable as far as the brain's door in a Series 1 episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information; today we finally step inside the junction box. And the result is worth saying slowly. The right optic tract carries the entire left half of the visual world, gathered from the right eye's temporal retina and the left eye's crossed nasal retina.

Host: So the dividing line between fibres that cross and fibres that stay is the vertical midline of the visual field. How sharp is that split in the wiring, and what are the actual numbers?

Guide: Remarkably sharp. The dividing line runs vertically through the fovea, and fibres nasal to that line, meaning fibres from the nasal half of the retina, cross, while fibres temporal to it stay. Now the numbers. In each optic nerve about fifty three percent of the ganglion cell axons come from nasal retina and cross, while about forty seven percent come from temporal retina and stay. Of one million two hundred thousand axons, that means a little over six hundred thousand fibres decussate from each eye. The two eyes contribute matching shares, so after the exchange each tract contains almost exactly equal cargo from the two eyes. One more point, and it matters: the split respects the macula too. The vertical line through the fovea divides the macula itself, so each macula sends half its fibres across and half straight back. Nothing about the exchange is approximate at the midline. The retina's own geography decides, fibre by fibre.

Host: Let us open the box and look at the actual crossing. Is the chiasm a tangled knot inside, or is there an orderly map, the way the retina itself is so beautifully organised?

Guide: There is order, looser than in the nerve but real, and anatomists call it retinotopy: fibres that were neighbours in the retina stay roughly neighbouring through the pathway. Three rules give you the whole map. Rule one, the fibres that do not cross, the temporal fibres, keep to the sides. They run through the lateral portions of the chiasm straight into the same side tract, fibres from the inferior temporal retina sitting lower and more outward, fibres from the superior temporal retina sitting higher. Rule two, the crossing nasal fibres respect up and down. Fibres from the superior nasal retina cross high and toward the back of the chiasm. Fibres from the inferior nasal retina cross low and toward the front. Rule three follows from the first two: the crossing is oblique, each fibre sliding diagonally across the midline from one nerve's territory into the opposite tract's territory. So inside that small twelve millimetre ribbon there is a quiet choreography. Up stays up and down stays down, while left and right trade places.

Host: You mentioned the inferior nasal fibres crossing low and forward. That is where I have heard of a famous loop, something called Wilbrand's knee. What is actually happening there?

Guide: Wilbrand's knee is the classic description of how far forward the inferior nasal fibres travel. In the traditional account, fibres from the inferonasal retina cross and then loop a few millimetres forward into the root of the opposite optic nerve before turning back to run into the opposite tract, like a knee bending in the wrong direction first. Now, intellectual honesty requires the caveat. Modern imaging and re-examination of fixed brains suggest that the knee as drawn in the old atlases may be an artefact of tissue preparation, a distortion created when brains are dehydrated for sectioning, and that it does not form in living tissue. The older diagrams remain in most textbooks, and the loop is still the standard teaching answer, so you should know the name and the claim. The safe anatomical statement is this: inferior nasal fibres cross low and anteriorly, and whether they truly loop forward into the opposite nerve by a few millimetres remains disputed.

Host: Now the part of the map I most wanted to hear about, the macular fibres. The macula is a tiny patch of retina, yet everything about it seems oversized in the pathway. Where do its fibres run in the chiasm?

Guide: Oversized is exactly the right word. Back in Season 8, episode ten, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, we saw how macular fibres travel as the papillomacular bundle, the straightest route from the macula to the disc, flanked by the arcing arcuate bundles. Those papillomacular fibres keep their priority through the chiasm. They occupy its central and posterior portions. The macular fibres that need to cross, those from the nasal half of the macula, decussate through that central posterior region, while the temporal half of the macula passes laterally without crossing. Think about the proportions. The macula is a patch of a few millimetres at the centre of the retina, yet its fibres claim a wholly disproportionate share of the chiasm's real estate, because ganglion cell density at the posterior pole, which we marvelled at back in Season 7, episode eight, Macula, is the highest anywhere in the retina. So the central posterior core of the chiasm is, in effect, dedicated highway for central vision. The peripheral fibres take the more anterior and lateral routes; the macula owns the deep centre.

Host: So after the swap, does each fibre still know its address? And while we are on wiring, are all the fibres headed to the same destination, or do some peel off along the way?

Guide: Both questions have clean anatomical answers. First, order survives. Retinotopy is loosened inside the chiasm, but each tract still carries an organised map, and it stays organised all the way to the lateral geniculate body, which is where episode four will take us. Second, the main cargo is unified. After the exchange, each optic tract is one complete hemifield cable, as we said: the right tract carrying the left visual field from both eyes. Episode three, Optic Tract, follows that cable around the midbrain. Third, a few small streams do peel off, and I will name them without unpacking them. A thin bundle of retinal fibres reaches the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a small cluster of the hypothalamus sitting just above the chiasm; it is the light sensor that synchronises the body's daily clock. And the fibres serving the pupillary light reflex ride with the pathway and leave it before the lateral geniculate body. Their full arc is the story of episode seven, Pupillary Pathway, so we will park it there.

Host: Now the relations, which is where this structure earns its reputation. Start above the chiasm. What is directly over its upper surface?

Guide: Above, the chiasm belongs to the brain's plumbing and wiring. Its upper surface helps form the floor of the third ventricle. Just behind it rises the lamina terminalis, a thin sheet that seals the front of the third ventricle, and between the chiasm and that sheet is a small pocket of the ventricle called the suprachiasmatic recess. In front and above sweep the two anterior cerebral arteries, each passing backward over the optic nerve and the top of the chiasm, joined to each other by a short bridge called the anterior communicating artery. Those vessels form the front of the circle of Willis, the arterial ring at the base of the brain that links the two internal carotid systems with the system from the back of the brain. So the chiasm sits inside the front curve of that ring, with major arteries draped across its upper surface. That is the neighbourhood above: ventricle behind and above, great arteries arcing over the top.

Host: And below? You have hinted that the pituitary gland is the closest neighbour underneath. Take me down there layer by layer.

Guide: Going down from the chiasm's under surface, first comes cerebrospinal fluid, the variable gap we measured earlier, then the diaphragma sellae, the dural shelf with its central opening, then the pituitary gland itself sitting in the sella turcica, the saddle of the sphenoid bone, and below the bone, the air of the sphenoid sinus. Sella turcica means Turkish saddle, a name from older anatomy, and the gland sits in the seat. Directly behind the chiasm the pituitary stalk, the infundibulum, descends through that opening in the diaphragma toward the gland. Now recall the three chiasm positions and you will see why we bothered with them. With a central chiasm the gland lies almost directly beneath. With a prefixed chiasm the gland lies behind and below it. With a postfixed chiasm the chiasm floats farther back over the stalk. The whole vertical stack, chiasm, fluid, shelf, gland and sinus, is only a few centimetres tall, and every layer is packed tight.

Host: What about the sides and the area behind? You mentioned the internal carotid arteries earlier. Exactly how close do those come to the crossing itself?

Guide: Intimately close. On each side, at the anterolateral angle where the optic nerve arrives, the internal carotid artery ascends from the cavernous sinus, the large venous channel tucked against the body of the sphenoid, and it grooves the lateral angle of the chiasm, sometimes visibly indenting it. So the great artery of the eye and brain presses directly against the crossing. Farther below and lateral lie the cavernous sinuses themselves. Behind the chiasm the territory belongs to the hypothalamus: the tuber cinereum, the thin grey band around the base of the pituitary stalk, with the paired mammillary bodies, two small round prominences, just behind it. Notice the theme of this tour. Above, arteries. Below, gland and bone. Each side, artery and venous lake. Behind, stalk and hypothalamus. The chiasm has almost no free space of its own. Its margins are other people's territory.

Host: That tight packing is presumably why this structure matters so much in practice. For the optometrist listening, what is the payoff of knowing these neighbours so precisely, without getting into the disease patterns themselves?

Guide: The payoff is explanation. Structures this close eventually press on each other. A tumour of the pituitary gland growing upward meets the chiasm's under surface first. An aneurysm of the internal carotid artery bulges toward its lateral angle. Fluid and space are scarce here, so displacement, not distance, is the rule. When a patient arrives in your chair with unexplained visual complaints and a story that does not fit the refraction, the anatomy you learned today is the reason the optometrist is often the first professional to suspect something deep is involved, and the reason your referral letter can name the neighbourhood precisely. What we are deliberately not doing today is mapping what happens when each fibre family is damaged, the classic field patterns. That story, which fibres fail and what the patient stops seeing, belongs to a future clinical series. Today the wiring is the point. Know the wiring, and every later clinical story will feel inevitable rather than memorised.

Host: One more system to visit before we close the box: blood supply. The chiasm is living nervous tissue with a working metabolism. Who feeds it?

Guide: Two arterial floors, matching the relations we just walked. The upper surface and upper body of the chiasm are supplied by branches of the anterior cerebral arteries, reinforced from above by the anterior communicating artery. The lower surface is supplied by branches of the internal carotid arteries and the posterior communicating arteries, with a dedicated contribution from the superior hypophyseal arteries, small branches of the internal carotid that also feed the stalk and its surroundings. All of these reach the chiasm through a fine pial plexus, a net of tiny vessels on its surface, and from there small perforators dive inward. One classic observation belongs in your notes: the central zone of the chiasm, where the crossing macular fibres run, is served most sparsely, by the smallest and longest branches. And for contrast, remember that the retina inside the eye has its own separate supply story, the central retinal artery and the choroid, which we mapped back in Season 8, episode one, Retinal Blood Supply. Two different addresses, two different supply systems.

Host: You said the chiasm hangs in fluid, tethered at its corners. Say a little more about that. What covers it, and how does it sit in the fluid spaces of the skull base?

Guide: The chiasm is invested by the same soft coverings as the rest of the pathway. Pia mater hugs its surface, arachnoid mater wraps outside that, and the nerve sheaths arriving from each optic nerve blend into this covering, so the subarachnoid space that travelled with each nerve opens out around the chiasm and continues along the tracts. That space here is called the chiasmatic cistern, part of the larger suprasellar cistern. In practical terms the chiasm floats, suspended in fluid, anchored at four corners by the two nerves in front and the two tracts behind, and nearly free on its upper and lower surfaces. That floating arrangement is why masses from below can lift it and stretch it without tearing it, and why the exact pattern of displacement depends so much on the position variants we covered earlier. Anatomy is geometry under pressure, and nowhere more literally than here.

Host: One question I cannot resist before we recap. Is this half-crossing arrangement uniquely human? What do other animals do at the same crossroads?

Guide: The half-crossing is the signature of forward facing eyes. Our two eyes share a large overlap of visual field, and the crossing fraction is tuned to that overlap: we cross a little more than half, and the two halves of the world reunite in the opposite hemisphere. In animals whose eyes are set on the sides of the head, like rabbits and many hoofed animals, the fields barely overlap, and almost every fibre crosses, so each hemisphere serves the opposite side of the animal's world almost exclusively. As eyes migrated forward across evolution and the overlap grew, the crossed fraction shrank toward half. That shared map is what makes fusion and depth from the two images possible at all, the ability we celebrated back in a Series 1 episode called Depth Perception. So the chiasm is not an oddity. It is the hinge that turns two eye cables into two world halves, and only wiring built this way lets a brain compare the two views point by point.

Host: Give me the pocket summary, then. If a listener keeps only one card from this episode in their mental drawer, what should the card say? Walk us through the whole structure one last time, numbers and neighbours together.

Guide: Here is the card. The optic chiasm is a flattened ribbon at the base of the brain, about twelve millimetres wide, eight millimetres deep and four millimetres thick, hanging in the suprasellar cistern above the diaphragma sellae and the pituitary gland, usually centrally placed, but prefixed in about ten percent of people and postfixed in a similar proportion. It receives one optic nerve at each anterolateral corner and sends one optic tract from each posterolateral corner. Inside, about fifty three percent of the fibres, those from nasal retina, cross, and forty seven percent, from temporal retina, stay, divided by a vertical line through the fovea. Temporal fibres keep the lateral flanks. Superior nasal fibres cross high and posteriorly. Inferior nasal fibres cross low and anteriorly, with the disputed forward loop called Wilbrand's knee. Macular fibres own the central and posterior core. Above: third ventricle, lamina terminalis, and the anterior cerebral arteries. Below: fluid, diaphragma, gland, bone, sinus. Each side: the internal carotid artery. Behind: the stalk and the hypothalamus. Supplied above by the anterior cerebral circulation and below by the internal carotid and its neighbours. That is the whole structure in one breath.
