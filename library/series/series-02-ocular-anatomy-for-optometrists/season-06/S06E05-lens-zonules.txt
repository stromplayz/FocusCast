# Description: The lens floats in the middle of the eye, yet it never falls, because a ring of astonishingly fine fibres called the zonule of Zinn holds it centred behind the pupil. In this episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks follows the zonular fibres from their birth on the ciliary epithelium to their anchorage in the lens capsule, and names the three classic fibre groups: anterior, posterior and equatorial. Expect real numbers, real eponyms, and the clinical anatomy of zonular weakness, from Marfan syndrome to zonular dialysis. This is part five of ten in our season on lens and accommodation anatomy, made for optometry students and the whole eye care team.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library where we take one topic at a time and give it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series together, and we are in the middle of season six, which is all about the anatomy of the lens and the machinery behind accommodation. Last time, in episode four, Lens Fibres, we filled the lens itself, tile by tile, with those hexagonal, life-long cells laid down in shells. Today we step outside the lens and meet the straps that hold the whole organ in place, a ring of threads so fine that most of us never see them directly, yet the entire focusing system of the eye depends on them. They are called the zonules, and they deserve a whole episode. Here to walk me through them is our guide, a patient teacher of anatomy. Welcome back.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to every listener. Let me set the map before we start walking. In episode one, Lens Location and Relationships, we placed the lens just behind the iris and said one sentence that we have been unpacking ever since: the lens is suspended by a ring of fibres. Episode two gave us the capsule that those fibres grip. Episode three gave us the epithelium that keeps the lens alive. And last episode gave us the fibres inside the lens. Today is the suspension system itself, the zonule of Zinn. I am going to hold us strictly to three questions. What are the zonular fibres made of. Where exactly do they begin in the eye, and where exactly do they end. And how do they group themselves between those two points. What happens when the ciliary muscle pulls on them is episode six, and the assembled machine of accommodation is episode nine. Today, only the straps.

Host: Good, because I already want to hold you to that. Then let us name the thing first. The word is zonule, and it also comes with a person's name attached, Zinn. Say what the word means, describe the structure in plain words, and tell me who Zinn was.

Guide: The zonule is a ring of very fine strands that runs all the way around the eye in the gap between the ciliary body and the equator of the lens, the equator being the lens's widest rim, its middle circumference. Ring shaped is the key image. Anatomists call it an annular structure, from the Latin word for ring, the same word that gives us annular eclipse. Every strand in the ring is a zonular fibre, and the whole ring works as one suspensory ligament, which is why you will also hear it called the suspensory ligament of the lens. The full name, the zonule of Zinn, honours Johann Gottfried Zinn, an eighteenth century German anatomist who described it with real care, and whose surname also survives in another structure near the optic nerve. In the living eye the ring hides behind the iris, bathed in the aqueous humour, the clear fluid that fills the posterior chamber, the small space behind the iris that we mapped in Season 5, episode two, Posterior Chamber Anatomy. With the pupil widely dilated and a bright, tilted beam from the slit lamp, you can occasionally catch the gleam of the outermost fibres in the far periphery. Mostly, in practice, you see their consequences rather than the fibres themselves.

Host: You said suspensory ligament, and that word ligament is exactly where I want to pull. The ligaments in a knee are tough cords of collagen. Is the zonule built from the same material?

Guide: That is the first surprise, and it matters. Most ligaments in the body are bundles of collagen, a stiff, rope-like structural protein. The zonule is not. It is built from microfibrils, which are threads of protein so fine that the light microscope cannot resolve them clearly, and the defining ingredient of those microfibrils is a glycoprotein called fibrillin. Let me unpack that word. A glycoprotein is simply a protein that carries sugar groups on its surface, and fibrillin is the protein that gives the zonule its identity, its strength and its behaviour. Each microfibril is about ten nanometres across, and each strand you would call a zonular fibre is a bundle of hundreds of these microfibrils bound into a cable by other glycoproteins around them. So the zonule is protein machinery of a different family from collagen, with a different feel and a different job. The old embryology textbooks had a lovely name for this region, the tertiary vitreous, meaning the third layer of the vitreous story, because the zonule develops in continuity with the front of the vitreous. Modern writing prefers to call it simply the zonule, or the zonular apparatus, and treats it as its own structure with its own signature protein.

Host: A nanometre. Talk me down to that scale, because I want to feel how delicate this suspension really is. How thin is thin, and what do the fibres feel like when they are tested?

Guide: A nanometre is a billionth of a metre. A sheet of paper is about a tenth of a millimetre thick, so a microfibril of ten nanometres is roughly ten thousand times thinner than that page, and a single zonular strand, one bundle of those microfibrils, is only a few micrometres wide, a micrometre being a thousandth of a millimetre. Now here is the second surprise, and it is the property that makes the whole system work. The zonule is elastic. Experimental stretching of human zonular fibres shows that they elongate under load and spring back when the load is released, like a fine elastic cord rather than a steel wire. So the picture to carry is not a rigid cable clamping a lens in a vice. It is a ring of soft springs, strong enough to carry the lens for eighty years and springy enough to hand back every pull it receives. That springiness becomes a main character when we reach the accommodation episodes, so file it away now.

Host: I love that image. Now take me to the starting line. You said the fibres begin on the ciliary epithelium. Where is that exactly, and which layer are we talking about?

Guide: We need the ciliary body from last season. In Season 5, episode seven, Ciliary Body, we mapped it as a ring of tissue just behind the root of the iris, with two parts. The pars plicata sits in front, and its inner surface carries about seventy plump, finger-like ciliary processes, the folds that make the aqueous humour. Behind them lies the pars plana, a smooth ring roughly four millimetres wide that runs back to the ora serrata, the saw-toothed front edge of the sensory retina. The entire inner surface of that ring is lined by two layers of epithelial cells sitting nose to nose, an outer pigmented layer and an inner non-pigmented layer, and the non-pigmented layer is the one that faces the posterior chamber, facing our zonule. Now recall episode two. Every epithelial sheet in the body rests on a basement membrane, a thin sheet of protein that anchors and supports it, and the lens capsule we studied there is itself a basement membrane, the thickest in the body. On the ciliary side, it is the basement membrane of the non-pigmented epithelium that serves as the birthplace of the zonular fibres. So the phrase I will keep repeating today, from the ciliary epithelium to the lens, is meant almost literally. The fibres grow out of that epithelial surface like fine roots from a wall.

Host: But I remember from my own student days that they do not start from every square millimetre of that surface. There was something about the pars plana, and something about valleys. Give me the two starting zones.

Guide: Yes, this is one of those details that anatomy examiners love, and it is cleanly taught. The zonular fibres take origin from two zones. The first is a band across the pars plana, about one and a half millimetres wide, where the fibres rise from the epithelial surface between the ora serrata and the processes. The second is the valleys between the ciliary processes, the grooves that separate one process from its neighbours. Notice what is missing from that list: the crests. The summits of the ciliary processes are left free of zonular origin, which is why those little folds can push backward into the zonular ring without being tethered at their tips. Why the fibres are arranged to skirt the processes in exactly this way, and what that geometry does for the flow of aqueous and for tension, is the opening story of episode six, Ciliary Zonule Relationships. And keep one link in your pocket from last season, episode eight, Ciliary Muscle. The muscle sits inside the substance of the ciliary body, and the fibres we are describing today are its remote handhold on the lens.

Host: And the far end of the story. After that long walk from the ciliary surface, where do the strands finally grip the lens?

Guide: On the lens capsule, in a band that wraps around the equator. If you take the lens equator, which we measured in episode one as a disc close to ten millimetres across in the adult eye, the zonular fibres land on the capsule over a zone reaching from about one millimetre in front of the equator to about one and a quarter millimetres behind it. They do not simply rest on the surface. They dive into the outer lamella of the capsule, the outermost sheet of that thick basement membrane, and blend with it, so the junction can resist decades of continuous pull without peeling. Picture a belt around the middle of a lens-shaped drum, with most strands sweeping forward to a line slightly in front of the belt and a second set angling backward to a line just behind it. That double-sided anchoring, in front of the equator and behind it, is exactly the setup we need for the next question.

Host: Near and far, in front and behind. So let us finally sort them into their groups. How do anatomists classify the zonular fibres?

Guide: Into three classic groups, and almost every textbook draws the same three. There are the anterior zonular fibres, the big set that runs forward to the capsule in front of the equator. There are the posterior zonular fibres, the backward set that anchors behind the equator. And there are the equatorial zonular fibres, the shorter strands that address the equator itself. Between them they cover the whole ring, and where their territories meet they talk to each other, which will matter in a moment. Let me walk each group from birth to anchor, because each path is worth knowing on its own.

Host: Take the anterior group first. Walk me along one of those fibres, from its birth on the ciliary epithelium to its grip on the capsule.

Guide: The anterior zonular fibres are the main mass of the zonule, the long haulers of the system. A fibre of this group rises from the pars plana band or from a process valley, sweeps forward through the posterior chamber, and runs gently inward, converging toward the lens. Near the iris root it passes just behind the posterior surface of the iris, hidden in the angle of the posterior chamber, and then it lands on the anterior capsule about one millimetre in front of the equator, fanning out into the outer lamella. The fibres of this group travel in the company of their neighbours, in gentle radial bundles that give the anterior zonule a sheet-like appearance. In a meridional section, a slice straight through the ring, like cutting a wheel from rim to hub, the anterior group reads as a long, thin, forward-tilting sheet. It is the longest journey in the zonule, and it is worth savouring: the anterior strands have the furthest to run and the most graceful curve, from a quiet starting band all the way to a firm grip point just in front of the lens equator.

Host: And the backward set. What is different about the posterior zonular fibres, in their path and in their neighbourhood?

Guide: They start from the same two zones, the pars plana band and the valleys, but they dive backward instead of forward, and they land on the posterior capsule about one and a quarter millimetres behind the equator. They are, as a rule, shorter and thicker than the anterior strands, because they do not have as far to travel, and their course is more direct. Their neighbourhood is the interesting part. These fibres run just in front of the front face of the vitreous, the transparent gel that fills the main cavity of the eye, so the posterior zonule and the anterior hyaloid, as that gel surface is called, are close travelling companions separated by the thinnest film of fluid. The posterior fibres therefore form a shorter, steeper sheet, a kind of back guy-line for the lens, and in section they complete the triangle that the anterior sheet begins. Front sheet, back sheet, and a woven belt at the equator between them: that is the skeleton of the whole zonule.

Host: Which leaves the third group, the ones whose name points at the equator itself. Are the equatorial fibres simply the shortest version of the same idea?

Guide: Almost, with two qualifications. The equatorial zonular fibres arise from the valleys between adjacent ciliary processes and run the shortest possible course to the lens equator itself, rather than to the capsule in front of it or behind it. First qualification: they are thinner and less constant than the other two groups, and honest textbooks describe them as variable. Second qualification: near the equator, fibres from neighbouring valleys cross one another, strand over strand, and this weaving produces a net-like layer that the texts call the zonular plexus. So the equatorial region of the zonule is not a tidy bundle of parallel wires but a woven collar. I am going to resist tracing every crossing today, because the full three dimensional geometry of how the fibres span the processes and the valleys between them is exactly the ground we cover in episode six. For today, own the three names, anterior, posterior and equatorial, and the image of a woven collar at the equator.

Host: All of this is suspended in fluid, in the posterior chamber and behind it. Do the spaces around the zonule carry names of their own?

Guide: They do, and you should recognise two of them. The annular space that the zonular fibres themselves occupy, the ring-shaped compartment between the anterior and posterior sheets around the lens equator, is called the canal of Hannover. Behind the zonule, between its fibres and the front surface of the vitreous, there is a further narrow ring of fluid called the space of Petit. Both are named rather than mysterious. The canal of Hannover is the zonule's own lane, and the space of Petit is the lane just behind it. The complete tour of the posterior segment's chambers and spaces, with the other named gaps around the lens and the vitreous, is a story we will take up in Season 7, episode five, Vitreous Chambers and Spaces. For today, those two names are the ones that belong to the zonule's own address.

Host: Before the clinical chapter, I want the origin story. When does this suspension system appear in a human life? Does the zonule form with the eye, or after it?

Guide: With the eye, and surprisingly early. Around the third month of development, the gap between the growing equator of the lens and the inner rim of the ciliary region is filled by a loose mesh of cells and fibres, and from that mesh the zonular fibres condense and tighten into straps. Because this mesh was counted as the third differentiation of the vitreous, the old texts called it the tertiary vitreous, the name I mentioned earlier. The elegant detail is who makes the material. The ciliary epithelium itself, the same non-pigmented layer whose basement membrane we chose as the starting line, secretes the fibrillin that assembles into microfibrils. So the tissue builds the strap it will later be anchored to. From there the zonule grows with the eye, and as the lens equator expands toward its adult diameter, the attachment band migrates outward with it, always around the equator, always spanning the same gap. By birth the suspension is in place, and apart from its slow ageing it is the same ring we will examine in our patients.

Host: Now the part the clinic cares about. Why does an optometrist, who is not holding a scalpel, need this anatomy on the tip of the tongue?

Guide: Because when the suspension fails, the lens moves, and you can see it move. Two named signs belong here. Phakodonesis is a trembling of the lens: when the patient shifts the eye from side to side, the lens wobbles on whatever fibres remain, because the support is uneven or gone. Iridodonesis is the trembling of the iris that goes with it, because the iris rests against the front of the lens and loses its firm backing when that backing wobbles. Both are slit lamp signs, seen with a beam on the eye while the patient moves it, and both are quiet announcements that the zonule is compromised somewhere around the ring. There is a deeper anatomical point too. The lens and the iris together form a soft partition between the anterior and posterior segments, the lens-iris diaphragm, and the zonule is what keeps the lens component centred and steady so that partition holds its position behind the pupil. Centring matters as much as support: because the fibres pull evenly from all sides of the ring, the lens sits square in the visual axis, and the day the pull becomes uneven is the day the lens begins to drift.

Host: What conditions actually weaken these fibres, and what does each look like in the anatomy we have just learned?

Guide: Four classics, and each maps beautifully onto today's structures. First, Marfan syndrome, an inherited condition caused by a mutation in the gene for fibrillin-1 on chromosome fifteen. Remember that fibrillin is the signature protein of the zonule, so in Marfan the strap is built from faulty material from the start, and the lens partially displaces, a subluxation, classically upward and outward, usually in both eyes. Second, homocystinuria, another inherited metabolic condition, which also weakens the zonule but tends to displace the lens downward and inward, a direction contrast that examiners never tire of. Third, blunt trauma. A ball or a fist compresses the globe, the equator stretches outward in that instant, and a sector of zonular fibres can tear from their anchors. The loss of fibres in one sector is called a zonular dialysis, dialysis meaning a splitting away, and it leaves that quadrant of the lens unsupported. Fourth, pseudoexfoliation, in which flaky fibrillar material, a kind of dandruff of the lens and capsule, is deposited across the anterior segment, including over the zonular fibres, coating them and weakening them. It matters enormously before cataract surgery, because coated fibres can tear under instruments. Four failures, one common thread: the strap, the ring you now know fibre by fibre.

Host: And time itself. We have aged with every season of this series. Do the zonules age, and what does that do to the anatomy?

Guide: They do, and gently. The elastic quality of the fibres declines across the decades, so the straps stiffen little by little, and because their protein turns over very slowly, they must simply endure rather than renew. Age changes in the zonule never act alone, though. The capsule loses its give, the lens grows and stiffens from within, and the ciliary muscle changes its habits with the years. We have met the combined consequence before, back in Series 1: there was a whole episode titled Accommodation, and another titled Presbyopia, and they told the functional story of arms becoming too short for fine print in the forties and fifties. What I want you to take from today is the anatomy half of that story, the straps losing their spring. And when we reach episode nine of this season, Anatomical Basis of Accommodation, we will assemble the whole machine, capsule elasticity, zonular tension and ciliary muscle geometry, into one moving system.

Host: One confusion to clear before we close, because the words rhyme and my notes keep tangling them. Lens fibres, zonular fibres. Are they the same kind of thing, two names for related structures, or completely different animals that share a word?

Guide: Completely different animals that share a word, and it is worth ten seconds to separate them for good. Lens fibres, last episode's subject, are the cells that fill the lens itself, hexagonal slabs packed in shells inside the capsule, the living glass of the eye. Zonular fibres are entirely outside the lens. They are non-cellular strands of fibrillin microfibrils, suspended in the posterior chamber, running from the ciliary epithelium to the capsule, and not one of them is a cell, and not one of them is inside the lens. One is the stuff the eye is made with, and the other is the rope the eye is hung with. If a question or a colleague ever blurs them, go back to location. Inside the capsule, lens fibre. Outside the lens, between ciliary body and equator, zonular fibre. The words stop tangling the moment you hang them on their addresses.

Host: Then walk me home. Give me this episode in one minute, the way you would want it to survive my memory.

Guide: Gladly. The zonule of Zinn, the suspensory ligament of the lens, is an annular ring of fine elastic strands spanning the gap between the ciliary body and the lens equator, hidden behind the iris in the posterior chamber. It is built not of collagen but of fibrillin-rich microfibrils about ten nanometres across, bundled into strands a few micrometres wide. The fibres are born on the basement membrane of the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium, from a band about one and a half millimetres wide across the pars plana and from the valleys between the roughly seventy ciliary processes, and they anchor in the lens capsule from about one millimetre in front of the equator to about one and a quarter millimetres behind it. They group into three classic sets, the anterior zonular fibres, the posterior zonular fibres and the equatorial zonular fibres, with a woven plexus at the equator. The canal of Hannover is the ring of space the fibres occupy, and the space of Petit lies just behind them. Marfan syndrome, homocystinuria, blunt trauma and pseudoexfoliation are the classic ways the ring fails, and phakodonesis and iridodonesis are the signs that announce it at the slit lamp.
