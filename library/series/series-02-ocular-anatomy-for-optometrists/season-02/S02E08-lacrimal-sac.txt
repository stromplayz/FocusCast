Host: A reservoir, a chamber, buried in bone. I want to find it physically before we measure it. If I press a fingertip just below the inner corner of my eye, beside the bridge of my nose, am I anywhere near it? And where exactly does it sit within the orbit we mapped at the very start of this series?

Guide: You are warmer than you might think. The sac sits on the medial wall of the orbit, in its lower anterior part, immediately behind the skin and muscle that you can pinch at the inner corner. Remember that in the very first episode of this series, The Orbit: An Optometrist's Anatomical Map, we described the orbit as a four-sided pyramid with its base opening onto the face and its peak pointing backward toward the optic canal. Take the medial wall of that pyramid, the side that faces the nose, and run your attention down toward its front edge, just behind where the eyelids meet at the medial canthus. There, set into the bone like a vertical groove, is a hollow called the lacrimal fossa, and the lacrimal sac lives inside that fossa the way a fist sits inside a cupped hand. It is small, it is hidden, and yet it lies close enough to the surface that its neighbourhood is one of the few drainage addresses you can genuinely examine from the outside. And unlike most buried orbital structures, its height on the wall is marked for you by the tendon and the skin folds of the medial canthus, so this is less a hidden landmark and more a known address.

Host: A groove in the bone. Which bones are we standing on when we look into that fossa? Give me the names, the crests, the whole wall. And since you said buried, tell me how tall this hollow really is.

Guide: The fossa is built where two bones meet along a vertical seam, and each bone raises a ridge, a crest, along that seam. In front stands the frontal process of the maxilla, the strong bone of the cheek and nose-side region, and its ridge is the anterior lacrimal crest. Behind stands the lacrimal bone itself, a small, delicate plate, and its ridge is the posterior lacrimal crest. Between the two crests lies the fossa, a vertical groove roughly fifteen to sixteen millimetres tall and only a few millimetres deep. Now comes the detail that makes this fossa memorable. The two bones are nothing alike in strength. The frontal process of the maxilla is dense and solid, firm enough that you can feel the anterior lacrimal crest through the skin of a living patient with a fingertip. The lacrimal bone behind it is paper thin, so delicate that on a dried skull you can sometimes see light through it. One sturdy wall in front, one fragile wall behind, and the tear chamber cradled in between. The crests themselves stand only a few millimetres apart, which is why the whole fossa fits within a fingertip's width on the face.

Host: Paper thin. That phrase always makes me lean in, because thin bone usually means two things, that something important sits on the other side, and that something sooner or later goes through. What is actually behind that delicate plate of lacrimal bone?

Guide: The nose, and that is the architectural punchline of this entire region. Immediately behind the thin lacrimal bone, and often behind the upper part of the fossa too, sit the air cells of the ethmoid, including a small forward cell called the agger nasi. Beyond those cells lies the nasal cavity itself, and along its side wall stands the middle nasal concha, a curved bony shelf you would see if you looked upward inside a nose. So this tiny tear chamber at the corner of the eye has only a sheet of paper-thin bone and a few air cells between it and the air you breathe. Surgeons use that fact deliberately. In a dacryocystorhinostomy, an operation for a blocked drainage system, they open the floor of the fossa through that thin bone and build a new doorway, so that tears can bypass a blocked duct and flow directly into the nasal cavity. The anatomy allows it because the anatomy was always that close. The air cells themselves vary from face to face in number and size, but the thin lacrimal bone in the fossa is the one constant doorway between the two sides.

Host: So the sac faces the eyelids on one side and the nose on the other, with almost nothing between. Now measure the chamber itself for me. How long is it, how wide, and what shape does it hold when it is at rest, between blinks, doing nothing?

Guide: Anatomically, the lacrimal sac is simply the upper, dilated, blind-ended beginning of the nasolacrimal duct, so picture a funnel whose top has been closed into a rounded dome. The standard textbook figures are these. The sac runs about twelve to fifteen millimetres from its rounded top to the point where it narrows into the duct, and it measures roughly four to six millimetres across. Its walls are soft and lax, and that matters more than the numbers suggest. Empty, which is its resting state for most of the day, the sac is a collapsed, slack chamber, almost a potential space rather than an open cavity. When tears are driven into it, it distends gently, and when it empties it wrinkles closed again, like a thin rubber bulb that never quite holds its shape. The downward continuation into the duct, with its own bony canal and its own guarding valve, is a story with a beginning all its own, and that story belongs to our next episode. Only when blockage or inflammation keeps filling the chamber does it lose its slack and begin to hold a visible, rounded shape, which is exactly the distension we will read against the tendon later in this episode.

Host: You said the top is a rounded dome, and you have been holding back a word from today's outline, fundus. Split the sac into its named parts for me, and give me a number for each part, the way a textbook would.

Guide: With pleasure, because the sac has a two-part address, and the boundary between the two parts is a strap you can palpate on a living face. The upper part is the fundus, the blind, rounded dome. It extends from the very top of the sac down to the medial canthal tendon, the fibrous strap that crosses in front of the upper sac, and it rises about three to five millimetres above that tendon. Below the tendon begins the body of the sac, the larger working chamber, roughly eight to ten millimetres in vertical height. At the bottom of the body the lumen narrows and turns slightly downward to continue as the nasolacrimal duct. So when you hear fundus, think dome above the tendon. When you hear body, think main chamber below the tendon. One strap, two rooms, one continuous chamber, and every examination finding at the inner corner is read against that strap.

Host: Stop there, because that strap is doing a lot of work in this episode and it has earned its own portrait. What is the medial canthal tendon, where does it come from, and how exactly does it grip the sac?

Guide: The medial canthal tendon, which older texts call the medial palpebral ligament, is the fibrous anchor that ties the inner ends of both eyelids to the bone of the nose-side wall. Follow it from its origin. The tarsal plates, the stiff supporting skeletons of the upper and lower lids, send their medial ends toward each other, and a strong band of white fibrous tissue gathers them together and runs horizontally to the lacrimal fossa. There it divides its personality in two. The anterior limb crosses in front of the upper part of the sac and fixes to the anterior lacrimal crest on the maxilla. The posterior limb sweeps behind the sac and fixes to the posterior lacrimal crest on the lacrimal bone. So the tendon does not simply sit on the sac, it slings around it, one limb in front and one limb behind, and the rounded fundus bulges upward above the strap like the top of a pear above a collar. Two anchor points, one strap, and a chamber suspended in the loop. Functionally it anchors the lid margins against the pull of every single blink, and it is counted among the strongest soft-tissue attachments of the whole ocular adnexa.

Host: A collar with a pear poking through it, I can picture that. But give me the clinical payoff of this picture. Why did our anatomy teachers insist that we learn exactly where that tendon crosses the sac, level by level?

Guide: Because the tendon is the level you read swellings against, and few landmarks in our field are as decisive. The body of the sac lies below the tendon, close under the skin and muscle of the inner corner. When the sac is infected and distended, which is dacryocystitis, or slowly ballooned by a chronic blockage, which is a mucocoele, the swelling announces itself as a rounded bulge just below and in front of the medial canthal tendon, pointing downward along the side of the nose. It is one of the most location-specific signs we ever see. A lid infection, by contrast, spreads along the lid itself, above and in front of the tendon, following the lid margin. So the tendon becomes a diagnostic line drawn across the face, and the same landmark guides procedures, from the direction of probing to the placement of a dacryocystorhinostomy incision. The map tells you whether a swelling belongs to the sac or to the lid before you touch a single instrument, and that is why the level matters. Learn the level, and the swelling names its own origin, which is the quiet gift of this one landmark.

Host: Now the muscles, because you used the word slung, and slung implies squeeze. What muscle fibres attach themselves to this chamber, where do they come from, and what are their names?

Guide: The blink muscle, orbicularis oculi, sends two sets of hands to the sac, one superficial and one deep. The superficial set arrives with the tendon itself. Some of the fibres gathering into the medial canthal tendon, coming forward from the lids, cross over the front of the upper sac as they head for the anterior lacrimal crest, so they lie directly against the sac's front wall. The deep set has its own name. It is Horner's muscle, the lacrimal part of orbicularis oculi, and it arises behind the sac, from the posterior lacrimal crest and from the fascia covering the back of the sac. From there its fibres sweep forward, wrapping around the sac's posterior border, and blend with the pretarsal orbicularis of both eyelids. Remember from episode seven how some orbicularis fibres also spiral along the canaliculi. The same grip continues here. The whole inner corner, chamber and channels together, is clothed in the blink muscle. And the region is busier still, because even the medial rectus muscle of the eye sends its check ligament, the strap that limits and cushions the muscle's pull, to anchor near the posterior lacrimal crest, so one little crest serves the lids, the tear chamber, and eye movement all at once.

Host: So every blink tugs on the sac through those two sets of fibres. Walk me through what the chamber actually experiences during a single blink of mine. Compress, fill, empty. What is the sequence, and what keeps it all moving in one direction?

Guide: Here is the anatomical skeleton of the story, and I will be honest that the full physiology, valve by valve and frame by frame, is saved for our season finale in episode ten, where the whole drainage pathway is assembled. The sac is a slack, elastic chamber with muscle attached on two sides and fascia stretched across it. When the lids close, the pretarsal fibres and Horner's muscle shorten together. Their shortening deforms the chamber, tensing the tendon and the fascia and changing the sac's shape, so the tear that the blink squeezes along the canaliculi is received inside. When the eyes open again, the muscles relax and the elastic walls recoil, pushing the stored tear downward toward the nose. Two folds of mucosa make that flow one-way. The valve of Rosenmuller guards the canaliculus entry above, and we met it in episode seven, and a second valve guards the exit below, waiting for us next time. Fill on the blink, empty between blinks. That is the rhythm the architecture imposes. Notice also that no separate drainage muscle is needed, because the anatomy borrows the blink, the one movement the eyes make many thousands of times a day.

Host: You called the walls slack and elastic, and I want the wall itself now, layer by layer, because this is an anatomy series and we always open the chamber walls. What exactly am I made of, if I am the lacrimal sac wall?

Guide: Gladly, from the outside inward. Around the fossa, the periosteum of the orbit behaves unusually. Instead of hugging the bony floor of the groove, it bridges across from the anterior lacrimal crest to the posterior lacrimal crest, forming a sheet called the lacrimal fascia that roofs over the sac like the canvas of a tent. Between that fascia and the sac wall there is only a thin cleft, through which small vessels wander. The sac wall itself is fibroelastic tissue lined on the inside by mucosa, the same moist lining family as the nose and the canaliculi. Its surface is columnar epithelium sprinkled with goblet cells, which add mucus to the stored tears, and it is thrown into small folds and ledges that let the chamber expand without tearing, the way a bellows folds. Beneath the epithelium lies a loose layer rich in elastic fibres and a fine network of small veins, a soft, spongy cushion that helps the wall distend smoothly and recoil completely. The mucosa, incidentally, is continuous upward with the lining of the canaliculi and downward with the lining of the duct and the nose, one unbroken inner skin for the entire pathway.

Host: Those folds you mentioned, one of them sounded like a door in your pump story. Remind me where the canaliculi actually enter the sac, and where that door hangs. Just the address, please, without re-teaching the whole canaliculus episode.

Guide: A fair request, since episode seven, Canaliculi, owns that journey in full. Here is only the address. The upper and lower canaliculi converge and enter the sac on its lateral wall, the side facing the eye, right at the junction of the fundus and the body. That entry point is called the internal common punctum, and it sits a few millimetres below the dome, guarded by a fold of mucosa, the valve of Rosenmuller, which we described carefully in episode seven. Notice what this placement means. Tears do not pour into the top of the fundus like rain into an open tank. They enter below it, on the eye-facing wall, which is why the fundus above the entry behaves as a slack reserve dome, and why the pump can fill even while the eye between blinks looks perfectly dry. The entry point also marks the border you now know by heart, fundus above, body below. In a distended, inflamed sac, it is the tension of these very walls, stretched close against the fascia and the periosteum of the fossa, that turns tenderness into a reliable finding.

Host: Now give me the neighbourhood. A chamber this buried must have neighbours on every face. Anterior, posterior, lateral, medial, inferior. Take me around the lacrimal sac like a tour guide on a street corner.

Guide: Let us walk the compass slowly. Anterior to the sac runs the medial canthal tendon, and just in front of the tendon, between it and the skin, pass the angular artery and vein, the vessels of the nose-side corner. That is a neighbour worth respecting, because anything aimed at the sac meets those vessels first, and they bleed readily. Posterior lies the origin of Horner's muscle on the posterior lacrimal crest, with a little fat and fascia behind that. Laterally the sac faces the orbit itself, separated from it by the lacrimal fascia, and it is that lateral wall which receives the common canaliculus. Medially, beyond the thin lacrimal bone, come the ethmoid air cells and the middle nasal concha we described earlier, and beyond them the air of the nose. Inferiorly the sac narrows and hands its contents to the nasolacrimal duct. Lid and muscle in front, muscle behind, orbit to the side, nose beyond the wall, duct below. That is the full address.

Host: Blood supply next. Which arteries feed this small chamber, and where does its blood leave? You already waved at the angular vessels in front of the tendon, so connect that thread for me completely.

Guide: The sac is fed generously for its size. The medial palpebral branches of the ophthalmic artery reach it from the orbit side, following the same corner the eye itself is served from, and the infraorbital artery sends contributions from below and outside. Venous blood leaves through two doors. Some drains backward into the superior ophthalmic vein of the orbit, and some drains forward into the angular vein in front of the tendon, which joins the facial veins of the face. Keep both doors in mind, because they mean the sac region sits on a crossroads between the venous world of the face and the venous world of the orbit. That crossroads is one anatomical reason infection in this corner is never dismissed lightly. A tender, inflamed sac is not merely a plumbing problem. It is a place where two venous territories touch, and the anatomy never lets us forget it. The same small veins in the sac wall we met a moment ago belong to this network, which is why the wall looks spongy and why congestion shows up there early.

Host: And the nerves. This is a corner of the face where I would want to know exactly which nerve carries sensation, both for understanding the pain a patient describes and for understanding what anaesthesia is numbing.

Guide: The sensation of the sac region travels with the infratrochlear nerve. Follow its pedigree upward, because it is a well-bred branch. It is the terminal branch of the nasociliary nerve, which itself is a branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, the great sensory nerve of the face. The infratrochlear nerve emerges near the trochlea, the little fibrous pulley through which the superior oblique tendon slides, and then runs down the nose-side wall, supplying the lacrimal sac, the region of the canaliculi, the medial canthus, and the side of the nose. That is why inflammation of the sac produces pain that localizes so precisely to the inner corner and beside the bridge of the nose, and it is the nerve that goes quiet when anaesthesia is placed near the medial canthus. Alongside the sensory fibres, sympathetic fibres travelling with the vessel walls follow the arteries in, as they do throughout the orbit. Pain in this corner has a nameable address, and now you know it.

A watering eye means that somewhere along the drainage route the flow is failing, and your anatomy hands you the stations in order, puncta from episode six, canaliculi from episode seven, sac today, duct next time. The sac announces itself in a location no other structure claims, that rounded swelling below the medial canthal tendon, warm and tender when it is infected, silent and slowly enlarging when it is chronically blocked. Press gently over the swelling and content may reflux through the canaliculi if the entry valve gives way, which again is anatomy answering a clinical question. Remember from Series 1, in the episode Recognising Red Flags, how we trained ourselves to separate the urgent from the routine. An acutely inflamed, painful, tense sac sits firmly on the urgent side, partly because of that venous crossroads we described, and partly because the tissue space here is small and unforgiving. Every step you observe, document, or refer for is a line on the map you learned today.

Host: Before we close, walk me through the whole episode in miniature. The fossa, the dimensions, the tendon, the muscles, the walls, the supply. A recitation I can carry into clinic tomorrow morning.

Guide: Here is the episode folded small. The lacrimal sac is the blind upper chamber of the nasolacrimal duct, sitting in the lacrimal fossa, a vertical groove about fifteen to sixteen millimetres tall on the lower anterior part of the medial orbital wall, floored by paper-thin lacrimal bone behind and firm maxilla in front, with ethmoid air cells and the nasal cavity just beyond. Its blood arrives from ophthalmic and infraorbital branches, its veins join the facial and orbital worlds, and its sensation rides the infratrochlear nerve. That is the sac.
