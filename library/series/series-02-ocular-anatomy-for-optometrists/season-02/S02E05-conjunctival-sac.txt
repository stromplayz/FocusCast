# Description: Where do tears actually live between blinks? In this episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks opens the conjunctival sac, the thin continuous lining that turns eyelids and eyeball into a sealed tear reservoir. Learn how the palpebral and bulbar sheets meet at the fornices, why the marginal tear strips and the tear meniscus hold most of the seven microlitres the sac owns, and what the lacrimal caruncle and the plica semilunaris really are. A clear anatomical map for optometry students, and the perfect on-ramp to the drainage episodes ahead.

Host: Welcome back, old friend. This is Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library where one eye care topic at a time gets the room it deserves, and we are deep inside our second series, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists. We are in season two, the season of the lacrimal system, the anatomy of tears, and we have arrived at episode five. In the first four episodes we built the supply side of the story. Episode one, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy, gave us the main gland sitting in its fossa in the roof of the orbit. Episode two split that gland into its orbital and palpebral lobes. Episode three met the accessory glands of Krause and Wolfring. And last time, in episode four, we laid the tear film out in its three layers and followed its renewal. But a supply is only half of any system. Today we study the container itself, the quiet space where every one of those tears actually lives. It is called the conjunctival sac. Walk us in.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to every listener. Let me start with the word itself, because the name of this structure is a small lesson all by itself. Conjunctiva comes from Latin roots that mean to join together, and that is literally the job of this membrane. It is a thin, transparent sheet of tissue that joins the inside of the eyelid to the front of the eyeball, so that the lid can glide over the eye without the two linings ever separating. Now, a mucous membrane, in plain words, is a wet internal lining, the same family of tissue as the lining of your mouth. It stays moist, and its surface cells can add mucus to the film. The conjunctiva has two great sheets. The sheet lining the back of each eyelid is the palpebral conjunctiva, from palpebra, the Latin word for eyelid. The sheet draped over the front of the eyeball is the bulbar conjunctiva, named from the bulb, the old name for the globe itself. And the enclosed space between those two sheets is our subject today, the conjunctival sac.

Host: A sac. I want to picture it honestly, because the word suggests something like a leather pouch, and the space between an eyelid and an eyeball is not obviously shaped like one. Is it really a bag? Where are its walls, and where does it open?

Guide: Picture it not as a pouch hanging in space, but as the inside of a folded envelope. Take the upper lid. At the free edge of that lid, right along the margin, the dry skin stops and a wet lining begins. That lining climbs up the inside of the lid, smooth and tight, until it reaches a deep fold at the top. There it turns around, folds back on itself, and comes down onto the front of the eyeball as the bulbar conjunctiva. It slides down the white of the eye until it reaches the limbus, the border where the transparent cornea begins, and there the lining stops, because the cornea has its own epithelium. Across the exposed front of the eye, between the two lid margins, the wall of the sac is not tissue at all. It is the tear film itself, a thin curtain of fluid bridging lid margin to lid margin. Then the same loop runs again in reverse along the lower lid. So the conjunctiva is one continuous sheet, and the space it encloses is sealed wherever the lids meet. The only opening is the palpebral fissure, the slit between the lids, and that opening exists only while the eyes are open. Close the eyes, and the sac becomes a fully closed chamber.

Host: A closed chamber, then, whenever we sleep. Before we walk its walls piece by piece, give me the headline numbers. How much liquid does this chamber actually hold? When I hear the word reservoir, I confess I imagine something large.

Guide: Here is the surprise, and it is one of my favourite numbers in all of ocular anatomy. The conjunctival sac at any given moment holds about seven microlitres of tears. A microlitre, for anyone who has not handled the word, is a millionth of a litre, a thousandth of a millilitre. Seven of them is less than a single drop from a dropper bottle, because a typical eye drop is around thirty to fifty microlitres. And the absolute capacity of the sac, the most it can hold before tears spill over the lid margins, is somewhere between twenty-five and thirty microlitres. So one drop, on its own, already exceeds what the sac can retain. Most of a drop either spills across the margin or is carried away into the drainage system within moments. That is why I love this pair of numbers. It tells you that the reservoir we are studying is not a cup. It is a tiny, constantly renewed film system, closer to the water held in a squeezed sponge than to a water tank. Last episode, episode four, Tear Film Anatomy, we met the steady trickle that feeds it and the turnover that renews it. Today we meet the vessel that holds it.

Host: Then let us walk the vessel. Start behind the lids. What exactly lines the wall that faces the eye when the lid is down?

Guide: That wall is the palpebral conjunctiva, and its defining feature is grip. It is sewn firmly to the tarsal plate, the stiff rectangle of dense connective tissue that gives each lid its shape, so this lining lies perfectly smooth, with no slack and no folds. That firm bond is why the lining never wrinkles when the lid moves. Anatomists divide the palpebral conjunctiva into three named portions, travelling from the edge of the lid toward the vault. First comes the marginal portion, a narrow strip at the very edge of the lid, right where the wet lining begins. Second is the tarsal portion, the thin sheet fixed over the tarsal plate itself. This part is remarkably thin and richly supplied with blood vessels, which is why, when an upper lid is everted, you are looking at a translucent membrane through which the vessels are clearly visible. Third is the orbital portion, between the upper edge of the tarsal plate and the fornix. Here the bond loosens, the tissue becomes redundant and folded, ready to be gathered up or paid out as the lid and the eye move against each other. Three portions, one gradient, from sewn tight at the margin to hanging loose at the vault.

Host: Stop at that edge for a moment, because the marginal strip is one of the treasures of today's episode. What exactly happens at the border where the dry outside of the lid becomes the wet inside? Anatomists draw a line there, do they not?

Guide: They draw a line with a beautiful name, the mucocutaneous junction, the frontier where skin, with its keratin and its lashes, hands over to mucous membrane, wet and smooth. On each lid margin it runs along the back border, just behind the row of meibomian gland openings, those little oil ports that stand in a line behind the lashes. Behind that frontier, the wet world begins. The strip of conjunctiva between the mucocutaneous junction and the next landmark is called the marginal conjunctiva, and just beyond it, on the upper lid, there is a shallow horizontal channel called the subtarsal groove, lying about three millimetres from the lid margin. That groove matters far more than its size suggests. It is a classic resting place for stray foreign bodies, a tiny shelf where a grain of grit or a lost eyelash can settle and hide, which is exactly why an everted upper lid is examined with such care. The deeper story of the lid margin itself, its glands and its lines, belongs to the later season that walks the lids. And the full anatomy of the palpebral conjunctiva, zone by zone, waits for next season in an episode called Palpebral Conjunctiva. Today we keep the strip as a boundary, because the boundary is where the tears begin.

Host: Cross over to the eyeball then. Describe the second wall, the one draped over the moving globe itself.

Guide: The bulbar conjunctiva is the loose twin. From the fornices it drapes forward over the sclera, the white outer coat of the eye, and for most of that journey it is only loosely attached, lying on the globe the way silk lies on a shoulder. It is transparent, which is why the white of the eye and its fine vessels are visible at all, and the looseness gives it slack, so it folds and shifts as the eye rotates beneath it. Then comes the striking exception. As it approaches the limbus, the conjunctiva welds itself down, tightly, for the last millimetre or so, binding firmly to the tissue beneath and blending with the epithelium of the cornea beyond. Listeners who have been with us since Series 1 will remember that border from the episode called The Front of the Eye, where we met the cornea as the clear window of the eye. One more layer belongs to this wall. Between conjunctiva and sclera there sits a thin fibrous sheet called Tenon's capsule, the socket's own slipcover, and the partnership between conjunctiva and Tenon, along with the whole surface story, is next season's territory, in an episode called Bulbar Conjunctiva. For today, this wall's job is simple. It is the smooth, transparent back wall of the tear reservoir.

Host: Now the opening. The sac is only a sealed sac when the lids are shut. While we are awake and looking at the world, there is a slit open to the air. How big is that opening, and what does it mean for the tears inside?

Guide: The palpebral fissure, in an adult, measures roughly thirty millimetres from corner to corner, and about ten millimetres from edge to edge at its widest. That opening is a compromise the anatomy has struck. Wide enough to admit light and the world, narrow enough that the exposed surface stays small, and every exposed square millimetre of it has to be kept wet by the film. While the lids are open, gravity and evaporation pull the tears downward, so the standing stock gathers along the lower lid. When the lids close, the fissure seals at the margins, and the geometry changes completely. The sac becomes a closed, capillary space, and the tears are drawn away from the margins into the deep recesses. So the shape of this reservoir is not fixed. It is a shape that breathes with the blink, opening and closing, and that single fact is the key to everything we say from here.

Host: Which brings us to the corners, the places where the two sheets actually turn from wall to wall. The fornices. I know listeners have heard the word before. Make it physical for us.

Guide: Fornix is Latin for vault or arch, the same root that names the brick vaults of Roman cellars. Each fornix is the fold where the palpebral sheet turns around to become the bulbar sheet. There is a superior fornix above, an inferior fornix below, and a lateral fornix at the outer corner. Their depths, measured back from the limbus, are standard textbook figures. The superior fornix reaches about ten millimetres behind the limbus. The inferior fornix about eight. The lateral fornix is the deepest of the three, extending about fourteen millimetres. On the medial side, toward the nose, there is no true fornix at all, because two structures interrupt the reflection, and we will meet them in a moment. Now, why do these depths matter. The redundancy of the fornices is mechanical. They are the pleats of an accordion, spare folds of lining that unfold as the eye swings to its extremes, which is why full gaze in any direction never tears the membrane. And they are the reservoir's back pockets. The superior fornix is precisely where the main lacrimal gland delivers its tears, through the handful of fine ducts, classically counted between six and twelve, that we traced back in episode one, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy. The accessory glands of Krause, from episode three, Accessory Lacrimal Glands, nest in the fornices too. There is a whole episode called Fornices waiting next season. Today, hold the image of deep, loose pockets that receive fresh tears where they are born.

Host: Then let us go to that medial corner right now, because you promised us an interruption. When I look in a mirror at the inner corner of my eye, I see a small pink mound and, beside it, a little crescent of tissue. What are they?

Guide: The pink mound is the lacrimal caruncle, from the Latin for a little piece of flesh. It is a small nodule of modified skin sitting in the corner, complete with its own fine hairs, sebaceous glands and sweat glands. I find that delightful. It is the one patch of true skin living inside the otherwise wet world of the sac, and its tiny oily glands can shed fine flakes onto the tear surface. Immediately beside it, on its outer side, lies the plica semilunaris, a crescent-shaped fold of conjunctiva whose name means simply the half-moon fold. This is the ghost of an older anatomy. In birds, reptiles and many mammals there is a third eyelid, the nictitating membrane, a translucent shutter that sweeps across the eye. In humans that shutter has dwindled to this single fold of slack tissue, and its remaining job is mechanical. When the eye turns fully toward the nose, the structures in that corner would be pushed and stretched. The plica folds and unfolds like a bellows, absorbing the change in length so the lining never tears. Between the caruncle and the plica, and pooling around them, lies the lacrimal lake, the small standing pool of tears at the inner corner. Remember that lake. It is the exit lounge of the reservoir.

Host: Now the heart of the matter. We know the container, its walls, its pockets, its corner furniture. Tell us where the tears themselves actually sit. Between two blinks, with the eye open, if I could stain every microlitre and light it up, where would the water be?

Guide: You would see the answer as two glowing strips. Along the back edge of each lid margin, upper and lower, runs a wedge of tears, a strip with a triangular cross-section, tallest against the lid margin, thinning out toward the eye, with a gently concave top surface. That concave wedge is why it is called the tear meniscus, from the Greek meniskos, a little crescent moon, the same word physics uses for the curved surface of water in a narrow tube. What holds it there is capillarity, the same quiet force that makes water climb between two glass slides pressed together. The angle between the lid margin and the surface of the eye is a narrow gap, and a narrow gap grips fluid and draws its surface upward into a curve. The two strips are not equal. The lower one, resting along the lower margin where gravity collects everything, is the larger and is the main visible reservoir of the open eye. Its height, in the normal eye, is somewhere around a third of a millimetre, generally under half. The upper strip is smaller and largely hidden behind the margin. So when anatomists speak of the marginal tear strips, they mean these two wedges, the shelves on which the sac keeps most of its working stock.

Host: That genuinely surprises me. I pictured the tears as a thin puddle lying evenly over the whole eye, like water on a table. You are telling me the water is gathered at the edges instead. Why a wedge at the margin rather than a flat sheet across the middle?

Guide: Because the eye is not a dish, and the tears are not poured onto it. They are held by surface tension in a system that has no flat bottom. The precorneal tear film, the coating over the exposed cornea, is continuous with both marginal strips. There is no border where film ends and meniscus begins. It is one connected body of fluid, and if you imagine a soap film stretched between two rings, the rings are the lid margins and the thickened edges of the film are the menisci. Surface tension pulls the edges up the margins and pulls the middle thin. So the distribution of our seven microlitres is lopsided by design. Most of the volume stands in the two strips and in the fornices behind them. Only a remarkably thin coat, the kind of thickness we quantified last episode, actually rides over the cornea between blinks. The sac is less like a cup of water and more like the ink held inside a fountain pen, most of it stored away in the reservoir, only a whisper of it delivered to the point of work.

Host: And this whole distribution is alive, in motion, because of blinking. Walk us through the blink from the reservoir's point of view. And then the longer stillness, when the eyes close for the night. Where does the water go?

Guide: Watch the lower lid first, because it does the quiet work. With each blink the lid sweeps upward like a squeegee, gathers the strip ahead of it, spreads a fresh layer across the exposed surface, and redistributes the reserve. On closure, the margins meet and the seal is made. Now the geometry inverts. The fissure is gone, the capillary spaces of the superior and inferior fornices open up, and the tears are drawn out of the shrinking marginal gaps into those deep recesses, held there by capillarity against the folded lining. The sleeping sac is a closed chamber with its water tucked into its pockets. On waking, as the lids part, gravity reclaims its authority, the film is pulled back down, and the reservoir re-forms where we can see it, along the lower margin, ready to drain. Nothing is lost in any of these transitions, because the lining is continuous from margin to margin, and the tears never leave the system on their way around it. The reservoir simply changes address with every blink and every closure, from shelves to pockets and back again.

Host: Which brings us to the exit. A reservoir is not meant to hoard. Where does the water leave, and what waits at the door?

Guide: The exit is at the medial corner, and the strips themselves are the gutters that lead there. Along the lower marginal strip, tears creep toward the nose, and the upper strip runs its own parallel path, because the capillary groove of each margin conducts fluid toward the inner canthus. There they gather in the lacrimal lake we met earlier, the pool tucked against the caruncle and the plica semilunaris. From that lake, tears pass into tiny openings set on small swellings of the lid margins near the inner corner, and those openings are the puncta. And that is exactly where we pause today, at the doorway. The anatomy of the puncta, their position on that little swelling, their diameter, the direction they face, and the small vertical well just beneath each one, is the whole subject of our next episode.

Host: Let me ask the question an optometrist carries out of this room. No management talk, just anatomy. What does knowing this container change about the way you look at an eye at the microscope? What does the structure let you see?

Guide: Four bridges, all built from structure alone. First, the strip is a level, a visible line of fluid, and its height is a direct read-out of the reservoir. A normal lower strip stands about a third of a millimetre; when it climbs toward half a millimetre and above, you are looking at a reservoir fuller than it can comfortably hold, tears at the margin about to spill. Second, the capacity arithmetic explains a familiar everyday event. A thirty to fifty microlitre drop meets a sac that holds about seven and can retain at most twenty-five to thirty, so the anatomy predicts that most of any drop leaves the sac at once. Third, the depths we memorized are search geometry. A superior fornix reaching ten millimetres behind the limbus can conceal a foreign body or a lost lash beyond a casual glance, which is why the lids are everted and why the subtarsal groove and the fornices are examined deliberately. Fourth, the medial corner is a signpost district. Caruncle, plica and lacrimal lake are the fixed landmarks from which every drainage structure of the coming episodes is located. None of that requires a word about disease. It is pure architecture, and the architecture pays rent every single clinic day.

Host: One clarification before we close, because I suspect a listener is holding this question. Is the cornea inside the sac? You said the lining stops at the limbus, so what is the status of that bare, exposed window in the middle?

Guide: Think of the cornea as the courtyard, not the rooms. The conjunctival lining wraps the walls, the lids behind and the sclera in front, and it stops at the limbus because the cornea brings its own specialised epithelium, the one we studied back in The Front of the Eye. But the space of the sac still includes the surface of that courtyard, because the tear film lies over the cornea continuously with the strips at the margins. When the eye is open, the film itself is the front wall of the sac across the exposed zone, a wall made of water instead of tissue. That is why the cornea, with no blood vessels of its own and nothing but its epithelium for cover, depends absolutely on the reservoir we have described today. Its moisture is borrowed. It lives inside someone else's container.

Host: So, in one breath, the whole structure. Give me the conjunctival sac the way you would want a student to hold it.

Guide: A single continuous, transparent mucous membrane, one sheet with three names, palpebral behind the lids, bulbar on the globe, fornices at the folds. It encloses a potential space, sealed at the margins, open only at the palpebral fissure, holding about seven microlitres of tears, with a maximum nearer twenty-five to thirty. Most of that stock stands in the two marginal tear strips, wedges of fluid about a third of a millimetre high along the lid margins, continuous with the film that bridges the cornea, topped up in the superior fornix where the gland ducts deliver, and gathering medially in the lacrimal lake. The fornices, ten and eight and fourteen millimetres deep, are the pockets that let the eye move and the lids close without a tear in the lining. And at the inner corner, the caruncle and the plica semilunaris mark the doorway where the reservoir hands its water on. That is the sac. A tiny reservoir, precisely built, endlessly refilled.
