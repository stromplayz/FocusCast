# Description: Two tiny channels carry every tear from the lid margin to the lacrimal sac, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks follows them all the way. We trace the vertical and horizontal canaliculus, the common internal punctum, and the valve of Rosenmuller, and map their relations to the lid margin and the medial canthus. A clear, optometry-student guide to the middle stretch of the tear drainage pathway, with the real measurements and landmarks that make the anatomy memorable.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series, and we are seven episodes into the season on the Lacrimal System, walking the complete life of a tear, structure by structure. Here is the ground we have covered. Episodes one to three gave us the producers, the lacrimal gland with its lobes and ducts and the small accessory glands tucked into the fornices. Episode four examined the tear film itself, layer by layer. Episode five mapped the conjunctival sac, the quiet reservoir where tears pool before they leave. And in episode six, Puncta, we found the two tiny doorways on the lid margins where drainage officially begins. Today we follow those doorways inward, into channels so small that most patients never suspect they exist, and yet every clear, comfortable eye depends on them every few seconds. Today, the canaliculi.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome. Let me define the word before we travel, because it does all the work today. A canaliculus is simply a little canal, a miniature tube, and canaliculi is just the plural; every person normally has two lacrimal canaliculi, one in the upper lid and one in the lower. Each begins at a punctum, the pinhead opening we mapped last episode, and each ends at the lacrimal sac, the small reservoir resting against the nose-side bone of the orbit. Between those two points runs a journey of roughly ten millimetres with genuine architecture inside it: a vertical drop into the lid, a sharp bend, a long horizontal run toward the nose, a meeting of the two channels, a shared doorway, and a fold of tissue acting as a gate. Back in Series 1, an episode called The Front of the Eye showed us the tear film sweeping over the surface with every blink. Today we follow what that blink sweeps away, and we start with the first two millimetres. Keep the scale in mind as we go: everything today happens within about a centimetre of the corner of the eye, and almost all of it is hidden from any ordinary glance.

Host: Then let us pick up exactly where episode six left us. We were on the lid margin, on a small mound of tissue called the papilla, looking at the punctum on its summit, an opening of a fraction of a millimetre facing the eye. Just beyond that opening sat the ampulla, the little flask-shaped widening of the channel. The tear has passed the doorway and rests in the flask. What happens as it goes deeper?

Guide: Next comes the first segment of the canaliculus proper, and its direction surprises students every season, because the channel does not run sideways at first. It runs vertically. From the punctum, the canaliculus travels about two millimetres straight into the substance of its own lid. In the upper lid the channel ascends, climbing upward from the margin; in the lower lid it descends, sinking downward from the margin. The two vertical segments mirror each other, each diving away from the exposed surface and into soft tissue, and fibres of the pretarsal orbicularis wind around each one as a snug muscular collar, because this stretch of the channel, like the doorway above it, lives inside muscle rather than beside it. Picture the path of a probe entering the lower punctum: it points downward for two millimetres, and only then is it turned. That first vertical stroke is not an accident of development; it is a deliberate piece of plumbing, and its purpose becomes clear when we ask why the channel does not simply slide sideways along the margin.

Host: Let us ask exactly that. The punctum already sits on the lid margin, and the sac waits further along the same margin, toward the nose. Why waste two millimetres travelling perpendicular to the direction of flow? Why vertical at all?

Guide: Two good anatomical reasons. The first is geometry. The punctum opens on the summit of a papilla that protrudes from the margin, and its opening faces the eye; the channel must first bury itself beneath the marginal surface before it can turn and run toward the medial canthus. The vertical segment does that burying. The second reason is the little standing reservoir it creates. The ampulla you met last episode sits along this vertical stretch, and because the segment runs perpendicular to the margin, the ampulla holds its small pool of tears tucked below the level of the opening, out of the direct sweep of the blink. That combination, a perpendicular first segment and a sharp turn after it, is also the beginning of the one-way design of the whole drainage system, because the wall of the channel is muscular. I will say more about that muscle when we reach the bend, and the full pumping mechanism gets its own showcase in episode ten.

Host: Then take us to the bend. Two millimetres in, the channel changes its mind and turns. Tell us what happens there, both in shape and in the tissue of the wall.

Guide: At the deep end of the vertical run, the canaliculus turns sharply, close to a right angle, and becomes the horizontal canaliculus. Now look at the wall, because this is where the histology earns its keep. The channel is lined by stratified squamous epithelium, which in plain words means many layers of flat, skin-like cells, the same family of cells that line the outer surface of the eye, except that here they stay moist and never form the hard, dead keratin layer. Under the epithelium lies a thin basement membrane, and around everything a loose, well-perfused connective tissue, so the channel is a layered tube, not a bare tunnel. Beneath that lining sits a mesh rich in elastic fibres, stretchy protein strands that let the tube widen and spring back. And around the outside runs a sleeve of skeletal muscle fibres lent by the orbicularis oculi, the muscle that closes the lids, the one we built fibre by fibre back in season one, episode eight, Eyelid Muscles. Some anatomists describe this little sleeve as the muscle of the canaliculus. What it does at every blink is the story of the lacrimal pump, and I am deliberately saving it for episode ten, where all the parts are on the table together.

Host: Then let us run the horizontal stretch. Eight millimetres sounds short, but it crosses some crowded real estate. Where exactly does it travel, and what is it embedded in?

Guide: The horizontal canaliculus is the long leg, about eight millimetres, and it runs medially, which simply means nose-ward, within the tissues of the lid margin itself. Notice what it travels between. The tarsal plate, the stiff fibrous scaffold that gives each lid its shape, extends across the lid and peters out just short of the punctum, so the medial lid has no stiff plate; it is soft muscle and fibrous tissue, and that is precisely where our channel runs. We gave the tarsal plates their full attention in season one, episode ten, Eyelid Anatomy in Clinical Practice, so recall their firmness and then imagine the soft, tendon-like neighbourhood just beyond their medial ends. Behind the canaliculus, on the eye side, lies the conjunctiva lining the lid. In front of it lie the fibres of the orbicularis. The channel is therefore sandwiched, a tiny hidden tube inside the margin, running toward the corner of the eye.

Host: How close to the surface does that run take it? If the channel is inside the lid margin, how much tissue lies between it and the outside world, and why does that matter to us?

Guide: Very little, and that is the clinically important answer. The horizontal canaliculus hugs the margin, running in the tissues immediately deep to the marginal surface, within a couple of millimetres of the skin and the grey line. It is, in effect, a superficial structure, which is why trauma finds it so easily. A cut through the full thickness of the lid margin anywhere medial to the punctum has a real chance of slicing the channel, because the channel is exactly there, just behind the margin, running in the same direction as the cut. Notice the geometry of the danger: the channel and the wound lie in the same horizontal plane, so a slicing injury parallel to the margin is the classic offender, while a tiny punctate nick perpendicular to the margin may spare the tube entirely. This is the anatomical fact behind a rule every clinician carries: a full-thickness lid margin laceration in the medial third of the lid is a possible canalicular injury until proven otherwise. The anatomy turns a simple-looking cut into a structural question.

Host: Let us stay with that for a moment, because it changes how you look at lid injuries. Paint the scene for us. What kind of injury reaches the canaliculus, and what should the anatomy whisper to the optometrist who sees one?

Guide: The typical culprits are avulsion and sharp trauma near the nose-side corner: a hook catching the lower lid, a fist, glass, a fall onto the edge of a table. When the lid is torn medially, the margin splits and the hidden tube splits with it. And remember that the corner concentrates the risk: both canaliculi converge into that small medial canthal area, so a deep injury there can threaten both channels at once. In Series 1 we spent an episode with the Patient With Red Eye, and the lesson there was to ask what happened before the eye looked angry. Here is the anatomical cousin of that lesson. When the presentation is a lid wound rather than a red eye, the question becomes structural: where does the cut sit relative to the punctum and the margin, and what lies just beneath? The canaliculus answers: it lies there, superficial, medial, and indispensable.

Host: Which of the two channels pays the higher price in practice? The upper and lower canaliculi are surely not equally exposed. Tell us how trauma distributes itself along this anatomy.

Guide: The lower canaliculus suffers more often, and the reason is anatomical. The lower lid is the more exposed curtain of the two: it is struck first, caught by flying fragments, hooked by foreign objects, and torn in falls, so the channel inside it is caught along with the lid. But do not let the statistics dull your respect for the upper channel, because the system carries an elegant redundancy. Each canaliculus is an independent route for the full ten millimetre journey, so as long as one channel stays open, the eye usually keeps a working exit, which is exactly why precision matters so much when the other one is cut. The anatomy hands you two chances and asks you not to waste them.

Host: Now let us follow the two channels to the corner itself, the medial canthus. Before we get there, describe that region for us, because it has its own little population of structures that students often mix up.

Guide: Gladly, because the corner deserves respect. The canthus is simply the corner where the upper and lower lids meet, and the medial canthus is the nose-side corner. In front of it lies the lacus lacrimalis, the small lake where tears gather before they enter the puncta; we drew that lake and its meniscus back in episode five, Conjunctival Sac. Inside the corner itself sits the caruncle, a tiny pink mound of skin-like tissue bearing fine hairs and glands, and just lateral to it, toward the eye, lies the plica semilunaris, a crescent-shaped fold of conjunctiva that lets the eye swing freely toward the nose without dragging the conjunctiva tight. Now the crucial relation for today: the canaliculi do not run through this lake or across these folds. They converge beneath it all, deep to the skin and the orbicularis, hidden in the soft tissues between the lid margins and the sac. The lake is the visible surface of the corner; the canaliculi are its hidden plumbing. Capillarity and the meniscus we described in episode five are what feed this lake, and the lake is what feeds the puncta; keep the chain in mind, because the canaliculi are simply its next link inward.

Host: And how exactly do the two channels arrive? They have run horizontally, one in the upper lid and one in the lower, toward the same corner. What meets what first, the channels or the ligament everyone mentions at this point?

Guide: The ligament enters the story first, and the relation is a classic. The medial palpebral ligament, which many clinicians call the medial canthal tendon, is a strong strap that anchors the medial ends of both tarsi to the bone beside the nose, and it passes in front of the lacrimal sac, shrouding it from the outside world; the sac itself is next episode's subject. Now place the channels. The classic description is beautifully simple: the upper canaliculus crosses above the ligament, and the lower canaliculus crosses below it, so the strap runs between the two channels like a hand slipped between two cables. Both channels are therefore in front of the sac, one above the strap and one below, converging as they go. Only after crossing that region does the drainage line reach the sac wall. Keep that image, because it explains the surface landmarks a clinician uses when working near the corner.

Host: Let us give that ligament its own moment, because it keeps appearing in this season. What is it made of, where does it attach, and why does it matter so much as a landmark?

Guide: The medial palpebral ligament is dense fibrous tissue, a true tendon in character, running horizontally at the medial canthus. Medially it fastens to the bone at and around the anterior lacrimal crest, a vertical ridge of bone that stands just in front of the hollow holding the sac; that hollow, the fossa, is the centrepiece of episode eight, so I will leave the bone for then. Laterally the ligament splits to blend into the tarsi of both lids. Functionally it anchors the lid margins to the nose-side skeleton and keeps the corner stable while the lids move. As a landmark it is priceless: visualize it and you know the sac lies immediately behind it, the canaliculi cross above and below it, and any instrument heading toward the sac must respect all three. In season one, episode nine, Eyelid Blood Supply and Innervation, we crossed this corner on the way to the lids; now you know what lies beneath it.

Host: Now the question everyone asks. The upper channel arrives, the lower channel arrives. Do they enter the sac as two separate streams, or do they join?

Guide: In roughly ninety percent of people they join. Just before the sac, the upper and lower canaliculi unite into a single short passage, the common canaliculus, about one to two millimetres long, and it is this common segment that opens into the sac. In the remaining ten percent or so, the two canaliculi keep their independence and open into the sac side by side, without a common channel. Students should hold both plans in mind, because anatomy is a population, not a diagram. When someone probes or intubates the drainage system, the common channel is the expected finding, but separate openings are a documented normal variant, not a pathology. The engineering logic is worth noticing too: the join creates a single bottleneck where a one-way gate can be installed once, rather than twice.

Host: Before we step through that doorway, let us pause on the name. The external openings on the lids are puncta, and this one is the common internal punctum. What is the logic of that naming, and why does keeping the two straight matter?

Guide: The logic is pure symmetry. Punctum is Latin for a small point, a pinprick, and the drainage system wears its puncta in two kinds: the two external puncta, one on each lid margin, visible on any ordinary examination, and the internal one, the hidden opening where the common canaliculus meets the sac. Same word, same idea, opposite side of the wall. Keeping them straight matters because records and case discussions lean on the terms constantly. An external punctal problem is a doorway problem, the territory of episode six, while an internal punctal problem lies behind the lacrimal fascia and belongs to the world of episodes eight and beyond. The names map exactly onto the anatomy, which is the best kind of naming.

Host: And that brings us to the doorway itself. Name it for us, and place it precisely. Where does the common canaliculus actually meet the sac?

Guide: The opening is called the common internal punctum, the internal counterpart of the external puncta on the lid margins. It sits on the lateral wall of the lacrimal sac, the wall facing the eye, about two to three millimetres below the summit of the sac. To be precise about the summit: the topmost, blind pocket of the sac is called the fundus, and the entry sits around the junction of the fundus and the body of the sac below it. Now, one more layer. The sac is not bare; it is wrapped in the lacrimal fascia, a curtain of the periorbita, the tough connective sheet that lines the whole orbit, drawn over the sac like a shroud. The common canaliculus pierces that fascia to reach the sac cavity, and it does so obliquely, slanting into the wall at an angle rather than punching straight through. That oblique path sounds like a footnote; it is actually the secret ingredient of the gate we are about to meet. This wall, this fascia, this angled entry: all of it is the sac's front doorstep, and next episode we cross it.

Host: Then name the gate. The valve of Rosenmuller is a phrase that appears in every textbook and every viva, and it is guarding exactly this doorway. What is it really, and what does it do?

Guide: The valve of Rosenmuller is a fold of mucous membrane at the entry of the common canaliculus into the sac, and the name valve is generous, because there is no hinged flap, nothing mechanical like a heart valve. It is a fold of the sac's lining, and it works together with the oblique course of the channel. Tears flowing from the canaliculus slide easily down the slanted passage into the sac. But pressure rising inside the sac presses the thin wall and the fold closed against the channel, so nothing climbs back up: not tears, not air, not the contents of the sac. That is the one-way design we kept meeting: a vertical segment, a sharp turn, an oblique entry, and a fold, all conspiring to keep flow moving toward the nose. And remember the system has a second named gate at its far bottom end, the valve of Hasner, where the drainage route finally opens into the nose; that one belongs to episode nine, Nasolacrimal Duct.

Host: One more measurement for our collection. How wide is this channel? When we say the punctum admits a pinhead opening, what does the canaliculus itself offer in terms of calibre?

Guide: At rest, the lumen is narrow, roughly half a millimetre, but the wall is built for stretch. That elastic fibre mesh we described beneath the epithelium lets the canaliculus distend to around a millimetre when something passes through, and then spring back to its resting calibre. This is why a fine probe can be eased along the channel after gentle dilation of the punctum, and why the channel, tiny as it looks, can carry the entire tear load of the eye between blinks. Narrow at rest, distensible on demand: another small lesson in how connective tissue is engineered.

Host: You said the wall is built for stretch. What happens when the channel itself falls short of that plan? Are there people in whom the canaliculus never forms quite right?

Guide: There are, and they extend last episode's story from the doorway to the corridor behind it. A canaliculus can be narrowed from birth, a condition called canalicular stenosis, or interrupted by an intervening membrane, or in rare cases never fully tunnelled at all, an atresia. The favourite places for such membranes are exactly the landmarks we have mapped today: somewhere along the vertical segment, or at the common internal punctum, where a thin veil of tissue can sit across the opening. The teaching point is anatomical rather than therapeutic. The pathway is a sequence of defined locations, so when a channel does not behave, the first question is which landmark is involved, and today's map is what makes that question answerable.

Host: That distensibility explains something clinicians do constantly, probing and irrigation. Walk us through the anatomy of that instrument path. What does the shape of the canaliculus predict about how the probe must travel?

Guide: The shape dictates a two-step path, and now you can predict it. Step one, the probe enters the punctum and travels vertically for its two millimetres, up in the upper lid, down in the lower. Step two, the probe is turned toward the nose and advanced horizontally for its eight millimetres, hugging the lid margin. And then the anatomy warns you to stop: near the end of that run the tip meets the sac wall obliquely, guarded by the fold, and it should slide in gently or meet a soft stop, never be forced. The channel is only about ten millimetres from doorway to sac, so a hand that pushes beyond that is not following anatomy anymore; it is manufacturing a false passage through a wall that is, at rest, about half a millimetre of delicate tissue. Respect the numbers and the tissue stays intact. Every step of the instrument path is anatomy rather than technique for its own sake: the probe simply walks the shape of the channel.

Host: One last clinical angle before we close. A patient reports a persistently watering eye. How does today's anatomy sharpen the way an optometrist thinks about that complaint, without drifting into the management we will assemble later?

Guide: It turns a vague complaint into a mapped question. Watering, which textbooks call epiphora, means tears are being made faster than they leave, or cannot leave at all, so the structural thinker asks where the traffic stops. Is the doorway patent, the punctal question we settled in episode six? Is the channel open along its ten millimetres, the question of today? Does the sac receive and pass the load, which is the business of episodes eight and nine? Each stretch of the pathway has its own anatomy, its own dimensions, and its own failure modes, and knowing the boundaries of each episode means you can describe exactly what you observed and where. Then, when the season reaches its capstone in episode ten, Complete Lacrimal Drainage Pathway, the whole route assembles in one piece, pump included.

Host: Then let us walk it once more, end to end, as a memory circuit. Give us the whole canalicular journey in one flowing paragraph, with the numbers attached.

Guide: With pleasure. Two canaliculi, one per lid. Each begins at the punctum, dives vertically for about two millimetres, upward in the upper lid and downward in the lower, past the ampulla we met last episode, and turns a near-right angle at the bend. Then comes the horizontal run, about eight millimetres, hugging the lid margin, sandwiched between conjunctiva behind and orbicularis in front, in the soft tissues just beyond the tarsal plate's medial end. The channels converge at the medial canthus, crossing in front of the sac, the upper above the medial palpebral ligament and the lower below it. In about ninety percent of people they unite into a common canaliculus of one to two millimetres, which pierces the lacrimal fascia obliquely and opens into the lateral wall of the sac at the common internal punctum, two to three millimetres below the fundus, guarded by the valve of Rosenmuller. Narrow, elastic, sleeved in muscle, and superficial enough to be injured, deep enough to be forgotten. That is the canaliculus.
