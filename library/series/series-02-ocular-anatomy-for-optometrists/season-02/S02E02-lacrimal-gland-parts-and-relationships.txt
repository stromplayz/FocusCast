# Description: How does one lacrimal gland become two working parts? In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we split the main lacrimal gland into its orbital lobe and palpebral lobe and follow the levator aponeurosis, the flat tendon of the eyelid lifter that divides them. We trace the ten to twelve lacrimal ducts as they travel with the aponeurosis to open in a row in the superior conjunctival fornix, and map the gland's relations to the lacrimal fossa of the frontal bone, the orbital septum and the upper eyelid. A clear anatomy listen for optometry students and clinicians who want to understand what they see when they evert a lid.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to our second season, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, where this season we are taking the lacrimal system apart, one structure at a time. Last week, in episode one, we met the main lacrimal gland, the almond shaped tear factory tucked into the roof of the orbit, and we heard how its secretion is switched on. Today we go one level deeper, because the gland is not one simple blob. It arrives in two parts, threaded onto a moving tendon, with about a dozen tiny exit doors. Shall we open it up?

Guide: Shall we indeed. Here is the shape of today's walk. Back in episode one, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy, we located the gland in its shallow bowl of bone, gave it its size of roughly twenty millimetres across, described its lobular, grape cluster structure, and stopped just before asking how the gland is internally arranged. Today is that next step. The gland divides into an orbital lobe and a palpebral lobe, a larger part above and a smaller part below, and the dividing wall between them is not gland tissue at all. It is the aponeurosis of the levator palpebrae superioris, the flat tendon of the muscle that lifts your upper eyelid, a sheet that slides a little with every blink. Then there are the ducts, ten to twelve of them, and they do something surprising. They do not leave the gland wherever they like. They travel together with that tendon and open in one tidy row into the pocket called the superior fornix. Around all of this sit neighbors worth knowing: the frontal bone above, the eyelid in front, the conjunctival fornix below. That is the territory, and it explains a remarkable amount of what you see in the chair.

Host: You said the dividing wall is not gland tissue but a tendon. Before we tour the lobes, remind me what that tendon actually is. You called it an aponeurosis, which is a word I want handled properly, in plain language, because I suspect the whole geography of today's episode depends on it.

Guide: An aponeurosis is simply a tendon flattened into a broad sheet. Instead of a cord, like the cable running down your forearm to a finger, picture a wide ribbon of dense, glistening, white fibrous tissue. The levator palpebrae superioris, the lid lifter, is a slim muscle that arises high in the orbit, from the lesser wing of the sphenoid bone, and runs forward just under the roof toward the upper eyelid, where it does not end in a cord. It ends in this sheet, the levator aponeurosis. The sheet fans out sideways and downward, passes behind the orbital septum, the thin fibrous fence that separates the eyelid from the deeper orbit, and attaches along the front of the tarsal plate, the stiff little frame inside the lid, while other fibres slip forward into the skin to create the upper lid crease. Now put the lacrimal gland back into the picture, sitting in the superolateral corner of the orbit, and imagine this fanning sheet arriving and cutting horizontally between the gland and the eyelid. The portion of gland above and behind the sheet becomes the orbital lobe. The portion below and in front becomes the palpebral lobe. One gland, one moving shelf passing clean through it, two lobes. And because the sheet moves every time you blink, both lobes live on either side of something in constant, gentle motion.

Host: Two lobes, then, separated by a moving sheet. Give me the sense of proportion. If this were farmland divided by a road, how does the land split, and is the road a clean division all the way along, or do the two properties share a boundary fence somewhere out of sight, where workers and animals cross between them?

Guide: The split is roughly two thirds and one third. The orbital lobe is the big estate, about two thirds of the gland, and it is the part that actually sits inside the bony fossa. The palpebral lobe is the smaller remainder, about one third, and it lives down inside the upper eyelid. And they are not fully separate islands. Around the lateral end of the aponeurosis, a bridge of glandular tissue connects the two lobes, so their plumbing and their architecture stay in continuity. That little bridge matters in surgery, because it is why you cannot lift one lobe without the other coming along. Functionally the two lobes are the same tissue, serous acini arranged in lobules, the microscopic berry shaped secretion units that made the grape cluster picture we painted in episode one. Serous simply means they make the watery part of tears, and where that water fits among the oil from the lid margin glands and the mucus of the surface is a story we deliberately saved for episode four. What differs between the lobes is position, and position changes everything.

Host: Then take me to the orbital lobe first, the big one. Paint the scene. Where exactly does it sit, what is it leaning against above, and what is it brushing underneath as the eye moves?

Guide: The orbital lobe occupies the lacrimal fossa, the shallow depression we described last week, pressed into the orbital plate of the frontal bone just inside the superolateral rim of the orbit. Its upper surface is convex, moulded to fit that bony bowl the way a stone seats itself in clay. Its under surface is concave, because below it lies the eye, and the gland drapes gently over the globe's upper outer curve. Medially it meets the levator palpebrae superioris and, just beneath that muscle, the superior rectus, so the lid lifter and the eye's main elevator are its medial neighbours. Inferolaterally, down toward the outer corner, the lateral rectus and its fascial check ligament slide past. Anteriorly, its face is pressed against the orbital septum, which is why gland trouble announces itself in the lid rather than deep in the socket. Behind, the gland's posterior border is free of bone, cushioned in orbital fat. And wrapping the whole gland is a thin connective tissue capsule, which sends delicate septa inward between the lobules, keeping the grape clusters neatly packed and giving a surgeon a plane by which to lift the gland from its fossa. One more relation clinicians should carry: the orbital lobe is not visible when you evert the upper lid. It is too high and too far back, above the aponeurosis and behind the septum. Whatever you can see on lid eversion is, by definition, not the orbital lobe.

Host: Which brings us neatly to the palpebral lobe, the one third that actually lives in the eyelid. Describe its position precisely. Above what does it hang, below what is it tethered, and what would I actually see if I flipped a patient's upper lid in my chair tomorrow morning?

Guide: The palpebral lobe sits inside the upper eyelid, in the space between the under surface of the levator aponeurosis above and the superior conjunctival fornix below. The fornix, if we define it plainly, is the pocket where the conjunctiva, the thin transparent lining we first met all the way back in the Series 1 episode called The Front of the Eye, folds back on itself to become the lining over the globe. So the palpebral lobe is the last structure before that fold. Evert the upper lid and, in many patients, you can see it: a soft pink, faintly lobulated bulge of gland shining through the conjunctiva, most obvious in the outer part of the lid, and best displayed when the patient looks down and toward the nose, which rolls the lobe into view. In some people it is prominent, in others barely visible, and in older patients it may prolapse, sliding forward over the lid edge as a fleshy pink mass. That visibility is the palpebral lobe's signature. It is the only part of the main gland an optometrist can look at directly, with no instrument at all.

Host: Now the ducts. Last week you promised that tears leave through ducts, and today you have been hinting that the geography of those ducts is stranger than people assume. Walk me through it. How do tears actually get out of this two lobed gland and onto the eye?

Guide: Here is the beautiful, slightly alarming design. The gland carries roughly ten to twelve ducts, and the ones arising from the orbital lobe, the big upper lobe, cannot simply open straight onto the eye, because the aponeurosis and the palpebral lobe lie across their path. So they run downward, hugging the underside of the sheet, travelling with the levator aponeurosis, and they pass straight through the substance of the palpebral lobe, collecting its own smaller ducts on the way. Every channel, from both lobes, therefore funnels down through the lower lobe and opens together into the lateral part of the superior conjunctival fornix, in a neat row, roughly four to five millimetres above the upper border of the tarsal plate. Read that arrangement like a building. The orbital lobe is the factory on the upper floor. The palpebral lobe is the ground floor lobby, and every exit door opens through the lobby. Block or cut the lobby and the factory above becomes irrelevant. That single sentence carries more clinical weight than it seems to, and when we reach the drainage side of this system in episodes six and beyond, you will hear the same doorway logic repeated in a different place. One more detail, for contrast later: these ducts run wide open, with no valves along them, which is the exact opposite of the drainage system downstream, where nature installs one way doors at nearly every level.

Host: Let me say it back to make sure I have it. All of the doors open through the small lobe. So the palpebral lobe is not the junior partner at all. What happens if the small lobe or its ducts are damaged, while the big orbital lobe above is perfectly healthy?

Guide: The eye still dries, and that is the classic lesson. Think of ptosis surgery, where a surgeon shortens or reattaches that very aponeurosis to lift a drooping lid, or an upper lid blepharoplasty that trims redundant tissue nearby. Dissect above the aponeurosis carelessly and you can sever the ducts exactly where they cluster along and through the palpebral lobe. The orbital lobe keeps making tears, but the deliveries are cut, and the patient can present with a strikingly dry eye after what looked like a routine lid procedure. It is one more reason a dry, grumbling surface after lid surgery is a real, anatomically explainable event, not bad luck. The corollary is just as useful. If the orbital lobe is ever removed, as occasionally happens in disease, the palpebral lobe with its intact ducts can still deliver basal tears, helped along by the accessory glands we will meet next week in episode three, Accessory Lacrimal Glands. In other words, protect the lower third and its exits and the surface usually stays comfortable. Lose the doorway, and the factory upstairs does not matter at all.

Host: I want to walk around the whole gland now, like a compass survey, because you keep dropping the names of neighbours: septum, muscles, bone, fat. Start wherever you like, but take me face by face around the gland, so the map in my head has every direction filled in before we talk clinic.

Guide: Let us use the orbital lobe as our reference, since it sits deepest. Above it: the frontal bone and its lacrimal fossa. In front of it: the orbital septum, and beyond the septum the soft tissues of the upper lid, which is why gland swelling shows in the outer upper lid before anything else. Behind it: orbital fat, and arriving through that fat its two visitors, the lacrimal artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery, and the lacrimal nerve, a sensory branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, both entering at the posterior border of the gland. Medially: the levator palpebrae superioris, with the superior rectus just beneath it. Below and lateral: the lateral rectus and its check ligament. The palpebral lobe, one floor down, keeps a shorter list: aponeurosis above, conjunctiva and fornix below, septum and lid tissue in front. And laterally, the whole gland relates to the frontozygomatic suture, the bony seam where the frontal and zygomatic bones meet at the rim. Every direction on that compass gets tested in the clinic sooner or later, as you are about to hear. One quiet note for completeness: lymph from the gland finds its way forward to the preauricular nodes, the small filters sitting in front of the ear, a fact that will explain a small sign in our clinic talk shortly.

Host: That suture you just named, the seam between frontal and zygomatic bone, sits near the gland at the rim. Tell me more about the fossa itself, the bowl the gland lives in. What exactly forms it, how snug is the fit, and what happens out there when the gland inside it swells?

Guide: The lacrimal fossa is a shallow depression, a gentle flattening of the normal curve, on the orbital surface of the frontal bone, just inside the superolateral orbital rim and sitting above the frontozygomatic suture. It is not a deep cave. It is a smooth, hollowed area barely wider than the gland itself, which is why the gland fits snugly and why enlargement is felt and seen early. Press gently just beneath the outer third of the upper rim and you are palpating over this fossa. When the gland inflames or grows, three things happen that every optometrist should recognise. The outer upper lid swells, with a characteristic droop over its outer third. The lid contour can take on an S shape, the classic sigmoid deformity of acute dacryoadenitis, which is simply inflammation of the lacrimal gland, because the swollen gland displaces the lid outline downward and outward. And a mass can become palpable beneath the rim, firm and sitting up against the bone. That S shaped lid is one of those findings that is pure geography. You are looking at the silhouette of a gland that has run out of room in its bowl.

Host: You mentioned the fornix as the pocket under the palpebral lobe, and the ducts opening there. Slow down on that junction, because it is the exact meeting point of gland and eye surface. Describe the superior fornix and that row of openings as if I were looking at them.

Guide: Picture the conjunctiva as one continuous sheet lining two surfaces, the inner eyelid and the front of the globe. Where the lid lining turns back to become globe lining, high up under the lid, it forms a deep, loose recess: the superior fornix, a vaulted cul-de-sac that holds the tear reservoir and lets the lid glide freely. The palpebral lobe hangs just above this vault, inside the lid. And along the outer part of the vault, roughly four to five millimetres above the tarsal border, the ten to twelve duct openings appear as a row of tiny, barely visible points, running from the outer corner toward the nose. You would need magnification to see the openings themselves, but you can see their effect every second. Every blink sweeps part of that reservoir across the surface, but what the tear film itself is made of, its layers and its turnover, is a story we deliberately saved for episode four, Tear Film Anatomy, and the sac as a whole belongs to episode five, Conjunctival Sac. For today, hold only the geometry. Gland above, fornix below, and the ducts as the bridge between them.

Host: Now a mechanical question I have been saving since the start. The aponeurosis moves with every blink, thousands of times a day, and it passes right between the two lobes. Does the gland simply sit still while a tendon slides across it? What holds this whole assembly in place?

Guide: Partly it does sit still, and partly it is restrained by a proper suspension system. Strands of fascia run from the gland's capsule to the periosteum, the bone's living lining, anchoring it in the fossa. Below and in front, it is supported by the region of Whitnall's ligament, also called the superior transverse ligament, a fibrous band formed where the sheath of the levator thickens, roughly ten millimetres above the superior tarsal border. The check ligament of the lateral rectus and the surrounding intermuscular septum add support from the outer side. Together these suspenders hold the gland up in its bowl while the aponeurosis glides across it. With age, or after repeated episodes of swelling, those supports can slacken, and the palpebral lobe prolapses, sliding down and forward into view as that pink mass we described. There is also a subtler consequence of the arrangement. Because the ducts travel with the aponeurosis, each blink tugs gently at the region of the duct openings in the fornix, and the sheet sweeping down passes over the gland's exit row. Compact, but nothing in the design is accidental.

Host: You mentioned prolapse a moment ago, and it makes me wonder about time. This assembly of lobes, sheet and suspenders has to last a working lifetime. Does the gland itself change as the patient in your chair gets older?

Guide: It does, and the changes are structural, which is why they belong in today's episode. Through adult life the whole gland weighs a little under a gram, about the weight of a paperclip, small enough that the fossa barely needs to indent the bone. With the passing decades the microscopic picture shifts: acini, those berry shaped units, become fewer and smaller, and fibrous and fatty tissue creep in among the lobules, so an older gland is partly a scaffold with fewer factories on it. That slow loss of working tissue is one structural piece of the puzzle in older, drier eyes, though how the surface tells that story is episode four's territory. The suspension system slackens too, which is why the palpebral lobe prolapse we described belongs mostly to older faces. And the fascial strands anchoring the capsule share the same aging, so the gland settles slightly differently in its fossa with the years. Nothing dramatic. Just a reminder that anatomy has a clock, and the examiner who knows the clock reads older eyes more kindly.

Host: You named the lacrimal artery and the lacrimal nerve entering behind the gland. For completeness, in one pass: what do I need to remember about the gland's supply lines, without re-filming last week's episode on how secretion is switched on?

Guide: Keep it to three lines. First, arterial supply arrives from the lacrimal artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery, which reaches the gland's posterior border with the nerve and sends small branches inward among the lobules. Second, venous blood leaves posteriorly into the superior ophthalmic vein. Third, the lacrimal nerve carries general sensation from the gland and the outer upper lid, while the switching of secretion, the parasympathetic wiring that turns the tap on for reflex tearing, was last week's story in episode one, so we leave it on the shelf. The only point to add today is positional. Those supply lines arrive from behind, which means a mass expanding within the gland stretches them early, and a deep, unexplained ache in the outer upper lid can be the very first hint that something in the fossa is growing. Position converts anatomy into symptoms.

Host: Let us move from the map to the chair. Tomorrow morning I evert an upper lid. Walk me through normal first, then abnormal. What does the healthy palpebral lobe look like through conjunctiva, and which findings should make me stop, put down the lens, and think?

Guide: Normal is quiet. Pink or pale salmon, smooth, faintly lobulated, sometimes visible as a gentle bulge in the outer part of the lid, especially when the patient looks down and in. Some patients show almost nothing, and that is normal too. Abnormal arrives in recognisable flavours. Acute dacryoadenitis: tender, warm swelling of the outer upper lid, the S shaped contour we described, injected conjunctiva overlying an enlarged gland, often with a feverish, unwell patient behind it, and often a tender preauricular node in front of the ear, the classic viral pattern, mumps being the textbook case. Chronic enlargement: painless, firm, slow, the gland quietly taking up space, as in the granulomatous inflammations such as sarcoidosis. And tumours, which have a geographic signature. A slow, firm, painless mass arising in the orbital lobe, the classic benign pleomorphic adenoma, expands inside the fossa and pushes the globe forward, downward and inward, because that is the only direction with room to spare: down and toward the nose. So when a patient's eye is displaced inferonasally and you find no ordinary explanation, let your mind walk straight to the lacrimal fossa. The position of the globe is a clue to the lobe hiding behind it.

Host: That displacement logic is exactly what anatomy is for. One more neighbouring topic before we close: lid surgery. Why does this gland keep showing up in discussions of ptosis repair and cosmetic lid work? What is the trap for the surgeon?

Guide: The trap is mistaken identity and shared walls. In a ptosis repair the surgeon works directly on the levator aponeurosis, which is the very sheet passing between the lobes and carrying the ducts, so the gland and its plumbing are inside the operating field. In cosmetic upper lid surgery, tissue near the superotemporal orbit is handled, and a normal or prolapsed palpebral lobe can be mistaken for orbital fat and trimmed or injured, with exactly the dry eye consequences we discussed. And in a prolapse repair, the operation is literally about the suspension system we described, repositioning the lobe and reinforcing its supports. The gland also explains why swelling after lid procedures prefers the outer upper lid, pooling where the gland and its fossa sit. None of this makes lid surgery dangerous. It makes anatomy the difference between a tidy result and a patient who returns blinking uncomfortably through a dry, unhappy surface.

Host: Time for the takeaways, as we always do. If a listener keeps only three things from today, from this tour of two lobes, one tendon and a dozen doors, what three things should those be?

Guide: Three things. One: the gland is two lobes built around a moving tendon. The orbital lobe, about two thirds, hides in the frontal bone's lacrimal fossa above the levator aponeurosis. The palpebral lobe, about one third, hangs below it inside the lid, where you can actually see it on eversion. Two: all ten to twelve ducts travel with the aponeurosis and funnel through the palpebral lobe to open in a row in the superior fornix, roughly four to five millimetres above the tarsal border. The small lobe is the doorway, and doorway damage dries the eye even when the factory above is healthy. Three: the outer upper lid and the position of the globe carry the messages. An S shaped lid with tenderness points to acute inflammation. A firm, painless mass pushing the eye down and in points to the orbital lobe in its fossa. Geography, read through an everted lid and a careful glance at where the eye is pointing.
