# Description: On the inner third of every eyelid margin sits a dark pinhead most patients never notice, and almost no tear escapes the eye without passing through it. In this anatomy episode of the Lacrimal System season, Focus Cast by focuslinks opens up the puncta: the lacrimal papilla each one crowns, its position about six millimetres from the medial canthus, its posterior-facing opening, its fraction-of-a-millimetre calibre, and the ampulla waiting just below. Learn why the tiniest doorway in the whole drainage pathway can drain a full day of tears, how outward gaze aligns the upper and lower puncta, and how this anatomy underpins dilation, plug fitting, and the watering-eye story. Made for optometry students, optics retail teams, and curious listeners who want the eye built piece by piece.

Host: Here is a small experiment you can run on yourself in the next five seconds. Pull your lower lid gently down and outward, tip your chin up, and peer into a mirror at the inner corner of the exposed margin. Just before the margin turns that soft rounded corner toward your nose, you will see a minute pink mound, and on its summit a dark pinhead of an opening. That tiny dot is the lacrimal punctum, and it is where today's episode lives. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one anatomical structure at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series, and we are deep in the Lacrimal System season, at episode six. The season has already built the tears; today we stand at the mouth of the drain. By the end of this episode you will know what a punctum actually is, where it sits and why it sits there, how wide it is, which way it faces, and what waits just below it. Walk us in.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back. Let me place us on the season map first, because position matters in anatomy as much as it does in geography. In episodes one, two and three we covered the secretion side of the lacrimal system: the main lacrimal gland in its fossa, its orbital and palpebral lobes, and the accessory glands of Krause and Wolfring that quietly top up the tears between blinks. Episode four, Tear Film Anatomy, took the product itself apart, layer by layer. Last time, in episode five, the Conjunctival Sac, we saw where all that tear volume collects: a thin reservoir wrapped around the front of the eye, deepest in a small lake tucked into the inner corner. Today we reach the threshold of the drainage half of the season. My brief is deliberately narrow and I will keep it: the punctum itself, the little papilla it crowns, its position and its diameter, the direction its opening faces, and the small chamber called the ampulla that lies just beneath. The long tube below that belongs to episode seven, the sac to episode eight, the duct to episode nine, and the whole pumping mechanism to episode ten.

Host: Before the details, give me the big claim of the day. You called this a doorway. Why does such a humble dot deserve a whole episode of anatomy?

Guide: Because nearly every tear the eye makes has to leave through it, and it is the narrowest point of the entire drainage pathway. Think about what you learned back in Series 1, in an episode called The Front of the Eye: the cornea and the tear film presented as an optical polish, the surface that makes vision possible. That polish is being made all day, and it is constantly refreshed, and the surplus has to go somewhere. The gland can be perfect, the tear film balanced, the reservoir full, and if this doorway fails in its position, in its calibre or in its patency, the patient sits in your chair with tears running down their cheek. One square millimetre of anatomy decides whether a person spends the day quietly crying. That is why it earns the hour.

Host: Then let us find it. Walk me to the lid margin and point. What am I actually looking at when you say the word papilla?

Guide: Start with the margin itself. Back in our first season, the orbit and adnexa season, we devoted an episode called Eyelid Layers to taking the lid apart, so you will remember that the margin is not the same along its whole length. Its long outer stretch carries the lashes, and just behind the lash line sit the openings of the meibomian glands, the lid's oil glands. Its short inner stretch, the last few millimetres before the corner of the eye, is rounded, smooth and bald, with no lashes at all. The lacrimal papilla sits exactly at the junction of those two stretches. It is a small mound of tissue, a fraction of a millimetre high, slightly pinker than the pale strip of margin around it, and completely smooth because it carries no lashes and no glands. The word papilla is simply Latin for a tiny nipple, and anatomy uses it everywhere for little bumps of tissue. On the very summit of that mound sits the opening we came for, the punctum, from a root meaning a point. So: punctum on papilla, a point on a mound. There is one on each lid.

Host: Two of them per eye, then. Where exactly along the margin, and how far from the inner corner?

Guide: On the lower lid, the punctum lies roughly six millimetres from the medial canthus, which is the anatomical name for the inner corner where the upper and lower lids meet at the nose. Measure along the margin from that corner in an adult and you will find the lower punctum about six to six and a half millimetres out. The upper punctum sits close to the same spot, usually a millimetre or so nearer the corner. Those numbers sound academic until you meet their clinical shadows. The short stretch of margin between the canthus and the punctum is the most mobile part of the whole lid, and it is exactly where lid lacerations and lid surgery tend to happen, which is why the punctum's precise address matters when that region is injured. One more positional fact to store away. The punctum sits on the part of the margin that rests in gentle, constant contact with the globe, not on the part that hangs free like the outer stretch. That contact detail is the key to its orientation, and orientation is where we go next.

Host: Orientation. I pulled my lid down and I still needed good light and a steady hand to find the thing. Why is this opening so shy?

Guide: Because the opening does not face you. It faces backward. The punctum is turned toward the globe, opening against the surface of the eye, which means that in the primary position, with the patient looking straight ahead at you, the opening is hidden against the eye itself. Think about why the design makes sense. The lid margin glides over the globe all day, wet with every blink. An opening that faced outward, toward the examiner, would face dry air and would never touch the tear film. An opening that faces backward lives pressed against a permanently wet surface, bathed in the strip of tears that travels with the lid margin. That is the whole trick. To see the lower punctum you pull the lid down and slightly outward and ask the patient to look up, which stretches the margin, lifts the papilla away from the globe and rotates the opening into view. For the upper punctum you do the mirror image: pull the lid up and out and ask the patient to look down. Every clinician performs this move dozens of times a day without pausing on the anatomy that forces it.

Host: And the eye's own position changes the picture too. I have watched experienced colleagues swing the patient's gaze sideways before doing anything else. What are they using?

Guide: A neat piece of applied geometry. With the eye looking straight ahead, the upper punctum sits slightly nasal to the lower one, a touch closer to the nose along its own margin. Ask the patient to look hard out toward the temple, though, and the globe rotates in the socket, carrying the lid surfaces around with it, and the two puncta come to lie one directly above the other. The two doors are now vertically aligned, like two manhole covers in the same street. That single manoeuvre is why upper lid plug placement, and irrigation, and probing, are all done with the patient gazing outward. You line up the target first. It also explains something you may have noticed on slit lamp photographs taken in primary gaze: the puncta look mismatched, offset from one another, and there is nothing wrong. Offset is the design. Alignment is the manoeuvre.

Host: Now the number everyone quotes at each other in clinic. How wide is a punctum?

Guide: The visible opening is tiny. The standard textbook figure is about zero point two to zero point three millimetres across, so roughly a third of a millimetre, narrower than the full stop at the end of a printed sentence. And here is the claim I most want you to carry out of today: that makes the punctum the narrowest point of the whole drainage pathway. Everything downstream is wider. The tissue around the opening is not soft skin, either. Ringing the punctum is a dense cuff of elastic and fibrous tissue, and elastic is the operative word. Stretch it gently with a tapered instrument and a punctum that measures a third of a millimetre will admit a probe approaching a full millimetre, then spring back to its own size when you let go. The papilla behaves the same way, because the elastic cuff runs right through it. That combination, a minute natural calibre plus generous stretch, is precisely what makes dilation possible: you are borrowing the tissue's elasticity, not tearing anything. It also explains the sad alternative. When years of chronic inflammation or scarring replace that elastic cuff with stiff fibrous tissue, the opening can narrow permanently, because fibrous tissue neither stretches nor recoils.

Host: Which raises the question I have been saving. The tear film is a few microns deep, the gland makes its steady trickle, and this door is a third of a millimetre. How can a pinhole drain the whole eye?

Guide: Two mechanisms carry the load, and both of them love a narrow tube. The first is capillarity, the same physics that pulls water up a thin glass tube against gravity. The narrower the channel, the stronger the pull, so a third of a millimetre opening is not a bottleneck; it is a wick. The tear film wets the rim of the opening, fluid creeps in, and the channel draws it downward into the chamber waiting below. The second mechanism is the pump, the rhythmic squeeze the whole drainage system receives with every blink, and that machinery, including the muscle fibres that do the squeezing, is the finale of this season in episode ten. What matters today is the geometry the pump will work through. In episode four we learned how briskly the tear film turns over, and in episode five we saw the surplus gathering in the inner lake. Two openings far too small to look sufficient accept that flow all day, and the system keeps pace from morning to night. In drainage anatomy, small is not a weakness. Small is the design.

Host: So something is waiting just under the dot. Take me below the surface. What is under there?

Guide: Below the punctum, the channel does something clever: it plunges. From the opening, the passage runs vertically, straight down into the lid tissue, for about two millimetres, and near the bottom of that drop it widens slightly into a tiny spindle-shaped chamber called the ampulla. Ampulla is anatomy's word for a small flask-like widening along a tube; the term turns up in several organs, and it always means the same shape. This one is barely a couple of millimetres deep. It is lined by smooth epithelium continuous with the surface lining, it is wrapped in that same generous elastic tissue, and it has one job: it is the holding chamber where tears gather between drains. When a clinician irrigates, the tip of the cannula enters vertically for about two millimetres and rests in the region of this chamber. When a punctal plug is fitted, its wider collar sits on the punctal ring and its shaft hangs down into the vertical channel and the ampulla. And when tears enter the system, capillarity and blink pressure move them into this little reservoir first. Everything beyond the ampulla, the sharp turn backward, the long horizontal run of roughly eight millimetres, and the first one-way valve of the system, the valve of Rosenmuller, belongs to the tube anatomists call the canaliculus, and that is episode seven's territory, in an episode called Canaliculi.

Host: You keep promising muscle fibres for episode ten. Is there any muscle doing anything around the punctum itself?

Guide: There is, and it belongs to a muscle we met back in the adnexa season: the orbicularis oculi, the sphincter that closes the lids. Some of its fibres are threaded right around the papilla and the upper reaches of the channel, a small dedicated sling, and deeper fibres from the portion of the muscle that runs behind the eye, sometimes called Horner's muscle after its describer, wrap the lower parts further down. Two consequences follow. First, the sling holds the papilla and its opening firmly against the globe, keeping the posterior-facing design honest all day long. Second, with every blink those fibres contract and relax, and the system below is squeezed and released. That is the pump I keep deferring to episode ten, so today I will only insist on this: the punctum is not a passive hole punched in a sheet. It is a mobile, muscular, elastic mouth that moves with every blink and repositions itself with every gaze. The dot you see in the mirror is alive.

Host: Now the variants. Does this doorway always form correctly, and what does it look like when it does not?

Guide: The punctum is the finishing line of a canalization process. In embryonic development the drainage pathway begins as a solid cord of cells that must hollow out into an open channel, and the punctum is the last doorway to open, right at the surface. That sequence explains the commonest congenital variant, the imperforate punctum, where the channel below has formed properly but a thin membrane still covers the surface opening, like a window painted shut. The papilla looks perfectly normal, there may be a dimple where the point should be, and nothing enters. It is missed more often than it should be, and it is usually discovered when a watering eye brings the patient in. Rarer still, a punctum can be genuinely absent from birth, and occasionally a papilla carries two openings, a supernumerary punctum, meaning an extra one, with one opening patent and the other blind. On the acquired side, the two big anatomical stories are stenosis, a narrowing of the opening and its elastic cuff, which creeps in with age and with years of chronic lid margin disease, and certain chemotherapy drugs are notorious for it; and changes of position, where a lax, outward-turning margin swings the opening away from the lake it is meant to sip from. Hold that last one, because it is the best example of position beating calibre.

Host: Then let us do exactly that. Take the watering eye, the patient with tears spilling onto the cheek. From the punctum's point of view, what are the ways this doorway fails?

Guide: The word for the overflow itself is epiphora, tears that spill over the lid edge onto the face instead of leaving by the drain. From the doorstep of the system, the anatomical failures sort into three groups. Group one is position. If the lid margin rolls outward, an ectropion, or simply becomes too lax with age, the punctum is no longer pressed against the globe and no longer sits in the tear lake. An opening that points at dry air cannot sip water, no matter how wide and healthy the tube behind it. Group two is calibre. The opening is stenosed, narrowed by scarring, chronic inflammation or medication, and a wick one tenth of its normal size simply cannot keep up. Group three is patency beyond the door: a blockage further downstream, in the canaliculus, the sac or the duct, and those addresses belong to episodes seven, eight and nine. Notice the clinical beauty of this doorstep. Irrigation, the standard first test of the drainage route, uses the punctum's own geometry, entering vertically about two millimetres toward the ampulla before turning horizontally. What the fluid does next helps sort group three from the other two. The doorway you have spent today studying is also the doorway you test through.

Host: Which brings us to plugs. Dry eye patients get little plugs popped into these openings all the time. Walk me through the anatomy of the fit.

Guide: A punctal plug is a deliberate, reversible blockage of the door. The logic rests on a redundancy we have not yet named out loud: there are two puncta per eye, four in a person, and the system tolerates one closed door, because the remaining door can handle the full flow. Block the door and the tears the eye already makes stay on the surface longer, which is exactly the goal when the problem is dryness rather than overflow. The anatomy of the fit is elegant. Plugs are sized in tenths of a millimetre. The chosen plug has a wider collar that rests on the punctal ring and the papilla, like the top hat of a bolt resting on its washer, and a narrower shaft that sits in the vertical channel and the ampulla we mapped a moment ago. Because the tissues stretch elastically, the same opening that passes a third of a millimetre of tears accepts a shaft several tenths of a millimetre wide. To seat a plug in the upper lid, the clinician uses our gaze trick, aligning the puncta on outward gaze to reach the papilla comfortably. A plug that is too large irritates the papilla; one that is too small migrates down the channel. The geometry has to be right, which is why the papilla is assessed, never guessed.

Host: And the lower door gets plugged first, I remember that from watching clinic. Is that where the seventy percent figure comes in?

Guide: Yes, and it is a figure worth saying out loud. Of the tears that leave each eye, the larger share, often quoted at around seventy percent, exits through the lower punctum. The reasons are anatomical. The lower lid is the shelf of the eye: it holds the bulk of the tear lake, and we saw in episode five that the meniscus, the crescent of tears riding along the lower margin, is the deepest strip of the reservoir. The lower punctum sits in the deepest part of the lake, sipping from the fullest water, and gravity lends its modest hand. The upper punctum works as the partner door and as the backup. Close it, and the lower route still drains; narrow the lower one, and the upper route compensates. That redundancy is the anatomical reason punctal occlusion is tolerable at all, and it is why the bigger drain is blocked first, gently, and then reassessed. One door, then the other, never both on the same day. The anatomy gave the therapy its staging.

Host: Let me try the slit lamp checklist. A student has the patient at the microscope. What should they consciously note about each punctum?

Guide: Four things, and all four are pure anatomy. First, position. Is the papilla where it belongs on the medial third of the margin, and is the punctum apposed to the globe and sitting in the tear lake, or has a lax lid let it roll outward? Second, visibility and shape. After a gentle pull of the lid, is the opening a neat round dot, or a slit, or a keyhole, or invisible behind a membrane? Third, the papilla itself. Chronic irritation from misdirected lashes, dry-eye inflammation or long hours of contact lens wear can enlarge the mound and sometimes gape the opening, so the papilla acts as a barometer of what the margin has endured for years. Fourth, symmetry. Compare like with like between the two eyes before deciding anything is abnormal. While you are there, note the neighbours, because the inner corner is crowded. The caruncle, the small pink nodule of skin-like tissue at the very corner, and the plica semilunaris, the crescent-shaped fold just lateral to it, form the inner wall of the tear lake our puncta sip from. The conjunctiva gets its own full season next; for today, simply see the puncta in their neighbourhood rather than in isolation.

Host: And fluorescein? Students watch dye disappear all the time. Is that test standing on this anatomy too?

Guide: Completely. Fluorescein is a harmless orange dye that glows green under blue light, and once it is instilled into the tear film, one of the simplest observations in eye care is simply to watch how long it takes to disappear. The dye leaves the eye the way the tears do, and the exits are our two puncta. Dye that lingers suggests the doorway or the road beyond is slow. Dye that vanishes briskly tells you the door, and at least the early stretches of the road, are open. The test cannot tell you exactly where a blockage sits; it only reports whether the exit is behaving, and pinpointing the level is precisely what irrigation and probing add. But the cheapest, gentlest drainage test in the whole toolkit is built directly on the doorway we have spent this episode inside. Anatomy first, instrument second.

Host: Recap time. Give me the words and the numbers once more, slowly, the way I will want them in my head.

Guide: Here is the episode on one page. Two lacrimal puncta per eye, one on each lid, each crowning a lacrimal papilla: a smooth, bald, slightly pink mound at the junction of the lash-bearing and bald stretches of the margin. The lower punctum lies about six to six and a half millimetres from the medial canthus, the inner corner of the eye, with the upper one a touch closer to the corner. The opening itself measures about zero point two to zero point three millimetres, the narrowest point of the entire drainage system, and it faces backward, pressed against the globe and bathed in the tear film, which is why we pull and tilt lids to see it. In primary gaze the upper punctum sits slightly nasal to the lower; on outward gaze the two align, the trick used for plugs, irrigation and probing. Around the opening runs an elastic cuff that will stretch toward a millimetre and spring back. Below the punctum the channel drops about two millimetres and widens into the ampulla, the little holding chamber where the downward journey truly begins. And the lower door takes the larger share of the outflow, often quoted around seventy percent. That is the doorstep of the lacrimal drainage system.

Host: And that doorstep is where the season changes direction. Six episodes on how tears are made and kept; from here, we follow them out.

Guide: Next time the journey continues, and the episode is called Canaliculi. From the ampulla, our channel makes a sharp right angle and runs roughly eight millimetres backward through the lid toward the inner corner, as the vertical portion becomes the horizontal one. We will meet the common internal punctum, where the channels of the two lids merge into one, and the first one-way valve of the system, the valve of Rosenmuller, the little flap that keeps tears and nose-wash from running the wrong way. It is the most surgically vulnerable stretch of the whole drain, and after today at the doorstep it will feel like the natural next room.
