# Description: The nasolacrimal duct is the last corridor a tear travels on its way from the eye to the nose, and in this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks we walk it from end to end. Learn how the bony nasolacrimal canal is built from three bones, why the duct opens into the inferior meatus rather than the middle meatus, and how the valve of Hasner works as a one-way gate. We also map the duct's neighbours in the nose and sinus, explain why a head cold makes eyes water, and why roughly a third of newborns are born with this exit still sealed. A precise, plain-language anatomy session for optometry students and eye care professionals.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one eye care topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, and we are deep in the season on the lacrimal system. Over the past few episodes we have followed a tear from the moment it is made to the moment it leaves the eye surface. Episode six found the puncta, those tiny openings on the lid margin. Episode seven threaded the canaliculi. Last week, in episode eight, we sat inside the lacrimal sac in its bony fossa. Today the tear finally goes underground. Our subject is the nasolacrimal duct, the tube that carries the tear from the bottom of the sac, through bone, into the nose. Welcome back, teacher. Take us down.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome to every listener. Today is a narrow, quiet episode, and I mean that as a compliment. Everything we do fits inside one corridor about the length of your little finger. Here is the plan. First, where the duct begins and how it continues the sac. Second, the bony canal it travels through and the three bones that build that canal. Third, the lining of the tube and the strange venous sponge wrapped around it. Fourth, the opening at the far end, under the inferior turbinate, and the famous fold of mucosa called the valve of Hasner. And finally, the nasal neighbourhood, which explains a surprising amount of everyday clinical life, from crying with a cold to the watery newborn eye.

Host: Let us set the starting point carefully, because last episode ended with the sac sitting in its fossa. Where exactly does the sac stop and the duct begin? Is there a visible border, or is it one smooth tube?

Guide: It is one smooth tube, and the honest answer is that the border is a convention. The lacrimal sac is the dilated upper pouch, roughly twelve to fifteen millimetres tall, tucked into the fossa between the anterior and posterior lacrimal crests, which we mapped in episode eight. Follow the sac downward and it simply leaves the fossa and narrows into the duct. Some textbooks name a small fold of mucosa at this junction, the valve of Krause, as a way of drawing the line, though it is not consistently present, and do not confuse it with the accessory glands of the same name from episode three. Functionally this is one continuous passage. The duct itself measures about fifteen to eighteen millimetres from that transition down to its opening in the nose. So the descent is real and it is measurable, but the parts merge the way a funnel merges into a pipe.

Host: You said the duct travels through bone. That phrase, the bony nasolacrimal canal, is one students meet in every textbook and every examination. Break it down for us. Which bones, and where is this tunnel sitting?

Guide: Picture the wall between the orbit and the nasal cavity. The lacrimal fossa we studied last episode is the top of that wall. Below the fossa, the same wall thickens into a vertical bony tunnel, the nasolacrimal canal, and the duct runs through it like a wire inside a conduit. Three bones contribute. The lateral wall of the canal is a plate of the maxilla, the great bone of the middle face. The medial wall is made of two stacked pieces: the lacrimal bone above, and, as the lacrimal bone ends, the inferior nasal concha below. So maxilla on the outer side, lacrimal bone and inferior concha on the inner side. Notice that the inferior concha is a bone of its own, a separate, delicate, scroll-like bone, unlike the middle and superior conchae, which are parts of the ethmoid. The bony canal is about twelve to thirteen millimetres long, and its walls are thin, often only a millimetre or two, which matters more than it sounds. The canal sits mainly in the frontal process of the maxilla, at the anterior margin of the lateral nasal wall, and where the three bones meet they roof it over completely. It is a true tunnel, not an open groove.

Host: And the direction of travel? If I am a tear standing at the bottom of the sac, which way do I fall?

Guide: You fall downward, but not straight down. The canal leans a little backward and a little outward as it descends, aiming toward the side wall of the nose beneath the front part of the inferior turbinate. That angle is worth memorising, because it is the angle any instrument must follow if it ever has to be passed down the system. It also explains the geography you see in the clinic. When the sac is chronically distended, the swelling appears as a bulge just below and medial to the inner corner of the eye, exactly where the tube lives. And here is the first big nasal relationship, the one that defines this whole episode. On the outer side of the canal is the maxillary sinus, the large air space in the body of the maxilla. On the inner side is the nasal cavity itself. The duct lives in the wall between them, borrowing its outer surface from one and opening into the other.

Host: So a swollen sinus on one side or a congested nose on the other could pinch this tube from either direction. Before we go there, describe the tube itself. What is it lined with, and what actually keeps it open?

Guide: The duct is a membranous tube inside that bony sleeve. Its lining is a mucous membrane continuous above with the lining of the sac and, through the canaliculi, with the conjunctiva we last met in Series 1 in the episode called The Front of the Eye. Below, it becomes the mucosa of the nose, so the same sheet of tissue lines the whole journey. The surface is a double layer of columnar cells resting on a basement membrane, with small mucus glands scattered beneath. And under the epithelium sits the feature students skip and clinicians regret skipping: a rich plexus of small veins, a soft venous sponge in the wall. The lumen is roughly three to four millimetres across when it is distended, but most of the time the walls lie against each other and the lumen is a slit, held barely open by the trickle of tears and kept moist by its own mucus. And because the same membrane runs unbroken from conjunctiva to nasal mucosa, infection can travel the hallway in either direction, which is why a nasal infection can inflame the sac, and why a chronically inflamed sac can keep re-irritating the eye. A collapsed slit is good design, because a closed tube cannot easily backflow.

Host: That venous sponge sounds just like the nasal turbinates themselves, which swell and shrink through the day. Does a head cold really close the tear duct by swelling?

Guide: Exactly that mechanism. The mucosa of the duct is, in origin and in behaviour, nasal mucosa, and it reacts to infection and allergy the way nasal mucosa does. When the nose is inflamed, the venous plexus around the duct engorges, the soft walls swell inward, and a slit lumen becomes a closed slit. Tears keep arriving from above, so they back up, and you get the everyday picture of a person watering at the eyes while blowing the nose. There is a second, upward half to that story which we will reach with the valve of Hasner, but already you can see why spells of watering so often ride along with colds, hay fever, and sinus trouble. The tube did not change its structure that day. It changed its calibre, because its lining swelled.

Host: Here is a design question before we go further. The canaliculi are soft tubes, the sac is a soft pouch, but the duct is locked inside bone. Why give this last stretch a bony sleeve?

Guide: Three reasons, and each one pays off clinically. The first is protection. The duct crosses from the orbit to the nose through one of the busiest, most mobile borders of the face, and bone keeps the corridor open when we rub our eyes, sleep on our faces, or take a knock. The second is guidance. The canal fixes the direction of the drainage so reliably that instruments can be passed along it almost by feel, which we will see shortly. The third is consequence. Because the tube cannot escape its tunnel, anything that narrows the tunnel narrows the flow. Fractures near the inner corner of the orbit, or injuries to the bridge of the nose, can pinch or scar the bony canal and leave a permanently watering eye long after the bruising has faded. When the soft parts above are normal and the watering persists, it is the anatomy that tells you to suspect the tunnel.

Host: Now the end of the road, and I suspect the most examined point in this whole season. Where does the duct actually open, and what is the inferior meatus?

Guide: Start with the lateral wall of the nose. From that wall project three curved shelves of bone covered in thick, vascular mucosa: the nasal conchae, which most people call the turbinates. Each shelf hangs over a corridor, and each corridor is a meatus, which is simply the Latin word for a passage. Under the middle concha is the middle meatus, and that is where the large paranasal sinuses drain, which is precisely why students mix the two up. Notice the irony here: the maxillary sinus, our duct's closest neighbour on the outer side, drains high into the middle meatus, so two structures separated by only millimetres of bone open into the nose at different heights. Under the inferior concha, the lowest and largest shelf, is the inferior meatus. That is our destination. The nasolacrimal duct opens into the anterior part of the inferior meatus, on its lateral wall, about twenty-five to thirty millimetres back from the entrance of the nostril. And apart from a few tiny vessels, the inferior meatus is the quietest corridor in the whole nose. The tear duct is essentially its only tenant.

Host: And that opening is not a clean little porthole, I take it. Which brings us to the valve of Hasner. Tell us what it actually is.

Guide: The valve of Hasner, sometimes called the plica lacrimalis, is a fold of mucous membrane that partially covers the lower end of the duct where it enters the meatus. Think of a soft curtain hanging across a doorway, not a mechanical valve with leaflets. When tears arrive from above and push downward, the fold yields and the tear passes into the nose. When pressure rises from below, when you blow your nose or sneeze, the same fold is pressed against the opening and seals it, keeping air and nasal mucus out of the sac and duct. Without it, every nose blow would inflate your tear drainage system like a balloon. It also has a role an optometrist can appreciate. Place fluorescein in the eye, wait a few minutes, and look into the inferior meatus with a nasal speculum. A green stain on the mucosa there proves the entire route is patent, from punctum to nose, end to end.

Host: You mentioned pressure working in both directions. Sniffing is pressure downward, is it not? Take us through what the nose does to the tear above it.

Guide: Sniffing sharply lowers the pressure inside the nasal cavity, and that negative pressure actively draws air and fluid downward through the drainage system. It is one reason tears clear faster when you sniff than when you dab at them with a tissue, and it is why a dye test can be helped along by asking the patient to sniff gently. Blowing the nose does the opposite. It is a burst of positive pressure, and the Hasner fold is what stands in the way. There is a lovely symmetry here. The venous plexus we described narrows the duct from inside when the nose is congested, reducing drainage downward, while the valve holds the line against pressure from below. The system is built to survive a cold, even if it cannot work well through one. It is also the anatomical reasoning behind the old advice not to pinch the nose and blow hard: a sharp pressure spike, if the valve yields, can drive nasal material back toward the sac.

Host: One more mechanical question while we are inside the tube. How much of the tear's downward journey is simply gravity? Is the duct just a drainpipe?

Guide: Far less gravity than people assume. Remember that the lumen is a collapsed slit, not an open pipe, so water cannot run down it the way it runs through plumbing. The walls are wetted, the tear moves as a thin film, and much of the transport is done actively by the blinking mechanism. That pump is a whole story of its own, and it is the centrepiece of next week's finale, so I will not steal it today. What the duct contributes is a straight, protected, mucosa-lined channel whose calibre is preserved by the bone around it. Gravity assists, capillarity helps, the pump drives, and the valve refuses the reverse.

Host: Now the newborn story, because the watering, sticky baby eye is one of the commonest things a worried parent brings to any eye care front desk. How does this one little fold explain that?

Guide: In the embryo, the whole drainage system begins as a solid cord of cells that must canalize, that is, hollow out into a tube, and this hollowing completes late in fetal life. The cord forms in the groove between the maxillary process and the lateral nasal process of the embryonic face, which is why the finished canal sits precisely along that seam. The last stretch to open is usually the membrane covering Hasner's valve. Around a third of newborns, roughly thirty percent, are born with that membrane still closed. Tear production ramps up in the first weeks of life, tears arrive and cannot exit, and the result is the classic watering and crusting in an otherwise healthy infant. It presents differently from the classic red, irritated eye we unpacked in Series 1 in the episode called Patient With Red Eye, but it arrives at the clinic the same way, as a parent's worry rather than the baby's complaint. Now the encouraging numbers. More than ninety percent of these sealed openings part on their own within the first year, helped along by gentle massage, which is simply hydrostatic pressure pushing the membrane open. And if both ends happen to seal at once, the Rosenmuller fold above and Hasner below, secretions distend the sac into a bluish swelling called a dacryocystocele.

Host: That probing angle you mentioned earlier now makes sense. Is that how the blocked membrane is actually reached, all the way down through the duct?

Guide: Yes, and it is a purely anatomical journey. A fine probe enters the punctum from episode six, runs the vertical then horizontal canaliculus from episode seven, slips through the common internal punctum and the Rosenmuller fold, drops into the sac from episode eight, and then follows the canal's own direction, downward, a little backward, a little outward, until it meets the membrane at Hasner and parts it. The bone guides the way, and the hand simply respects the tunnel. This is done in the first months of life under topical or brief general anaesthesia, and the fact that it can be done largely by feel tells you how constant that bony corridor is. The anatomy is the map, the measurement, and the instrument guide all at once, which is exactly why we spend a whole season learning it in this much detail.

Host: Let me ask the question every patient asks in winter. When we cry, why does the nose run as well as the eyes? Is that the duct at work?

Guide: It is the duct doing exactly its job, simply overwhelmed. Tears are produced far faster than the drainage system's usual rate, the conjunctival sac from episode five overfills, and the excess, instead of spilling over the lid margin onto the cheek straight away, pours down the open sluice into the inferior meatus. From there it mixes with nasal mucus and reappears, quite literally, as a runny nose. The tear you wipe from the eye and the drop the patient sniffles back are often the same water, minutes apart. Emotional tears are also unusually copious, which is why crying produces that unmistakable taste at the back of the throat. It is the anatomy we are studying today, experienced from the inside.

Host: Before we close, give the nasal side a little more attention. What else lives around that opening, and what does the nose do with a tear once it has it?

Guide: Around the opening you have the thick inferior turbinate hanging above, the smooth nasal floor below, and, beyond the thin bony plate on the outer side, the maxillary sinus. Just above and in front of our canal, in the same bony wall, sit small air cells called the agger nasi, the most forward members of the ethmoid air cell family, and when they enlarge they can crowd the duct. A deviated septum, a nasal polyp, or a chronically swollen turbinate can all press on the duct's nasal end and slow the outflow, which is why a watering eye is sometimes cured in the ear, nose and throat clinic rather than the eye clinic. As for repayment, once a tear reaches the meatus it spreads over the nasal floor, mixes with mucus, and is either carried back and swallowed, or evaporates with the outgoing breath. The nose is where the tear's journey actually ends.

Host: And when the duct is damaged beyond swelling and massage, after severe injury, repeated infections, or blockage that will not respond? Surgeons talk about bypassing it. What anatomy do they use?

Guide: The operation is called a dacryocystorhinostomy, usually shortened to DCR, and the name is the anatomy: dacryo for tear, cysto for sac, rhino for nose, stomy for an opening. The surgeon makes a window through the thin bones of the lacrimal fossa, the very maxilla and lacrimal bone we studied in episode eight, so that the sac drains directly into the nasal cavity near the anterior end of the middle turbinate, high in the middle meatus region. And because the new opening sits above Hasner's level, the valve is left behind entirely; the tear never enters the duct at all. The diseased duct is left behind and simply bypassed. In other words, the surgeon abandons the corridor we spent today walking and builds a shortcut through the wall one level higher. It is a beautiful example of why the nasal relationships matter. The whole operation lives in the boundary zone between orbit and nose, and its success is judged by whether fluorescein can finally be found inside the nose where it always belonged.

Host: And when someone wants to actually see this hidden tube before making such decisions, how is it pictured? What do the textbook drawings stand in for?

Guide: Mostly for dacryocystography, an imaging test with a wonderfully literal name. A fine cannula enters the punctum, contrast dye is flushed through the canaliculi into the sac and duct, and an X-ray captures the whole system outlined in white. On such films you can see the pouch of the sac, the duct's downward and outward lean, its narrowings, and exactly where a blockage sits. Modern cross-sectional scanners add views that show the bony canal's walls and the sinus pressing next door. Between such images and careful dissection of donated bodies, every number I have quoted today was measured, not invented. The tube is hidden from the outside, but it has been photographed, measured, and drawn for over a century, and the drawings agree remarkably well.

Host: Most optometrists cannot see into an inferior meatus at the chairside. If we suspect this tube is blocked, what can we actually observe about it during a routine examination?

Guide: You watch the entrance and time the exit. Instil fluorescein into the eye, as we do for pressure checks and corneal assessment anyway, and then simply wait. In a system draining well, the dye has left the tear film within about five minutes and the meniscus runs clear again. If dye lingers and the eye keeps staining green, the exit is slower than the supply, and the duct belongs high on the suspicion list. The logic is the same as watching a sink drain: you do not need to see the pipe to know it is partly plugged. What you cannot tell from the chairside is why, and exactly where. For the complete route, every valve in sequence, and the mechanism that actively drives the tears along, we deliberately wait until next episode.

Host: We have walked the canal, met the valve, and looked at the neighbours. Now give the listener one clean orientation exercise. A slow-motion journey of one tear through today's territory.

Guide: Let us shrink down and ride. We start at the bottom of the lacrimal sac, in the fossa, just below the territory of the medial canthal tendon that we mapped in episode eight. Behind and above us, the common canaliculus opens into the lateral wall, the doorway from episode seven. The walls close gently behind us as we slide into the duct. For twelve or thirteen millimetres we travel through the bony canal: a plate of maxilla pressing from the outer side, lacrimal bone and inferior concha holding the inner side, the whole tube leaning downward, slightly back, slightly out. Through the thin outer wall we can sense the air space of the maxillary sinus; through the inner wall, the moving air of the nasal cavity. Then the bone ends. We glide beneath the soft mucosa of the inferior turbinate for our last five millimetres, and ahead hangs a pale fold, the valve of Hasner. We push it aside, drop into the inferior meatus, land on the nasal floor twenty-five to thirty millimetres from the nostril, and are gone, swallowed within seconds. The tear film of episode four has finally left the eye for good.

Host: How fixed are all these numbers, millimetre by millimetre? If a student finds slightly different figures in another textbook, is that an error?

Guide: Not an error, just variation, and honest anatomy teaches variation. Duct length differs by a few millimetres between people and between the two sides of the same face. The nasal opening can be a slit, a round pore, or occasionally doubled. The Hasner fold can be single, double, or barely present in an adult, which is one reason anatomists argue about whether it deserves the word valve at all. The figures I have given today, fifteen to eighteen millimetres of duct, twelve to thirteen of them in bone, twenty-five to thirty millimetres from the nostril, three to four of lumen, are the standard averages, and they are the right numbers to carry into an examination room. In practice, instruments follow landmarks and resistance, not rulers. The anatomy varies around a stable plan, and the plan is what you must own.
