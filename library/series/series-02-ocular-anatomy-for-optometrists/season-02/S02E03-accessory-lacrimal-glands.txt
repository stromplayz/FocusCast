# Description: Small glands, big responsibility. In this episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks visits the accessory lacrimal glands, the tiny serous glands of Krause tucked into the conjunctival fornices and the glands of Wolfring lined up along the tarsal borders. We walk through their counts, addresses, microscopic design, ducts and nerve supply, and their share of the eye's everyday basal aqueous tears. You will also hear the classic finding that a life without the main lacrimal gland can still be a comfortably wet one, and what that teaches every optometrist about dry eye. A patient, anatomy-first listen for students, clinicians, and curious minds.

Host: Here is a small experiment you can try right now, wherever you are listening. Stop blinking for as long as you comfortably can. In a surprisingly short time, and it is shorter than most people expect, the eye begins to sting, the world softens, and blinking simply takes over. Now hold on to the question hiding inside that experiment. For every waking hour when you were not running that test, who was keeping your eyes wet? Not the dramatic tears of an onion or a farewell scene. The quiet, constant moisture that lets an eye stay clear and comfortable all day. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, the series where we take one structure at a time and learn exactly how it is built. This is our Lacrimal System season, and today, episode three, is called Accessory Lacrimal Glands. To guide us, as always, I have a patient teacher with me. Welcome back.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome to every listener. Let me set the map before we walk, because the season is young and I want today's episode to sit firmly in its place. In episode one, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy, we met the main lacrimal gland in its fossa by the roof of the orbit, and we traced how its secretion is switched on. Last week, in episode two, Lacrimal Gland Parts and Relationships, we split that gland into its orbital and palpebral lobes and followed its ducts arcing over the levator aponeurosis into the upper fornix. Today we go small. The accessory lacrimal glands are a family of tiny glands scattered in the conjunctiva itself, and the word accessory simply means attached helper, a structure working alongside a larger organ, doing a version of the same job. Two families carry the name, the glands of Krause and the glands of Wolfring, and today we will find their addresses, count them, and settle the most important question of all. How much of the everyday tear supply is actually theirs.

Host: Before we go to those addresses, I want the names for a moment, because names carry history. Who were Krause and Wolfring, and why does anatomy keep them on the payroll two centuries later?

Guide: Both names belong to nineteenth century European anatomists who were cataloguing the eye and its surroundings with microscopes and remarkable patience. Krause described the little glands that crowd the conjunctival fornices, and Wolfring described a smaller row of larger ones near the tarsal plates, which is why in some older books, especially those with an Italian leaning, you will see the same glands credited to Ciaccio and called the glands of Ciaccio. One structure, two nameplates, a very common situation in anatomy. And notice what the naming tells us about thinking at the time. These glands were catalogued as small copies of the main lacrimal gland, almost as footnotes to it. The wonderful twist, and the spine of today's episode, is that the footnote turned out to be doing the daily work. That happens more often in anatomy than textbooks admit. Structure first, importance later.

Host: All right, addresses then. Take me to the eye and point. Where exactly are we standing when we say accessory lacrimal glands?

Guide: We are standing in the conjunctiva, the transparent lining that covers the inside of the lids and wraps forward onto the eye. We first met that lining back in Series 1, in an episode called The Front of the Eye, and today it is the whole landscape. The key landmark is the fornix. The fornix is the pocket where conjunctiva lining the lid folds back on itself to become conjunctiva lying on the eyeball, like the inside corner of a loose sleeve. There is a superior fornix above, deep and capacious, and an inferior one below, shallower. Stretched within the loose tissue of these fornices sits the first family, the glands of Krause. They are embedded in the subconjunctival tissue, which is the soft, stretchy layer just beneath the conjunctival surface, so they live almost at the very surface of the eye, bathed by the very fluid they produce.

Host: How many of them are there? Because when you say a family of glands scattered in a pocket, I cannot picture the number.

Guide: The classic counts are these, and they are worth memorising because they are the sort of detail that separates a student who has truly dissected the region from one who has skimmed it. In the upper fornix there are about twenty to forty glands of Krause, with the majority crowded toward the outer, temporal side, which places most of them directly beneath the openings of the main lacrimal gland's ducts that we followed last week in episode two. In the lower fornix the family is much thinner on the ground, roughly six to eight glands. Each individual gland is tiny, about a millimetre across or less, a pale nodule of secretory tissue, and each is a compound tubuloacinar gland, meaning it is built from branching little tubes that end in rounded secretory pockets. So picture the upper fornix not as one factory but as a scatter of twenty to forty cottage workshops, threaded through the soft tissue, all pouring into the same room.

Host: And why would nature scatter little factories inside the tear pocket itself, instead of keeping all production up in the orbit with the main gland? That seems like a strange place to put secretory tissue.

Guide: It is strange only until you think about the geometry, and then it is brilliant. Where do tears collect? They pool in the fornices, in those same pockets, because gravity and lid closure gather fluid into the recesses of the conjunctival sac. So the glands of Krause are sited exactly at the reservoir. Their short ducts open directly onto the conjunctival surface of the fornix, and their product arrives with no plumbing at all, just delivered straight into the pool. There is also an engineering argument for distribution. A single central factory is efficient but fragile, one road closure and the city goes dry. Distributed production means the eye's moisture does not depend on any one address. I will be honest that the full geometry of the conjunctival sac, its shape and its reservoir behaviour, belongs to episode five, Conjunctival Sac, so today I am only borrowing enough geography to explain the addresses. But hold this much. The glands of Krause live inside the tear pool for the same reason a water company builds beside the reservoir.

Host: That leaves the second family. Where do the glands of Wolfring live, and how do they differ from the Krause set we have just placed?

Guide: The glands of Wolfring chose a different address, closer to the working edge of the lid. In the upper lid you find two to five of them, and they sit just above the upper border of the tarsal plate, embedded in the subconjunctival tissue as it passes from the tarsus toward the fornix. In the lower lid there are usually one to four, running along the lower border of the inferior tarsal plate in the same fashion. Now, the tarsal plate is the stiff, skeleton-like sheet inside each lid, and we gave it a full episode last season, episode seven, Tarsal Plates and Meibomian Glands, so if you want the plates themselves, that is your stop. Today the plates are simply landmarks, the fixed shelves against which the Wolfring glands line up. Two further contrasts are worth locking in. First, the Wolfring glands are larger than the glands of Krause, fewer in number but meatier, each one a more substantial secretory mass. Second, their ducts open onto the tarsal conjunctiva near the tarsal borders rather than into the depth of the fornix, so their product is delivered closer to the lid margin, close to where the tears actually spread across the eye at each blink.

Host: So we have two addresses, the fornix pockets and the tarsal borders. I am going to be honest, in an examination room those two names are exactly the sort of pair my brain swaps. Krause, Wolfring. Which gland goes with which address?

Guide: Use the letters as your hooks, because they are kind enough to oblige. K for Krause, and K for the kind of pocket the Krause glands love, the big open cul-de-sac of the fornix, where the crowd of them gathers like guests at a garden party, twenty to forty upstairs and six to eight downstairs. W for Wolfring, and W for the wall of the tarsal plate, the stiff shelf, where two to five of the larger ones stand like sentries just above the upper border in the top lid, with one to four below. Fornix crowd versus tarsal border sentries. Quantity versus size. Deep pocket versus working edge. If you can hold one pair of images, the crowd in the pocket and the sentries on the shelf, you own the geography of the entire topic, and every other fact we discuss today can be hung on those two hooks.

Host: One honest footnote before we leave the addresses. You have been giving me tidy numbers, twenty to forty, six to eight, two to five. Do those counts hold for every person, or is anatomy gentler than that?

Guide: Gentler, always. The classic counts are ranges gathered by anatomists who dissected limited numbers of donors and counted what they found, so the honest reading is that the numbers vary from person to person and the textbooks report the range where most people fall. One eye may carry a few glands more than its partner. What never varies is the pattern, and that is the part to trust. The fornices always carry the numerous small Krause population, with a strong temporal skew above. The tarsal borders always carry the smaller, larger Wolfring row. So treat the counts as orders of magnitude and the addresses as facts. Examinations ask where, and how many, and the safe answer is the range, because the range is what the literature actually says.

Host: Now let us go under the microscope. When an anatomist looks at one of these glands, what do they actually see, and how close is the copy to the main gland we studied in episodes one and two?

Guide: The copy is remarkably faithful. Each accessory gland is essentially a miniature of the main lacrimal gland, so much so that on a stained slide even an experienced eye could struggle to tell them apart. You find acini, the rounded secretory pockets, lined by serous cells, the kind of cell that manufactures a watery, protein-bearing fluid rather than slippery mucus or oily sebum. Those acini drain into small ducts lined by a double layer of epithelial cells, and the ducts run a short course to open onto the conjunctival surface. Wrapped like a basket around each acinus you find myoepithelial cells, special contractile cells that squeeze the pocket and help push the secretion down the duct, the same arrangement we saw in the main gland in episode one. And the contrast with their neighbours is exactly what makes the story clean. The goblet cells scattered through the conjunctiva are the mucus makers, the meibomian glands inside the tarsal plates are the oil makers, and the accessory lacrimal glands are the water works, serous and watery to the core. Why that division matters is precisely the subject of next week's episode, so I will only plant the flag today. Each tear ingredient has its own factory address.

Host: One more structural thread before we move on, and it is the kind of thing that makes anatomy feel inevitable rather than memorised. Where do these little glands come from in the first place? Why does lacrimal tissue end up buried in the conjunctiva at all?

Guide: Because they are born there, and development is the cleanest explanation of their whole anatomy. The main lacrimal gland begins life as tiny buds of epithelium sprouting from the conjunctiva high in the forming upper fornix, growing upward and backward until they meet the bone and condense into the gland we studied in episodes one and two. The accessory glands are the same story that stayed home. Buds of conjunctival epithelium grew down into the loose subconjunctival tissue of the fornices and became the glands of Krause, and similar buds near the tarsal borders became the glands of Wolfring. That origin explains everything we have placed today. It explains why they sit just beneath the conjunctival surface, why their ducts are short and open directly onto it, and why they are microscopic copies of the main gland, because they are branches of the same family tree. When anatomy is explained by development, the counts stop being trivia and start being a biography.

Host: Let us talk plumbing and wiring, then. The ducts we have, in principle. But what drives these glands? Do they have their own nerve supply, their own arterial line, or are they simply hanging off the main gland's systems?

Guide: They borrow the main gland's infrastructure, and that is exactly what you would predict from glands that are microscopic twins of it. A one-breath reminder of the route we traced in episode one, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy, will do. Parasympathetic secretomotor fibres, the wiring that switches tears on, leave the brainstem, travel with the greater petrosal nerve, relay in the pterygopalatine ganglion, and continue along the zygomatic and lacrimal nerves to reach lacrimal tissue. The accessory glands sit on that same delivery line, so the same signals that can wake the main gland can modulate the little ones. On the arterial side they are fed by branches of the lacrimal artery, itself a branch of the ophthalmic artery, the vessel we would expect to supply structures at the upper outer corner of the orbit. So one nerve line, one arterial line, several glands. In anatomy, as in a house, the wiring plan usually outlives any single appliance plugged into it.

Host: And now the question I have been saving, because it is the reason this episode exists at all. What is their share of the daily tear supply? The main gland is famous, it is the gland in the dictionary. What do the small ones actually contribute?

Guide: Here is the classic teaching, and it is one of the most useful divisions in the whole lacrimal season. Tear production comes in two modes. There is basal secretion, the slow, continuous housekeeping flow that keeps the eye wet all day, the flow you were enjoying before I asked you to stop blinking. And there is reflex secretion, the surge, switched on by irritation, injury, emotion, or a sudden bright light. In numbers, the basal flow of a resting eye is of the order of one to two microlitres per minute, a whisper of fluid, while a full crying reflex can push tear output toward something like thirty microlitres per minute, a completely different regime. And the division of labour, as classically taught, is this. The accessory glands, Krause and Wolfring together, supply the greater share of the basal aqueous flow, while the main lacrimal gland is the reflex factory, held mostly in reserve for emergencies and emotion. In the nineteen sixties the American eye anatomist L. Taylor Jones grouped the tear producers exactly this way, into basic secretors and reflex secretors, and generations of students have carried that frame ever since. The accessory glands are the base load power station. The main gland is the surge generator.

Host: Wait. Let me push back on that, because it sounds almost too tidy, and it seems to demote the famous main gland to an occasional fireworks display. How do we actually know the little glands carry the daily load? What is the evidence?

Guide: The pushback is fair, and the evidence is one of the great little stories in ophthalmic anatomy. Surgeons have long known that when the main lacrimal gland has to be removed, or has been damaged or irradiated by disease, the eye usually does not become dry. Think about what that observation means. A person can lose the single most famous tear gland and keep a comfortable, wet, healthy eye, because the accessory glands, tiny and scattered and never on the surgical stage, continue their quiet basal output. If the accessory glands were not carrying the daily load, every main gland removal would be a catastrophe for the cornea, and it is not. Now, in fairness to the main gland, modern research has softened the tidy version. The main gland does contribute a trickle to basal flow, and the two systems overlap more than the classic model admits, so the true picture is a spectrum with a heavy accessory share rather than a strict handover. But for the clinic and for the examination, the classic division remains the working truth. Basal aqueous, think Krause and Wolfring. Reflex surge, think main gland. One honest sentence, and then we move on.

Host: I love that observation, because it reframes the whole architecture. The eye deliberately built redundancy into its own moisture supply. Tell me why that redundancy matters so much, because a dry surface sounds uncomfortable, but you make it sound like something more serious than discomfort.

Guide: It is more serious, and the reason is the cornea. The surface of the eye is not like the skin, which is content to be dry for hours. The transparent front of the eye must stay wet every single waking moment, because its clarity, the very thing that lets light through, depends on a smooth, hydrated, continuously renewed surface. A surface like that cannot tolerate a single point of failure in its water supply. So the system is engineered the way critical systems usually are. Distributed base load production, scattered through the fornices and along the tarsal borders where the fluid is needed, plus a central reserve generator up in the orbit for surges. Lose the central generator and the base load keeps the lights on. That is the design logic of today's episode in one breath. And it is why I keep calling these tiny glands a big responsibility. A millimetre of tissue in a conjunctival pocket, times thirty or so, is quietly holding up the transparency of the most precisely engineered optical surface the body owns.

Host: Let us bring this into the clinic now, because our listeners are optometrists and students, not surgeons of the nineteenth century. When does the anatomy of today matter in a consulting room on a Tuesday afternoon?

Guide: More often than the gland's small size suggests, and let me give you the Tuesday afternoon versions. First, the dry eye clinic. The diseases that dry the eye are not polite to the accessory glands. In Sjogren syndrome, the autoimmune condition classically paired with dry eyes and dry mouth, the inflammatory attack hits the main lacrimal gland and the accessory glands together, because to the immune system they are the same tissue in different addresses. So do not teach yourself that the accessory glands are spare parts that survive the storm. The redundancy is built against losing one gland, not against disease attacking the whole secretory family. Second, simple lid examination. Every time you evert an upper lid to scan the tarsal conjunctiva, you are looking directly at the neighbourhood of the Wolfring glands along that upper tarsal border, and when you pull the lid down and gaze into the lower fornix, you are standing in Krause territory, six to eight glands and all. Knowing who lives there changes what you notice.

Host: What else is in the clinical column? You mentioned everting the lid, and I suspect there is more, because tears and drops and lenses all share this same geography.

Guide: There is more, and it is all geography. Any conjunctival procedure, whether it is a biopsy taken from the fornix, a repair after injury, or surgery around the fornices, is operating in the district where the glands of Krause are scattered, and tissue removed carelessly there removes basal tear capacity with it. Contact lens work lives in the same neighbourhood, because a lens floats in the tear film that these glands supply and sits within millimetres of both addresses, so the comfort of a lens is partly the comfort of the accessory gland output it must share. Even the humble instilled drop is geography, because a drop placed in the lower fornix, the way we teach patients to aim, lands exactly in the Krause district and spreads from there. And a last gentle pointer forward. When we eventually reach the puncta in episode six and the drainage story beyond it, you will see that everything the accessory glands pour out also has an exit, and the anatomy of that exit is its own set of episodes. Today we stay with the supply side.

Host: One more question in this vein, and it is the kind patients ask. Do both eyes carry the same set, and does this whole family of glands change as we age?

Guide: Both eyes carry essentially the same set, the same two families at the same two addresses, and that symmetry is the general rule for paired structures in the head. As for age, the accessory glands age with the rest of the lacrimal system. Histological studies show the same fate that befalls the main gland, a slow loss of secretory tissue and a drift of the remaining acini toward less active forms, so the basal aqueous flow available to an eighty year old cornea is genuinely thinner than the flow a twenty year old enjoyed. That is one anatomical reason tearing changes across a lifetime, and it connects to what patients tell you, that their eyes feel drier and less tolerant of long visual tasks than they once did. I am careful today to stay with structure rather than management, because the tear film as a whole, its layers and its turnover, is next week's territory. But the anatomical sentence is worth saying plainly. The little glands, so easy to forget, grow old too, and the eye notices.

Host: We have covered a lot of ground for such small glands. Walk me through the recap the way you would want a student to be able to recite it on the way out of the room.

Guide: Gladly, and let us do it as an address book. Family one, the glands of Krause, named for a nineteenth century anatomist, compound tubuloacinar serous glands about a millimetre or less across, roughly twenty to forty in the upper fornix crowded toward the temporal side and about six to eight in the lower, embedded in the subconjunctival tissue, their short ducts opening straight into the fornix. Family two, the glands of Wolfring, also called Ciaccio, larger and fewer, two to five above the upper border of the upper tarsal plate and one to four along the lower border of the lower one, their ducts opening onto the tarsal conjunctiva near the working edge of the lid. Under the microscope both are faithful miniatures of the main lacrimal gland, serous acini with myoepithelial baskets and double layered ducts. They share the main gland's wiring and plumbing, the parasympathetic secretomotor line and the lacrimal artery. And their job, the headline of the day, is basal aqueous secretion, the greater share of the everyday flow.

Host: So if I compress that to the three sentences a student should carry into an examination, what are they?

Guide: Sentence one, Krause are the crowd in the fornix, twenty to forty above, six to eight below. Sentence two, Wolfring are the sentries on the tarsal shelf, two to five up top, one to four below, larger but fewer. Sentence three, together they supply the bulk of basal aqueous tears, which is why a life without the main lacrimal gland can still be a wet life. Three sentences, two addresses, one design principle of distributed redundancy. If a listener can say those three sentences cold, they have today's episode in their pocket, and they have earned the right to smile at the next person who assumes the main lacrimal gland does all the work.
