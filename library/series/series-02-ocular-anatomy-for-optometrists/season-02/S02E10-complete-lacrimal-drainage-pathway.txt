# Description: The season finale of the Lacrimal System. In this capstone episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks walks the entire tear drainage route in one unbroken journey, from the punctum at the lid margin, through the canaliculi and their valves, down the lacrimal sac and nasolacrimal duct, to the nose. Along the way we assemble every valve in order, Rosenmuller above and Hasner below, and meet the lacrimal pump, the blink driven mechanism that moves tears against gravity. It is the full plumbing map of the eye, told for the ear.

Host: Here we are at the finish line of a remarkable season. Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. I am your host, and this is the season finale of our second season inside Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, the season devoted to the lacrimal system. For nine episodes we have been building a piece of anatomy one structure at a time, and today we do something different. We connect everything. The entire route a tear travels, from the moment it is collected at the lid margin to the moment it disappears inside the nose, in one unbroken journey. And we will finally meet the machine that moves it all. To guide us on this capstone walk, as he has all season, here is our teacher. Welcome.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome to the finale. Let me frame what we are doing tonight, because it will help you hold the whole episode in your head. The lacrimal system, as we said back in episode one, is really two systems wearing one name. There is the secretory half, the factories, the lacrimal gland in its fossa, the accessory glands in the fornices, and the tear film they produce. That was episodes one through five. And there is the excretory half, the plumbing, the collectors and pipes that carry the used tear fluid away. That was episodes six through nine, and tonight, episode ten, is where we weld the two halves into a single working circuit. There is very little new anatomy tonight. Mostly assembly, and one beautiful mechanism we have saved for last, the lacrimal pump.

Host: Before we walk, let me ask the question a patient would ask, the one this whole season has been quietly answering. You blink, your eyes stay wet, and you do not flood the room. So where does the tear fluid actually go?

Guide: That is the question, and here is the short answer we will spend the episode unpacking. Tears are produced, they are spread across the eye by the blink, and then they are collected at two tiny openings on the eyelid margins called the puncta, funnelled through little tubes called canaliculi, stored for a moment in a small chamber called the lacrimal sac, and finally carried down a long vertical duct into the nose, where they drain beneath a shelf called the inferior turbinate, in a passage called the inferior meatus. That is the entire route. Punctum to nose. Tonight we walk it station by station, and we pay special attention to the little gates, the valves, that keep the traffic moving in one direction only. And notice what this route has in common with everything we have built this season. Not one millimetre of it can be seen from the outside, and not one millimetre of it is optional. Keep the phrase punctum to nose in your mind. It is the spine of the episode.

Host: Then let us begin where a tear begins its exit, right at the edge of the eyelid. Set the scene for us. What does the inner corner of the eye look like to someone who now knows what to look for?

Guide: Picture the lower lid margin. Along its inner edge there is a thin, glistening ridge of fluid, a tiny river of tears that runs the whole width of the open eye. Anatomists call it the tear meniscus, and together with the pool it deepens into near the nose, the tear lake, it is the frontline of the conjunctival sac. We mapped that sac in episode five, the Conjunctival Sac episode, as the great reservoir of tears, and the meniscus is its edge. Each lid carries its own meniscus, roughly a millimetre tall, and the two ridges meet and pool at the inner corner, which is exactly where the drainage hardware waits. Now here is the elegant part. Sitting inside that river, like mouths at the edge of a stream, are the two drainage openings. The lids sweep the tear river toward the inner corner with every blink, like windshield wipers, and at the inner corner the fluid finds the intake valves waiting. Everything from here on is one way traffic.

Host: Those intake valves, the puncta. We gave them a full episode, episode six. Give us the essentials again, just enough to launch the journey.

Guide: Essentials, gladly. Each lid margin, upper and lower, ends near the nose in a small elevation called the papilla, a soft little mound carrying at its summit the punctum, the punctal opening. It is tiny, about a third of a millimetre across, which is why you will never find it casually on a mirror. The lower punctum sits roughly six to six and a half millimetres from the inner corner of the eye, the upper one slightly closer in, and both open not like little funnels but vertically, the upper one pointing down and the lower one pointing up, into short vertical passages called the ampullae. That vertical orientation matters, because it is the first segment of the tube we are about to walk. And notice the geometry. The punctal opening faces slightly toward the eye, so its mouth dips into the meniscus rather than rising above it, like an intake sitting at the waterline. In episode six we called the puncta the doors of the drainage system. Tonight, doors open.

Host: Two doors, then, one on each lid. Do they work equally, or does one of them do most of the hauling?

Guide: They are not equal, and the inequality is worth knowing. In most people the lower collector, the lower punctum and its canaliculus, carries the larger share of drainage, often quoted as around two thirds of the total tear flow. But the system is deliberately built with redundancy. Two doors, two tubes, converging on one chamber, so that if one route is narrowed, blocked, or surgically altered, the other can carry more of the load. And one alignment detail ties straight back to the meniscus. The puncta sit right against the eye, bathed in the tear lake, facing the fluid. When a lid loses that contact, when a punctum is turned away from the lake, the door is still open but nothing reaches it, and the eye waters. Anatomy explains the symptom.

Host: So the tear has entered the punctum and dropped into the ampulla. Now the real journey begins. Take us inward.

Guide: From the ampulla, each canaliculus runs about two millimetres vertically, then turns at nearly a right angle and runs about eight millimetres horizontally, hugging the lid margin toward the inner corner. Add the segments together and each canaliculus is roughly ten millimetres long, a tube narrower than a needle, lined with epithelium and wrapped in elastic tissue that lets it stretch and recoil like the best plumbing in the body. And the horizontal part is not free floating. It is woven into the substance of the lid, wrapped by fibres of the very muscle that closes the eye, a fact that will matter enormously when we reach the pump. Near the inner corner, in about ninety percent of people, the upper and lower canaliculi join into a single short common stem, the common canaliculus, which enters the lacrimal sac at a point called the common internal punctum. And right there, at the doorway into the sac, sits the first named valve of our journey, the valve of Rosenmuller. We met it in episode seven, the Canaliculi episode. Tonight it takes its place in the itinerary.

Host: Stop there for a moment, because a valve inside a tear duct deserves an explanation. What exactly is the valve of Rosenmuller, and what is it doing?

Guide: It is not a valve like a metal gate, nothing mechanical. It is a fold of the lining membrane, a small redundancy of mucosa that projects into the opening where the common canaliculus meets the sac. Think of a soft, one way flap, like the valves in your veins. Fluid pushed from the canaliculus into the sac parts the fold easily and passes through. But fluid trying to come back, from the sac outward into the canaliculus, presses the fold flat against the wall and seals the way. That is its whole job. The valve of Rosenmuller keeps drainage flowing inward and blocks reflux outward, so that whatever collects in the sac cannot wash back onto the eye. Remember the name and the position, uppermost of all the valves. When anatomists list the valves of the drainage system in order, Rosenmuller is always number one.

Host: Through the first gate, then, and into the chamber. The lacrimal sac had its own episode, episode eight. Rebuild it for us as we step inside.

Guide: The sac is the only soft walled chamber in the whole system, and it hides in a groove of bone. Its home is the lacrimal fossa, a vertical gutter on the inner wall of the orbit, formed in front by the anterior lacrimal crest of the maxilla and behind by the posterior crest of the lacrimal bone. The sac itself measures about twelve to fifteen millimetres in height and four to six millimetres across, with its upper end, the fundus, rising three to four millimetres above the medial canthal tendon, the strong fibrous band we described in episode eight. Lining it is a double layered epithelium rich in goblet cells, whose mucus keeps the inner surfaces slick so that tears slide rather than stick. Wrapped around it is a luxuriant venous plexus, a soft cushion of veins, and when you cry hard, that plexus engorges and adds its own squeeze, one more push in the right direction. The sac is not just a passive pouch. Hold that thought, because its walls are about to become the star of the show.

Host: From the sac, the journey turns downward, toward the nose. That was episode nine. Give us the duct.

Guide: The nasolacrimal duct, the tear to nose carrier. It is the longest stretch of the journey, about eighteen millimetres in total. Its upper twelve millimetres run inside a bony canal, a tunnel formed by the maxilla, the lacrimal bone, and the inferior concha, descending through the thin bone between the eye socket and the nasal cavity. Then, in its lower five to six millimetres, the duct leaves the bone and runs in the wall of the nose itself, angling slightly outward and backward as it goes. Follow its line on a skull and you see the logic. It aims not straight down but down, slightly back, and a little outward, toward a very specific destination, the lateral wall of the nasal cavity, just beneath the anterior end of the inferior turbinate. And unlike every station before it, the duct is invisible in the clinic, sealed inside bone and nasal wall, which is why we learn its course from skulls and textbooks rather than from the slit lamp. Everything above this point was collection. The duct is transport.

Host: And inside that long duct, are there more of those mucosal gates, or is it an open pipe?

Guide: An open pipe with checkpoints. Along its length anatomists have described smaller, less constant folds of mucosa, and names like the valve of Krause and the valve of Taillefer appear in the older texts, though it is honest to say their function is less certain. But at the bottom of the duct there is one final gate that is constant and important, the valve of Hasner. It is a mucosal fold guarding the actual opening of the duct into the nose, and its job is beautifully practical. It prevents air, mucus, and pressure from the nasal cavity blowing backward up into the duct and the sac. It is why you do not produce bubbles at your inner corners every time you blow your nose. So in order, from top to bottom, the gates of the system are Rosenmuller first, the minor folds of Krause and Taillefer along the duct, and Hasner last.

Host: So the duct ends at the inferior meatus, under the turbinate. What happens to a tear at the very end of the line?

Guide: It simply leaves the territory of the eye. At the opening beneath the inferior turbinate, the tear fluid passes into the inferior meatus, one of the air passages of the nose, where it mixes with nasal mucus, spreads over the nasal lining, and is eventually swallowed or evaporates. This is the answer to an everyday mystery. When you cry, the nose runs, because the excess tears are being pushed through this very exit faster than the nose can absorb them. The route can jam from below, too, which is why a badly congested nose can leave an eye watering, because swollen nasal lining crowds the exit. And one developmental pearl we noted in episode nine. In newborns, the lower end of the duct is often still sealed by a thin membrane at Hasner's valve, which normally opens within the first weeks to months of life. When it lingers, tears pool and overflow, the classic watery baby eye. The anatomy of the exit door explains it.

Host: We have walked every station. Before we meet the engine, give us the whole route in one breath, in order, so the map sits complete in the listener's head.

Guide: Here is the itinerary, top to bottom. The tear meniscus and the tear lake. The punctum, upper and lower, about a third of a millimetre wide, opening vertically into the ampulla. The vertical canaliculus, two millimetres. The horizontal canaliculus, eight millimetres. The common internal punctum, where the two meet in about ninety percent of people. The valve of Rosenmuller, gate number one. The lacrimal sac, twelve to fifteen millimetres tall, in its bony fossa between the two crests. The nasolacrimal duct, about eighteen millimetres, twelve in the canal and five to six in the nasal wall. The minor folds of Krause and Taillefer. The valve of Hasner, gate number two, at the opening into the inferior meatus, beneath the inferior turbinate. And finally the nose. Think of it as a staircase with landings. Two little steps down, one long step across, a chamber, a long staircase down, and a final flap into the open air of the nose.

Host: That is the plumbing laid out. Now the promise you made at the start of the season. Tell me the truth about how tears actually move. Is it gravity pulling them down this staircase?

Guide: Mostly no, and this is the revelation of the episode. Gravity helps when you are upright, and capillarity, the same force that pulls water up a thin straw, helps in tubes as narrow as these. But the great driver is muscular. Remember the numbers. The whole tear film on the eye holds only about seven microliters, and basal secretion adds roughly one to two microliters every minute, so the system is constantly balancing production against drainage. You blink about fifteen to twenty times a minute, and a mechanism classically described by Jones, the lacrimal specialist, explained what every one of those blinks does. Each blink is a stroke of a pump. Here is a thought experiment that proves it. Hang upside down, and your eyes still do not flood. Tears still vanish into the nose against gravity, because something stronger than weight is moving them. The mechanism is called the lacrimal pump, and it lives in the very structures we have already met, which is exactly why we saved it for the finale.

Host: Then name the cast. What structures, working together, make this pump?

Guide: The cast is short and elegant. First, the orbicularis oculi, the muscle that closes the eyelids, and specifically its pretarsal portion, the small fibres lying across the tarsal plates near the inner corner. Those fibres are arranged in two heads, a superficial head passing in front of the lacrimal sac, and a deep head, often called Horner's muscle, the lacrimal part of the orbicularis, passing behind it. Second, the medial canthal tendon, the fibrous anchor into which both heads and the canaliculi are woven. Third, the canaliculi themselves, with their elastic walls. And fourth, the lacrimal sac, with its stretchy fascia rich in elastic fibres. All of these are served by one nerve, the facial nerve, the seventh cranial nerve, which fires with every blink. When the muscle contracts, the whole inner corner apparatus squeezes and releases, and that squeeze and release is the pump.

Host: Walk me through a single blink, then, in slow motion, eyes closing. What happens at the inner corner?

Guide: Eyes closing. The pretarsal fibres contract, and three things happen almost together. The lids close and the punctum is pressed against the opposite lid and drawn toward the nose, sealing the intake door. The contraction shortens the horizontal canaliculus and squeezes the ampulla, pushing whatever tears it holds inward, through the valve of Rosenmuller. And at the same moment the deep head, Horner's muscle, tugs on the fascia around the lacrimal sac, pulling the sac walls apart and enlarging the chamber. Enlarging a chamber lowers the pressure inside it, so the sac becomes a suction chamber, drawing the expelled tears out of the canaliculus and down into itself. A closed door behind, a widening chamber ahead. The tear has no choice but to move inward.

Host: And now the eyes open again. What is the second half of the stroke?

Guide: The muscle relaxes, and everything springs back. The elastic tissue in the sac wall, which was stretched open during the blink, recoils, and the sac begins to collapse. Now the pressure inside the sac rises, and the tear fluid, with nowhere else to go, is pushed down the nasolacrimal duct, past the minor folds and through the valve of Hasner into the inferior meatus. Meanwhile the punctum reopens, the ampulla refills from the tear lake by capillary action, and the canaliculus is loaded for the next stroke. Blink, and the pump pulls. Open, and the pump pushes. This is also why a hard blink, or a squint against the wind, drives noticeably more fluid into the nose. The pump answers to how firmly the lids squeeze. Fifteen to twenty times a minute, all day, without you ever feeling it. The tear is moved through the whole system the way blood is moved through the veins, by muscular pumping against valves, not by pouring downhill.

Host: Beautiful. And like any machine, we understand it best when it breaks. What does the anatomy predict will happen if the pump fails?

Guide: It predicts a very specific symptom, and it is one of the most satisfying pieces of reasoning in this part of anatomy. If the facial nerve is paralysed, as in a Bell's palsy, the orbicularis no longer contracts properly. Tears are still produced normally, but the pump stops. No squeeze, no suction, no effective drainage, and the eye waters constantly, even though nothing is wrong with the production side. The same logic applies to the alignment pearl from earlier. An everted punctum is a door lifted out of the river. The anatomy tells you where to look. Watering is never a reason to assume overproduction. First you ask, is the tear lake loaded, is the punctum facing the water, is the pump moving. Structure first, then function. That is the whole discipline we have been learning this season.
