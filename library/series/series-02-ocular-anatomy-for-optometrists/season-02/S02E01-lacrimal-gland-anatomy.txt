# Description: Where do tears come from, and what kind of gland makes them? In this season opener of the Lacrimal System, Focus Cast by focuslinks maps the main lacrimal gland: its shallow fossa high in the anterolateral orbital roof, its almond-like dimensions, its lobular serous architecture, and the slender ducts that deliver the aqueous tears into the superior conjunctival fornix. We follow the lacrimal artery and trace the long parasympathetic pathway that carries the turn-on signal from the facial nerve through the pterygopalatine ganglion to the acinar cells, and we contrast the steady basal trickle with the reflex flood. A clear anatomy refresher for optometry students, optometrists, and eye care teams who want the lacrimal gland built piece by piece.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome, every listener, to Season Two. I am genuinely fond of this season, because the lacrimal system is one of those corners of anatomy that students skim past in the textbook and then meet in the chair on day one, in the patient whose eye will not stop watering, or the patient whose eye burns because it cannot make enough tears. The system is a small industry with two divisions. There is a manufacturing side, the glands that produce the tears, and there is a drainage side, the fine plumbing that collects the used tear film and delivers it into the nose. Today belongs to the head office of the manufacturer, the main lacrimal gland. Our plan is simple and we will hold to it. Where does the gland sit, how big is it, what is it built from, how do its ducts deliver the product, and what starts the secretion. One step at a time, as always.

Host: Then let us put it on the map first. Where exactly does the main lacrimal gland live inside the orbit?

Guide: Open the orbit in your mind, go high, and go out. The gland sits in the anterolateral part of the orbital roof, which means the upper and outer corner of the socket, tucked just behind the rim where the brow bone curves around toward the temple. If you slide a fingertip along the outer third of your own brow and press gently behind the rim, you are knocking at its door. Back in episode three of this series, the one we called Orbital Walls, we walked the walls plate by plate, and the front half of the roof is formed by the orbital plate of the frontal bone. Across a lifetime that bone has moulded a shallow, smooth hollow of its own to fit the gland, and that hollow has a name, the lacrimal fossa. A fossa, remember, is simply a depression, a moulded resting place in bone. So the gland is not floating loosely somewhere under the brow. It has an address. It rests in a hollow of the frontal bone, high in the roof, pressed up against it just behind the upper-outer rim. Hold on to that single sentence, because it explains half of the clinical signs we will build today.

Host: Stay with that hollow a moment, because I want the picture exact. How deep is the fossa, and what does the gland face on its deep side, away from the bone?

Guide: Shallow, not deep, so think of a fruit resting in a shallow dish rather than a stone sitting in a cup. The gland fills the fossa snugly, with only a little connective tissue, a fine web of fibres, vessels and lymphatics, separating it from the frontal bone above. Below and toward the eye it faces the orbit itself, and here I must ration my enthusiasm, because the gland's closest neighbours, including the flat sheet of tendon that belongs to the lid opener, and the way the gland divides around that sheet, are precisely the story of our next episode. Today we keep the gland whole, one organ resting in its dish. What matters now is the geography, and the geography is three words. High. Out. Behind the rim. Say those three words to yourself, and every clinical sign we discuss in a few minutes will land exactly where it should.

Host: Good. Now the measurements. How big is this organ, and what does it feel like?

Guide: The textbook numbers are modest, smaller than most students guess. From end to end the gland measures about twenty millimetres. Across its widest face it is about twelve millimetres, and it is only about five millimetres deep. In weight it comes in at roughly zero point eight grams, under a single gram, about the weight of a paperclip. The classic comparison is an almond, flattened side down, tucked into that shallow dish in the roof, and the comparison holds for texture too. The gland is soft, lobulated, and slightly rubbery. And because it is small, soft, and hidden behind the bony rim, here is the fact that follows directly from the address we gave it. A healthy lacrimal gland cannot be seen on the face and cannot be felt through the lid at all. Keep that in mind, because it becomes a clinical test in itself.

Host: You have just opened a door. If the normal gland is silent and unfelt, what does the examiner find when it swells? What changes at the bedside?

Guide: Then the gland announces itself by geography, and the three words pay off. A swollen gland grows out from its fossa, so it becomes palpable as a firm, tender mass just behind the upper-outer rim, and in the upper-outer pocket of the lid, the superotemporal fornix, which we will define in a moment. The examiner's trick follows straight from the anatomy. Ask the patient to look down and in, then lift the upper lid gently, and a swollen gland can be felt through the fornix, because that downward glance swings the eyeball inward, clearing the corner where the gland lives. Because it occupies the highest and outermost corner, an enlarged gland presses on the eyeball from above and from the side, pushing the globe down and inward, so the eye may sit visibly lower, a displacement called hypoglobus, and slightly toward the nose. And there is a classic signature worth naming. In dacryoadenitis, which is simply the word for inflammation of the lacrimal gland, the swollen gland drags the outer half of the upper lid margin into a gentle S-shaped curve. Every one of these signs is pure geometry. The gland lives high, out, and behind the rim, so its swelling grows high, out, and behind the rim.

Host: From place and size, let us go underneath the surface. What kind of gland is this, in the language of tissue and histology?

Guide: It is an exocrine gland, which means it exports its product through ducts onto a surface, rather than secreting into the bloodstream the way an endocrine gland does. More precisely, it is a serous gland, and serous means it makes a watery, protein-rich fluid, not mucus and not oil. In structure the books call it a compound tubuloacinar gland, a mouthful that only means it is branched, and that its working units are tiny tubes and tiny flasks. Wrap a thin coat of connective tissue around the whole organ, send partitions of the same tissue inward, and the gland divides into lobules, small clusters of units, like the segments of an orange. And because it is a compound gland, those units do not empty straight onto the surface. Every drop must cross a chain of small channels before it reaches a true excretory duct, and we will follow that chain in a moment. Each working unit begins with the acinus, a ring of secretory cells gathered shoulder to shoulder around a tiny hollow centre called a lumen. The cells pour their watery product into that lumen, and the product is armed. It carries lysozyme, an enzyme that digests bacterial walls, lactoferrin, a protein that starves bacteria of iron, and immunoglobulin A, an antibody that patrols the surface. Cradling each acinus from the outside are myoepithelial cells, small contractile cells that squeeze the flask whenever the secretion needs a push.

Host: That is the workshop. Now walk me through one drop of secretion, from the acinar cell all the way out. Where does the plumbing lead?

Guide: Follow the drop. It leaves the acinar cells into the lumen, drains into the smallest channels, the intercalated ducts, then collects into the interlobular ducts that run inside those connective tissue partitions, gathering neighbours the way tributaries gather into a river. What finally emerges is a spray of fine excretory ducts. Counts vary a little from body to body, somewhere between six and twelve, and many anatomy texts simply say about a dozen slender ducts. These ducts leave the lower part of the gland, run down and forward, and empty into the superior conjunctival fornix. The fornix is the wide pocket where the conjunctiva, the transparent membrane that lines the inner lid, folds back onto the front of the eye, and this is the upper one, sitting just above the upper edge of the tarsal plate, the firm half-moon of dense tissue that forms the lid's skeleton, the one we profiled in episode seven, Tarsal Plates and Meibomian Glands. Notice the direction of delivery. The gland faces downward, so its entire output is poured over the eye from above, where the blink can sweep it across the surface. This is also why a drop instilled into the outer corner of the lid, or a reflex flood from any corner, spreads from above downward, carried by the same gentle sweep. One more thing about those ducts, and then I will hold the rest. Their exact winding route depends on how the gland is divided internally, and that division is precisely where our next episode begins.

Host: You called its product watery and protein-rich. Before we ask what starts the flow, tell us what we should call this fluid when it lands on the eye.

Guide: The gland produces the aqueous portion of the tear film, the watery middle layer that wets and nourishes the exposed surface we described back in Series 1, in the episode called The Front of the Eye. I am choosing my words with care. The full tear film is a sandwich of layers, oil on the outside, water in the middle, mucus beneath, and that layered story, with its thicknesses and turnover numbers, is episode fourteen, Tear Film Anatomy. Nor is the main gland the only aqueous factory. Smaller accessory glands tucked into the folds of the conjunctiva share the workload, and they have earned their own episode, episode thirteen, Accessory Lacrimal Glands. For today, one sentence is enough. The main lacrimal gland is the largest aqueous producer and the undisputed star of reflex tearing, the sudden flood. Keep the other players in view. We will meet them properly soon enough.

Host: Now the supply lines. Who feeds this factory with blood, and by which route does the food arrive?

Guide: Blood arrives through the lacrimal artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery, the vessel that supplies the whole orbit from within the skull. The lacrimal artery is one of its larger branches. It arises deep in the orbit near the optic canal, then runs forward along the upper border of the lateral rectus muscle, the muscle that turns the eye outward. And it is not travelling alone. Riding beside it for that whole journey is the lacrimal nerve, and the artery and the nerve arrive together at the back surface of the gland, like a delivery van and its courier pulling up to one door. Once there, the artery supplies the gland itself, then sends branches onward to the conjunctiva and to the eyelids as the lateral palpebral arteries. The blood that leaves returns through veins that join the superior ophthalmic vein, the great orbital vein heading back toward the skull base. The gland has lymphatic channels too, and they slip away to the nodes in front of the ear, the preauricular nodes, which is one more reason a diseased gland can make that corner of the face feel busy. Remember the image of the van and the courier sharing one road, because the courier takes over the story right now.

Host: The courier. The lacrimal nerve. What does the gland actually listen to, and which of those signals makes it switch on?

Guide: Three kinds of signal arrive at that gland, and they are easy to keep in order. First, sensation. The lacrimal nerve is a slender branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve, the great sensory nerve of the face, and it carries feeling from the gland and from the outer corner of the lid. Second, and the star of today, the secretomotor signal, the switch that turns secretion on. It is parasympathetic, the calm, wet-and-restore arm of the autonomic nervous system, the part that runs the housekeeping you never have to think about. Its journey is the longest detour in eye anatomy, so walk it once, slowly. It begins in a tiny cell group of the facial nerve, the seventh cranial nerve, tucked in the brainstem and called the lacrimal nucleus. Fibres leave as the greater petrosal nerve, pick up the sympathetic fibres of the deep petrosal nerve, and the two run together as the nerve of the pterygoid canal to the pterygopalatine ganglion, a relay station high behind the nose. There the parasympathetic fibres synapse, handing the message to fresh cells, and ride outward on branches of the maxillary division of the trigeminal, the zygomatic nerve and its zygomaticotemporal branch, before hopping across to the lacrimal nerve for the final few millimetres. Third, the sympathetic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion in the neck ride the same canal, pass through the ganglion without stopping, and reach the gland as its vasomotor supply, tuning its blood flow. The gland listens to three voices, but the wet one is parasympathetic.

Host: That is a remarkable itinerary. The turn-on signal starts in the brainstem, detours through a ganglion behind the nose, borrows roads from two divisions of a different nerve, and hops onto a sensory nerve for the last stretch. Why would the body route one small gland so elaborately?

Guide: Because the gland's history wrote its wiring. The lacrimal gland is a descendant of the surface itself. In the second month of development, tiny buds sprout from the ectoderm of the superotemporal conjunctival fornix, the very pocket where its ducts will one day open, and they grow up and back into the roof to become the gland. So the gland and the conjunctival sac are family, and its ducts are the original doorway, still opening into the room it was born in. The long parasympathetic detour survives because the pterygopalatine ganglion is the great fixed interchange through which the region's branches pass, and the developing gland simply plugged into the nearest junction box. And here is the proof you can watch in the clinic. Many newborns cry without tears in their first weeks of life, because the reflex pathway is not fully online until the gland and its wiring mature across the first month or two. The hardware grows from the surface, the wiring arrives from the brainstem, and it takes time to power up.

Host: Which brings us to the question this whole episode has been walking toward. How does secretion actually start? What turns the tap from a trickle into a flood?

Guide: The gland runs in two modes. The first is basal secretion, a slow, steady trickle that continues all day and all night to keep the eye surface wet, and the main gland shares that duty with the accessory glands we mentioned. The second mode is reflex secretion, the surge, and that is the main gland's signature performance. Notice what the gland does not make. It has no oil and no mucus to contribute. Its gift is water with defenders dissolved in it, and that is why reflex floods are watery, never greasy. The switch is thrown by three families of triggers. Irritation of the surface itself, a dust speck on the cornea or a lash against the conjunctiva, fires the sensory nerves of the trigeminal. Very bright light, arriving through the retina, contributes its own input. And emotion, descending from the higher centres of the brain, reaches the same switch, which is why we weep at a farewell as well as at a speck of grit. All three converge on the lacrimal nucleus in the brainstem, which fires the parasympathetic outflow we just traced. Acetylcholine, the chemical messenger, lands on muscarinic receptors on the acinar cells, the cells pour out their watery product, and the myoepithelial cells squeeze the flasks for good measure. And one elegant rule follows from the wiring. The trigger may arrive from one eye, but the flood is bilateral. Both glands tear together, which is why a hair in your right eye waters your left as well.

Host: Let me bring this to the chair. A patient arrives with a red, watering eye, the very picture we once walked through in an episode called Patient With Red Eye. How does today's anatomy explain that flood while I examine?

Guide: Picture the reflex arc, end to end, and the flood stops being mysterious. In Series 1, The Front of the Eye described the exposed surface and how exquisitely sensitive it is. The cornea is one of the most densely innervated surfaces in the entire body, so even a trivial irritation fires a loud signal up the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve into the brainstem. The lacrimal nucleus answers, parasympathetic outflow releases acetylcholine onto the acinar cells, and the gland pours aqueous into the superior fornix, where every blink spreads it across the eye. The watering is not the disease. It is the gland doing precisely what it was built to do, diluting and flushing the insult. The tear film you see spilling over the lid edge is the gland's answer, never the question. The same logic explains why a bright windy day makes some eyes stream, why light itself can trigger tearing, and why grief and laughter share one tap. So when you see a watery eye, place the gland in its fossa in your mind and ask which part of the arc has been triggered. That is anatomy quietly serving the examination.

Host: And the other side of the ledger? When the gland underdelivers, what changes, and why does age matter so much here?

Guide: Then the aqueous layer thins, and the exposed surface pays the price. The most honest anatomical statement is this. Aqueous output depends on living secretory units and on an intact parasympathetic drive, so any loss of either reduces the flood first and, in time, the trickle as well. Age does exactly that. Across the decades the gland shrinks, its acini are progressively replaced by connective tissue and fat, and its output declines, which is one reason so many older patients describe burning, gritty, chronically uncomfortable eyes. The surface, remember, was designed around a generous water supply, and when the supply line narrows, the complaints arrive on schedule. There are also inflammatory conditions that attack the body's secretory glands together, Sjogren syndrome being the classic example, and the lacrimal gland is squarely among their targets. The full dry-eye conversation, its layers and its management, is a clinical story for another day, because this season keeps strictly to the anatomy. But the anatomical takeaway stands on its own. The almond in the roof is not optional equipment. It is the reservoir of the wet state of the eye.
