# Description: The medial rectus is the largest, busiest, and most operated of the four rectus muscles, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks gives it a full anatomy session of its own. Follow the muscle from its two-headed origin on the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex, along its course against the paper-thin medial wall, to an insertion that sits only five and a half millimetres behind the limbus. Learn about its connective tissue pulley, its check ligament, its blood supply from the ophthalmic artery, and the exact spot where the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve enters its ocular surface. Clear numbers, plain language, and the anatomical reasons behind every landmark, for optometry students and practicing optometrists.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to the deep end of our anatomy season. Last time, in episode one, Extraocular Muscle Overview, we stood at the apex of the orbit and met all seven movers of the eye at once. We saw where they arise around the annulus of Zinn, how four of them form the muscular cone around the optic nerve, and how two others travel outside that cone. That was the group photograph. Today we slow the camera down. From this episode to the end of the season, every muscle gets a whole episode to itself, because the anatomy of a single eye muscle, examined honestly, is enough to fill half an hour. And we begin, fittingly, with the muscle you will think about most often in clinic. The medial rectus.

Guide: And it deserves the first deep dive. Of the four rectus muscles, the medial rectus is the largest and the most powerful. It is the one closest to the front of the eye in its attachment, the one surgeons move most often, and the one that works hardest in everyday life, because every time you read, thread a needle, or look at your own nose, it is doing the pulling. So here is our plan for today, and it is the same plan we will use for each muscle this season. Five stops. First, the origin, where the muscle is anchored at the very back of the orbit. Second, the course, the path it takes forward through the orbit. Third, the insertion, measured precisely from the limbus. Fourth, the pulley, the soft tissue sling that quietly steers it. And fifth, the nerve entry, the exact point where its motor nerve disappears into the muscle. Five landmarks, all with real numbers attached.

Host: Then let us start at the very beginning. Where does the medial rectus actually begin?

Guide: It begins at the apex of the orbit, on a structure we met in episode one, the annulus of Zinn. That is the common tendinous ring, a collar of dense fibrous tissue wrapped around the optic canal at the back of the orbit, and the four rectus muscles all take their origin from it. The medial rectus springs from the medial part of that ring, the side toward the nose. But here is the detail that makes this muscle special. It does not arise as a single strap. It arises by two heads, an upper head from the upper medial part of the ring and a lower head from the lower medial part. The two heads then join together into one muscle a few millimetres forward of the apex. Picture a river that starts as two streams and merges into one channel. That is the origin of the medial rectus.

Host: Two heads. That means something has to pass between them, surely. What travels through that gap at the apex?

Guide: Something very important, and it is one of my favorite pieces of orbital geography. Passing between the upper and lower heads of the medial rectus, as they come off the annulus of Zinn, are the two divisions of the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve, along with the nasociliary nerve. So the front door of this muscle is built like a gateway, and the motor supply to most of the orbit walks straight through it. One more detail while we are at the apex. The annular ring is not free floating. It is blended with the dural sheath of the optic nerve, the tough sleeve of covering that the nerve carries from the brain with it into the orbit. That fusion means the origins of the rectus muscles and the optic nerve are mechanically tied together at the apex. When the eye turns, the whole complex at the apex is under gentle tension. Anatomists have long noted that this closeness explains a classic clinical observation, pain on eye movement when the nerve sheath behind the eye is inflamed. The strings and the drum are fastened to the same frame.

Host: So the muscle starts at the apex by two heads, and then it has to get to the eye. Walk me along its course. What does the journey forward look like?

Guide: From the apex, the medial rectus runs forward in the horizontal plane, hugging the medial wall of the orbit all the way toward the eye. That medial wall, as we heard back in Season 1, episode three, Orbital Walls, is dominated by the lamina papyracea, the paper-thin plate of the ethmoid bone, with the ethmoid air sinuses sitting just behind it on the far side. So imagine this muscle running forward with a wall of paper on one side and the globe on the other. Between the muscle and the bone lies a thin layer of orbital fat and the periorbita, which is the lining membrane of the bone, so the muscle does not scrape directly on bone. It glides in a soft sleeve. The course is remarkably straight. Of all the rectus muscles, this one lies closest to the plane of the visual axis when the eye looks straight ahead, and it travels the whole distance without crossing any other structure. It is the simplest, most honest track in the orbit, and we will see that simplicity written into its action later.

Host: It sounds like a quiet corridor. But nothing in the orbit is truly empty. What are the neighbors along that corridor?

Guide: Two sets of neighbors matter. Look above the muscle first, and there runs the superior oblique, coming along the upper medial corner of the orbit on its way to the pulley called the trochlea, which we will explore in episode six. Near the front of the orbit the superior oblique passes above the medial rectus, so if you slide a probe forward along the upper border of our muscle, you meet its tendon. Now look into the wall itself, just above the muscle, in the angle where the medial wall meets the roof. There, in small canals, run the anterior and posterior ethmoidal nerves and vessels, branches on their way to the ethmoid sinuses and the nose. Surgeons use those little foramina as landmarks for distances inside the orbit, and the upper border of the medial rectus is the landmark they measure from. And behind the muscle, of course, lies the muscular cone with the optic nerve inside it, which episode one mapped for us. Our muscle forms part of the cone's medial wall. It is both a mover of the eye and a wall of the room the optic nerve lives in.

Host: Now for the measurements, because this series lives on real numbers. How big is this muscle?

Guide: The medial rectus is the largest of the four recti, and the numbers back that up. Its total length from origin to insertion is about forty point eight millimetres, a little over forty millimetres, which is mid-range among the recti. What makes it the giant is not length but bulk. It has the widest belly, the greatest cross-sectional area, and therefore the strongest pull of the four straight muscles. Compare that with its tendon, because the contrast is beautiful. The tendon of the medial rectus is the shortest of the four, only about four millimetres long. So you have the strongest muscle in the group attached to the shortest cable, a short, wide, powerful strap driving straight at the globe. Remember that combination, big muscle, short tendon. It is one more clue that this muscle is built for frequent, forceful, reliable work rather than long delicate sweeps.

Host: And that brings us to the insertion, the business end. Where exactly does this muscle attach to the eye?

Guide: Here is where we need one word from Series 1, and we will reference rather than re-teach. In an episode called The Front of the Eye, we met the limbus, the border zone where the clear cornea shades into the white sclera, visible from the outside as the gray ring at the edge of the colored iris. The limbus is our measuring line for all rectus insertions, because you can find it on any patient with a slit lamp or even a good torch light. The medial rectus inserts into the sclera five and a half millimetres behind that limbus. Hold that number, because it is the smallest distance of the four recti. No other rectus comes closer to the cornea. The insertion is a gently curved, almost vertical line, just over ten millimetres from one end to the other, sitting on the medial side of the globe. So the strongest muscle in the orbit attaches nearest the front window of the eye, on the side toward the nose. Every part of that sentence is clinically useful.

Host: Why does the distance from the limbus matter so much? Five and a half millimetres is a small number. What does it buy us?

Guide: Three things. First, orientation. Each of the four recti has its own insertion distance, and the medial rectus, at five and a half millimetres, sets the near end of that family. If you lay the four distances out in order, they do not form a straight line across the globe but a spiral, winding further and further back as you go from muscle to muscle. That is the famous spiral of Tillaux, and we will assemble it properly, with all four numbers in place, later this season in episode ten, Anatomical Basis of Eye Movements. Second, the number matters because the limbus is visible. A surgeon planning surgery on this muscle measures from a landmark they can actually see through the conjunctiva, so the insertion distance becomes the starting coordinate for every measurement on the operating table. And third, proximity itself is power. Because its insertion sits closest to the cornea, this muscle has the longest stretch of sclera behind it to work with, which is one anatomical reason why the most commonly performed weakening procedure in eye muscle surgery, recession of the medial rectus, has so much room to move.

Host: You mentioned gliding and sleeves a few minutes ago. Let us stay on the muscle's surface now. What covers it, and what holds it in place on its journey?

Guide: Every rectus muscle carries a connective tissue jacket, and the medial rectus is no exception. The whole globe sits inside Tenon's capsule, a thin envelope of fascia that lines the orbit and separates the eye from the fat around it. Each rectus muscle pierces the back of that capsule and continues forward inside a sleeve of the same tissue, so the muscle never touches the globe directly. From that sleeve, thin sheets called intermuscular septa stretch sideways to the neighboring muscles, tying the four recti into one suspended unit rather than four loose strings. And there is one more strap with its own name. From the sheath of the medial rectus, a band of connective tissue passes back toward the medial orbital wall and attaches near the posterior lacrimal crest, the bony ridge behind the tear sac. That band is the medial check ligament, and its job is written into its name. It acts as a brake, limiting how far the eye can turn outward, away from the nose, by putting elastic tension on the medial rectus sleeve. The lateral side has its own check ligament, and the pair of them are the reason the straight gaze of each eye has such firm elastic boundaries.

Host: A brake on one side, and you said the muscle glides in a sleeve. That brings us to your fourth stop, the pulley. What exactly is a pulley here, when there is no bone ring like the trochlea involved?

Guide: This is one of the most beautiful rediscoveries in modern ocular anatomy, so let me define it carefully. Each rectus muscle passes through a pulley of its own, a ring or sleeve of dense connective tissue, rich in collagen and elastin and laced with smooth muscle, that is suspended in the orbit in front of the equator of the globe, roughly ten millimetres behind the limbus for the medial side. The pulley is anchored by fibrous bands to the periorbita of the orbital wall and linked to the pulleys of neighboring muscles. The muscle belly does not insert at that ring. It slides through it. So when the medial rectus contracts, the front end of the muscle, the tendon and the insertion, swings the eye inward, while the belly rolls backward through its pulley like a rope through an eyelet. Think of a fishing rod with rings along its shaft. The line does not attach to each ring. The rings simply control the direction the line pulls from. The rectus pulleys are the rings of the orbit, and they are the reason a muscle running along the wall can pull on the globe along a clean, predictable line.

Host: So the pulley decides the effective direction of pull. Does the medial rectus pulley have any special features of its own?

Guide: It does, and they suit its character. The medial rectus pulley is one of the most firmly anchored of the four, tied strongly to the periorbita of the medial wall, which suits a muscle that spends its life under steady tension. It is also woven into a network of connective tissue bands that couple it to the pulleys of the muscles above and below it, the superior oblique side and the inferior oblique side, so the four pulleys move as a coordinated system rather than as free-floating rings. The practical idea to carry away is this. The pulley works as the muscle's functional origin of pull, sitting closer to the globe than the annulus of Zinn is. The anatomical insertion is on the sclera, five and a half millimetres behind the limbus, but the effective line of force is set at the pulley. How that changes when pulleys shift, and what that means for the strange A and V patterns you will meet in strabismus clinics, belongs to episode ten of this season, where the whole pulley theory story is assembled. Today we simply establish that the ring exists, where it sits, and what holds it.

Host: Let us feed the muscle now. Where does its blood come from?

Guide: The arterial supply comes from the ophthalmic artery, the first big branch of the internal carotid artery inside the orbit, which we will study in its own right in a later season. As it passes through the orbit, the ophthalmic artery gives off muscular branches, traditionally grouped into a superior and an inferior muscular branch. The inferior muscular branch supplies the medial rectus along with the inferior rectus and the inferior oblique. From the muscle, the story continues forward in a way every optometrist should know. The recti give off the anterior ciliary arteries, which run with the tendons across the front of the globe. The medial rectus contributes two of these anterior ciliary arteries to the front of the eye. They ultimately join the circulation of the ciliary body and iris, the anterior segment of the eye. Venous blood leaves the muscle through veins that drain into the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins and out through the apex. So this muscle is not just a mover. It is a bridge that carries blood from the orbit to the front of the eye, and it is one of the reasons eye muscle surgery demands respect for the front-of-eye circulation.

Host: And now the fifth stop, the nerve. Which nerve drives this muscle, and where exactly does it go in?

Guide: The medial rectus is supplied by the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve, the same nerve whose two divisions we saw passing between the muscle's two heads at the apex. The inferior division runs forward below the optic nerve, and its branch to the medial rectus is short, because the muscle is right there waiting for it. The nerve enters the ocular surface of the muscle, the side facing the globe, and it enters at a predictable address. The rule for all four recti is that the motor nerve enters at the junction of the posterior third and the anterior two thirds of the muscle, on the deep ocular surface rather than on the cone side. For the medial rectus, that point works out to about fifteen millimetres behind its insertion, which places it roughly twenty millimetres behind the limbus. Write those two numbers down together. Fifteen from the insertion, about twenty from the limbus. They are among the most useful coordinates in the whole muscle, and here is why they matter.

Host: Yes, tell me why. Why does the exact entry point of a nerve into a muscle matter to anyone who is not cutting into it?

Guide: Two reasons, one surgical and one diagnostic. The surgical one first. Every extraocular muscle operation is planned around the nerve entry point, because tissue behind that point must not be disturbed. When a medial rectus is recessed, the muscle is detached near its insertion and reattached further back on the sclera, and the sutures and dissection have to stay comfortably in front of the point where the inferior division enters, about fifteen millimetres behind the insertion. Go hunting behind that line and you risk the nerve itself, and a medial rectus that has lost its nerve is an eye that no longer turns inward. The diagnostic reason is quieter but just as satisfying. Notice how short the nerve branch is, entering almost directly from below the optic nerve into the muscle, deep inside the cone, cushioned by fat and tucked between the other muscles. Anatomy like that is protection. An isolated paralysis of the medial rectus alone is genuinely rare, precisely because its nerve branch spends almost its entire length hidden inside the muscular cone. When an isolated medial rectus palsy does appear, the anatomy tells you to look hard for local trouble.

Host: One structural question I have been saving. The recti share so much, but do they all share the same fascial ties to the oblique muscles? Is our muscle woven into that same fabric?

Guide: No, and this is a genuine distinction worth having. The two vertical recti are famous for their fascial couplings. The sheath of the superior rectus connects upward to the levator palpebrae above it, which is why the upper lid follows the eye, and the inferior rectus is coupled forward to the inferior oblique through a hammock of connective tissue. The horizontal pair, the medial and lateral recti, carry no such attachments to the oblique muscles. The medial rectus runs its course tied only to its wall, its sleeve, its check ligament, and its pulley. That independence is anatomically consistent with everything else we have said today. This is the straightest, purest track in the orbit. It runs in one plane, it pulls in one direction, and it is connected to the minimum machinery needed to do that one job well. What that simplicity means for its action, we will say in one breath now, and in full detail later.

Host: Then give me that one breath. What does the medial rectus do, in anatomy's own words?

Guide: One word, adduction, turning the eye inward toward the nose, and it does that alone. Because its course lies in the horizontal plane and its pull runs nearly parallel to the visual axis when the eye looks straight ahead, it has no meaningful secondary or tertiary action. No elevating, no depressing, no twisting. Just a pure horizontal swing inward. We met the purpose of that movement in Series 1, in the episode called Convergence, where we saw both medial recti working together to bring the eyes onto a near target, hour after hour of reading and screen work. That constant workload is the anatomical explanation for everything we have measured today. The biggest belly among the recti, the shortest tendon, the nearest insertion, the firmly anchored pulley. This is a muscle engineered for its job. The full story of actions, primaries, secondaries, and the fields of action of every muscle, is episode nine of this season, Extraocular Muscle Actions, and it will be waiting for you with all four recti on the table.

Host: Let us bring it back to the chair, because our listeners examine eyes, they do not dissect them. When an optometrist checks eye movements on a patient, what part of what we described today is actually being tested?

Guide: The visible outcome of all this anatomy. We covered the examination methods themselves in Series 1, in Ocular Motility and Cover Testing, so today I only want to connect the anatomy to what you see. When you ask a patient to follow your target toward their nose, you are watching the medial rectus of each eye carry the globe through the whole arc of adduction, and because the muscle's pull is pure and horizontal, the arc should be smooth, full, and free of vertical wobble. Any restriction of that inward movement makes you think along the muscle's length, from a tight sleeve or heavy check ligament near the front, to a trapped muscle along the medial wall, to trouble at the apex where the nerve enters. The anatomy gives you the map of the questions. The episode you already heard gives you the method. That is how this series is meant to work, layer by layer.

Host: Before we close, one more piece of practical anatomy. We keep talking about the muscle cone. Why does it matter that this particular muscle lives inside it?

Guide: Because the cone is where the anesthesia goes. When eye surgery needs the whole globe still and painless, an anesthetic can be placed inside the muscular cone behind the eye, the approach we sketched in episode one, and the medial rectus is one of the large targets that region delivers. Its bulk, its position as a wall of the cone, and the short course of its nerve all follow from the same geometry. And there is a quieter lesson in the cone for every optometrist. The apex is a crowded room. Nerve branches, vessels, the optic nerve, and the origins of four muscles all share a space smaller than a fingertip, which is why the two-headed gateway of our muscle and the nerves marching between its heads are not trivia. They are the reason lesions at the apex tend to be messy and to involve several structures at once. Anatomy always explains the clinical pattern. You just have to walk the corridor slowly, the way we have today.

Host: Then let us walk it one last time, slowly, and collect the numbers. Guide, give me the medial rectus in five landmarks, the way you promised at the start.

Guide: With pleasure. Origin, the annulus of Zinn at the apex, by two heads from the medial part of the ring, with the two divisions of the oculomotor nerve and the nasociliary nerve passing between them. Course, about forty millimetres forward along the medial wall, gliding on the periorbita behind a thin veil of fat, with the lamina papyracea and the ethmoid sinuses beyond it. Insertion, five and a half millimetres behind the limbus, the closest of the four recti, on a curved vertical line just over ten millimetres wide, with the shortest tendon in the group, about four millimetres, in front of it. Pulley, a collagen and smooth muscle sleeve roughly ten millimetres behind the limbus, firmly anchored to the medial wall and linked to its neighboring pulleys. Nerve entry, the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve, entering the ocular surface at the junction of the posterior third and the anterior two thirds, about fifteen millimetres behind the insertion. Five landmarks, five numbers, one muscle. That is the medial rectus.
