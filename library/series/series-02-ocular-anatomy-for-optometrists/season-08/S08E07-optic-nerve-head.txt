# Description: Today Focus Cast by focuslinks goes beneath the surface of the optic disc and rebuilds the optic nerve head zone by zone. Meet the surface nerve fibre layer where more than a million axons turn toward the brain, the prelaminar zone with its astrocytic columns and border tissue, the laminar zone where the bundles pass through the scleral canal, and the retrolaminar zone where myelination begins and the nerve nearly doubles in diameter. Then the physiological cup and the neuroretinal rim are taken apart in cross-section, from the funnel wall to the laminar dots of a deep cup floor. A zone-by-zone episode for optometry students and clinicians who want real names, real numbers and plain words.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Series 2, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, in the season called Retina, Choroid and Optic Nerve, and this is episode seven, titled Optic Nerve Head. Last week, in episode six, Bruch's Membrane, we finished the thin boundary on which the retina rests, which means the sensor itself is now fully mapped. What remains is the structure that season seven promised us and deliberately left locked. Back in episode ten of that season, Optic Disc, we measured the visible pink oval, its margins, its rim, its cup and its ratios, and we were told the deep structure was waiting for a later episode. Today that debt comes due. As ever, my guide is here to walk us through it. Where do we begin?

Guide: We begin under the roof. Everything you saw in season seven, the pink oval with its pale centre, is only the visible cap of a much deeper structure. The optic nerve head, the point where the fibres of the retinal ganglion cells leave the eye, is a short cylinder about one millimetre deep, wedged into a hole in the back of the sclera. Anatomists slice that cylinder, mentally, into four stacked zones, and the names matter, so say them with me now. From front to back they are the surface nerve fibre layer, the prelaminar zone, the laminar zone, and the retrolaminar zone. Each floor has its own tissue, its own resident cells, and its own blood supply. Way back in Series 1, in the episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information, we met the optic nerve as the messenger cable carrying visual messages inward. Today we open the doorway that messenger walks through, floor by floor, and then climb back up to take apart the cup and the rim in cross-section, because the cross-section is where all four floors become visible in the living fundus.

Host: Before we descend, set the stage geometrically. You said a hole in the sclera and a cylinder sitting in it. Give me the frame, the canal and the walls, so that the four floors have a building to sit in.

Guide: The hole is the posterior scleral foramen, and it is small, roughly one and a half millimetres across, sitting about three millimetres nasal to the fovea, where we located it in season seven. Through it runs a short canal within the scleral substance, and the optic nerve head fills that canal from the retinal surface to the retrobulbar space. So picture a narrow chimney, about one millimetre deep and one and a half millimetres wide, punched through the eye's tough outer coat. The roof of the chimney, lying flush with or slightly proud of the surrounding retina, is what the ophthalmoscope greets as the disc. The walls are sclera, modified at the rim of the canal into the firm ring that anchors everything we will describe today. The canal need not run straight. In some eyes it is oblique, the nerve enters on a slant, and that is the anatomical root of the tilted discs and crescents you already met in the myopic fundus back in season seven. Inside the chimney, roughly a million axons are packed shoulder to shoulder, turning and diving on their way to the brain.

Host: Take me to the roof first. Floor one, the surface nerve fibre layer. What is actually there?

Guide: The surface nerve fibre layer is the innermost sheet, and it is simply the continuation of the nerve fibre layer of the retina, the thin innermost highway of ganglion cell axons we met in season seven, sweeping over the disc. Here those axons perform the turn of their lives. They arrive running flat, parallel to the retinal surface, and then they bend through close to a right angle and dive toward the scleral canal. The surface layer is that bend made visible. Its top is covered by a delicate glial membrane called the internal limiting membrane of Elschnig, a thin basement membrane laid down by astrocytes, the star-shaped supporting cells of the central nervous system, and astrocytic tissue threads between the axon bundles all the way down. There is often a trace of development here too. In the embryo a tuft of glial tissue called the Bergmeister papilla wraps the hyaloid artery at the disc, and we traced that artery's canal through the vitreous back in season seven, in episode five, Vitreous Chambers and Spaces. Occasionally a wisp of that glial veil persists over the disc in adults as an epipapillary veil, and now you know its family name. Look once more at this floor with the living fundus in mind. The retinal blood vessels run across the inner retina just deep to it, but the surface layer itself is the roof of the nerve head, and it is here that the retinal nerve fibre layer reaches its greatest thickness, as all the bundles crowd toward the canal. In discs with a shallow cup this layer simply bridges the centre, and in discs with a deeper funnel it sweeps around the rim. The surface layer, in short, is where the shape you measure as the cup begins.

Host: I want to slow down on that turn, because it still surprises me. The retina is a flat sheet. The nerve is a round cable. Where exactly do the fibres go when the flat world ends?

Guide: Into a funnel. The fibres that arrive nearest the disc bend first, with the gentlest curve, and they enter the disc most superficially, right at the surface. The fibres from the far periphery cannot simply bend ninety degrees on the spot. They run deeper within the retinal nerve fibre layer, sweep around the cup in a long arc, and leave through the more peripheral parts of the canal. So inside the nerve head there is orderly layering, fibres from near the disc occupying the superficial layers, fibres from far away passing deeper and more peripherally. The full traffic map, the arcuate bundles, the papillomacular bundle, and the way the entire retina sorts itself at this one doorway, is its own story, and it comes in episode ten of this season, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship. For today, one consequence matters. The surface layer you see at the disc is built from the shortest arrivals, the fibres arising in the retina immediately around the disc, and that is why the surface shows those fine striations, the visible edges of bundles fanning out across the disc like combed hair.

Host: And the plumbing of floor one? Something has to feed the busiest doorway in the eye.

Guide: The surface layer is fed by the retinal circulation. Its capillary network is derived from branches of the central retinal artery, the same supply that nourishes the inner retina all around it, and back in episode one of this season, Retinal Blood Supply, we followed that artery from the ophthalmic artery, down the middle of the optic nerve, out of the cup, and into the four arcades. So the roof of the nerve head is, in vascular terms, retinal territory. Keep that in mind, because within a few hundred microns the supply will change under our feet, and the border between the two circulations runs straight through the nerve head. That border is one of the most useful facts in this entire episode.

Host: Floor two then. Descend. What is the prelaminar zone?

Guide: The prelaminar zone runs from the underside of the surface layer down to the lamina cribrosa, and here the character of the tissue changes. The axons are gathered into distinct bundles, columns of nerve fibres separated by columns of astrocytes, like cables threaded through a sponge. Around the outside of the whole cylinder runs a glial sleeve that separates the nerve head from its neighbours, and it carries two names worth knowing. The border tissue of Elschnig is the inner part, continuous with the internal limiting membrane of the retina around the disc, and the border tissue of Jacoby is the outer part, extending deeper, separating the head from the choroid and reaching into the scleral canal toward the lamina. Together they wall the nerve head off from retina and choroid with glia rather than leaving raw contact. And one more signature of this floor. The axons here are still unmyelinated, packed tight, so the tissue remains compact, translucent, and pink with capillaries when you look at the living disc. Note also what is absent. The other nine layers of the retina stop at the disc margin, held back by that border tissue, so only the fibre highway and its astrocytes continue into the head. The nerve head, in other words, is not a piece of retina stuck in the wall. It is the retina's output, pure and simple.

Host: You said unmyelinated as though it were a rule with a reason. In the rest of the nervous system, long axons get insulation. Why is the eye's own stretch of this nerve bare?

Guide: Two reasons, one optical and one mechanical. Optically, myelin is fatty and opaque. If a million axons arrived at the disc wearing full insulation, you would have a white, light-scattering plug sitting in the middle of the light path, and the eye cannot afford that inside its clear media, the transparent interiors we have defended season after season. So myelination is held off until the axons are safely out of the light. Mechanically, the bare state keeps the nerve head slim enough to fit through a canal of about one and a half millimetres. When myelination does begin, and we reach that in floor four, the nerve nearly doubles in girth, and there is no room for that inside the globe. Occasionally the rule breaks, and myelin starts earlier than it should, spilling onto the retinal nerve fibre layer as a white opaque patch contiguous with the disc, the entity called myelinated nerve fibres. When you see it, you are looking at the normal sequence running a few millimetres too soon, nothing more mysterious than that.

Host: And the blood supply of floor two? I suspect this time it is not the retinal circulation.

Guide: Correct, and this is the hinge of the whole episode. The prelaminar zone is supplied mainly by the short posterior ciliary arteries, the family of small arteries we mapped in episode three of this season, Choroidal Circulation, as they sprint from the ophthalmic artery to the back of the eye. Their branches feed the peripapillary choroid, and from there, and from the ciliary territory within the scleral ring around the canal, capillaries reach the prelaminar bundles. The central retinal artery contributes essentially nothing to this zone. So the roof of the nerve head eats from the retinal plate, and the floor beneath it eats from the ciliary plate, and the watershed between the two runs right through the tissue. If you remember one sentence from today, remember that one, because it explains a great deal of what the disc does when it is stressed. Notice also how narrow the supply is here. An enormous axonal traffic runs on a handful of tiny ciliary branches, which is why anatomists have long flagged the nerve head as one of the eye's most finely balanced plumbing districts, a busy crossing fed by small pipes.

Host: Floor three. The laminar zone. I know this one has an episode of its own coming, so give me its place in the stack, not the full tour.

Guide: Agreed, the full tour waits for episode eight, Lamina Cribrosa, and it deserves it. The laminar zone is where the axon bundles pass through the lamina cribrosa, the sieve-like plate of connective tissue that bridges the posterior scleral foramen. The lamina is continuous with the sclera, essentially the sclera itself modified into a mesh, and its pores carry the bundles out of the eye in ordered groups. At its centre runs a wider channel through which the central retinal artery and vein enter and leave the nerve. Structurally it is the floor of the cup and the ceiling of the nerve proper, the load-bearing wall between the pressurised eye behind it and the brain ahead of it. Its blood supply comes from the circle of Zinn and Haller, an anastomotic ring formed by branches of the short posterior ciliary arteries within the sclera around the canal, with help from recurrent branches of the choroidal circulation. The beams, the pores, the regional differences, and the anatomy of why certain sectors of it matter more than others, all of that is next week. Today it takes its place as floor three of four. One positional note before we move on. The central channel through the lamina usually sits slightly nasal of the disc's centre, and the beams around it knit into the scleral ring in front and into the connective septa behind, so the sieve is stitched into the wall of the eye on both faces. Episode eight will pull on those stitches one by one.

Host: Floor four. Past the sieve. What changes at the retrolaminar zone?

Guide: Here the axons finally receive their insulation. Just behind the lamina cribrosa, oligodendrocytes, the myelin-forming glial cells of the central nervous system, wrap the fibres in myelin sheaths, and the diameter of the nerve jumps from about one and a half millimetres to about three. Connective tissue septa, thin sheets of collagen continuous with the coverings around the nerve, divide it into roughly a thousand fascicles, each carrying its share of the axon traffic. From here backward the optic nerve is genuinely brain substance, a tract of the central nervous system rather than a peripheral nerve, and we will follow its four segments and its three sheaths properly in episode nine, Optic Nerve Structure. For today, floor four is one sentence. Behind the sieve the fibres are myelinated, the nerve doubles in diameter, and the territory of the brain begins. And the myelin does more than add girth. It is insulation, and insulation is speed, so from this floor backward the visual messages travel in the fast lane, at the pace that brain tracts demand.

Host: Let me hold on that doubling for a second. One and a half to three millimetres is not a small adjustment. Why does that jump matter anatomically?

Guide: Because everything behind the globe is sized for the fat version of the nerve, and the eye is sized for the thin one. The bony optic canal at the apex of the orbit, which we toured back in episode four of season one, Orbital Openings and Communications, admits the myelinated nerve together with its coverings. The scleral canal admits only the bare trunk. The gap between those two facts is the retrolaminar zone, and it is also why the sheaths and the nerve behind the globe have room to expand in a way the intraocular portion never does. Anatomy keeps a budget, and myelin is the largest single line item on the nerve's account.

Host: Feed floor four. Who supplies the retrolaminar segment?

Guide: The retrolaminar zone lives mainly on the pial plexus, a network of fine vessels running in the pia mater, the innermost of the nerve's coverings, fed by recurrent branches from the ophthalmic artery and by contributions from the short posterior ciliary system. And threading the very centre of the floor, embedded in the nerve like a wire inside a cable, are the central retinal artery and vein. They pierce the lower surface of the nerve about a centimetre behind the globe and run forward in its core to reach the disc. The venous blood of all four floors, in the end, drains overwhelmingly into the central retinal vein. So even the floors fed by the ciliary system empty through the retinal door. And as a crossover curiosity, in roughly a quarter of eyes one branch of the short posterior ciliary system reaches the retina directly, arriving at the disc edge as a cilioretinal artery, living proof that the two territories sit side by side at the nerve head.

Host: Then let us climb back up to the surface, because the last part of today belongs to the cup and the rim. In season seven you gave us the ratios and the rules. Today take the cup apart. In cross-section, what is the physiological cup?

Guide: The cup is the shallow central depression of the disc, and the first thing to say is that it is a shape, not a hole. Nothing is missing. The fibres passing through the disc have funnelled around it, the way water parts around a stone in a stream. Its wall is the sloping mass of turning axons, the surface and prelaminar tissue bending around the central space, and in deeper cups its floor is the front surface of the lamina cribrosa itself, skinned over by a thin layer of astrocytes. Around it runs the neuroretinal rim, the ring of neural tissue you measured in season seven. In section, the rim is the inner edge of that funnel seen end on, the crowded tops of all the arriving fibres, laced with the capillaries that give it the pink. So the cup has a wall of axons, a floor of lamina, and a rim of axons, and the cup-to-disc ratio you learned is simply a measure of how much of the disc's face the funnel occupies and how much remains as living neural ring. Often the cup sits a shade temporal of the disc's geometric centre, echoing the slimmer temporal rim. And in the average healthy disc the funnel occupies well under half of the disc's face, which is simply another way of reading the ratio you already own, as a statement that most of the arriving axons remain visible as rim.

Host: That is a useful upgrade, from a number to a section. So what does the anatomy add that the ratio alone cannot tell you?

Guide: Three things. First, depth. Two discs can carry the same ratio while one funnel descends further back toward the lamina, and the floor you can see tells you how deep the funnel really goes. Second, the disc-size context you already own from season seven, that large discs carry larger cups and remain physiological, now makes sense in section, because a wide funnel in a wide chimney still leaves the same healthy neural ring around it. Third, the vessels. The central retinal artery and vein emerge at the centre of the cup and divide into their superior and inferior trunks right there, and in a deep cup the trunks can be seen bending over the rim as they climb out of the funnel. A cup, in other words, is not only a diameter. It is a three-dimensional structure with a floor, a vascular signature, and a rim you can interrogate in section, and every one of those features has an anatomical address in the four floors we walked today.

Host: In deep cups, examiners speak of small grey dots on the cup floor. What are those, anatomically?

Guide: Those are the laminar dots, and they are exactly what they look like. When the cup is deep enough that its floor is the lamina cribrosa, the pores of the sieve show through the thin astrocytic covering as grey punctate markings. Seeing them tells you the floor of the funnel has descended to lamina level, and it is one of the visual clues that a cup is genuinely deep rather than merely wide. They are also the perfect doorway into next week, because those dots are the ends of a structure with real architecture, beams that curve and thicken, pores that vary by sector, and a pattern of strength and weakness that is anything but uniform. We will name that architecture properly in episode eight.

Host: One gap in the map before we move to the rim. What about a disc with no visible cup at all? Season seven said the cup is present in most but not all healthy eyes. What is the anatomy of that exception?

Guide: In a small disc the exception is easy anatomy. If the canal and the disc are small, the axons exit through a flat, shallow funnel, and the surface layer may bridge the centre so completely that no depression shows at all. The rim then fills the whole face of the disc, the ratio reads zero, and the finding stays physiological as long as the rim is a healthy pink and the two eyes agree with each other. At the other extreme, the large disc carries its proportionally larger cup, the relationship you met in season seven. Both ends of that range are the same funnel anatomy scaled to different chimneys, which is why disc size always comes before cup judgement, never after.

Host: One more section view before we close. The rim in cross-section. Season seven gave us the ISNT pattern and its traffic explanation. Today, in the four-floor picture, what is the rim anatomically, and why is the temporal sliver so slim?

Guide: Anatomically, the rim is where the funnel wall meets the disc margin, the belt of surface and prelaminar tissue that carries the arriving axons, and its capillaries come from that same short posterior ciliary territory we placed in floor two. The temporal rim is slim because of the traffic, exactly as you heard in season seven. The papillomacular bundle, the compact stream from the macula, needs only a narrow doorway on the temporal side, while the arcuate streams from the temporal retina converge on the upper and lower poles and demand the widest gates. The full fibre geography, bundle by bundle, is reserved for episode ten of this season, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, so today we stop at the doorway widths. But hold the section in your head. The rim is not a coat of paint on the disc. It is the living cross-section of every axon the eye owns, and the four floors we walked today are exactly what is stacked inside it.

Host: Then give me the pearl of the day. Why did we bother slicing the nerve head into four floors at all? What does the zoning actually buy a clinician?

Guide: Two things. First, localisation. Each floor has its own blood supply, the surface from the retinal circulation, the prelaminar and laminar from the short posterior ciliary system through the choroid and the circle of Zinn and Haller, the retrolaminar from the pial plexus. That is why findings at the disc can point to different vascular territories, and why the nerve head is a meeting place of two circulations, each modest, with the border running through the tissue. Second, honest anatomy. Once you know the cup is a funnel with a lamina floor and the rim is the neural cross-section, every measurement you took in season seven stops being a number on a form and becomes a statement about real tissue. The zones are the difference between memorising the disc and owning it.

Host: Now the recap. Four floors, one breath each, and then the cup. Go.

Guide: Floor one, the surface nerve fibre layer, where the retinal axons turn from flat to vertical beneath the membrane of Elschnig, fed by retinal capillaries. Floor two, the prelaminar zone, where astrocytes sort the axons into bundles and the border tissue of Elschnig and Jacoby walls the head off, fed by the short posterior ciliary arteries. Floor three, the laminar zone, where the bundles pass through the scleral sieve, sustained by the circle of Zinn and Haller. Floor four, the retrolaminar zone, where myelination doubles the diameter and the pial plexus takes over. And threading all four floors, the cup, a funnel of axons with a lamina floor and a vascular signature, ringed by the rim, the neural cross-section of the entire retina.
