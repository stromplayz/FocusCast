# Description: At the bottom of the optic disc lies a miniature sieve, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks takes it apart beam by beam. We locate the lamina cribrosa in the posterior scleral foramen, count its pores and its bundles of ganglion cell axons, and examine the collagen, elastin, astrocytes and capillaries from which its beams are built. Then we map its famous regional variation, learning why the superior and inferior poles of the plate carry the thinnest beams and the least elastin, and what pressures the finished mesh must hold apart. A structure-first anatomy lesson for optometry students and clinicians who want the optic nerve head in full depth.

Host: Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists. We are deep in Season 8, Retina, Choroid and Optic Nerve, and today we reach episode eight, which leaves only two episodes in this season's account. Last week, in episode seventy-seven, Optic Nerve Head, we stood on the surface of the disc and looked down through the four zones of the nerve head, the surface nerve fibre layer, the prelaminar zone, the laminar zone, and the retrolaminar zone, like floors of a building seen through a skylight. Today we stop walking past the third floor. Today we go down inside it, because that laminar floor has a name of its own, the lamina cribrosa, and a story that deserves a full episode. It is, without exaggeration, one of the most elegant pieces of structural engineering in the entire eye. My guide is back with me, patient as ever. Take us down.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to everyone. Before we descend, let me set the ladder squarely against the wall, because everything today hangs on last week's map. In episode seventy-seven we divided the optic nerve head, from its visible surface down into the nerve behind, into four zones. The surface nerve fibre layer is the thin cap where the retinal axons arrive and turn. The prelaminar zone is the layer beneath it, where the nerve tissue is hugged by choroid rather than by sclera. The laminar zone is the floor of the whole structure, the perforated plate of connective tissue through which every axon actually leaves the eye. And the retrolaminar zone is the beginning of the true nerve behind the globe, which is next week's territory. Today belongs to the laminar zone alone. We will take its name, its position, its beams and pores, its materials, its blood supply, and the famous asymmetry between its upper and lower poles.

Host: Then the name is the right place to start, because it is one of the loveliest in all of ocular anatomy. Lamina cribrosa. Two Latin words set side by side. Say them slowly and they already sound like a description. What are they actually telling us, and does the structure itself live up to the poetry of its label?

Guide: The name is an honest label, which is rarer than you would think. Lamina means a thin plate or sheet, and cribrosa comes from cribrum, the Latin word for a sieve. So the lamina cribrosa is, literally, the sievelike plate, and the anatomy earns the name completely. Picture the round metal sieve in a kitchen drawer, a shallow pan punctured by hundreds of openings, still rigid enough to hold its shape while letting everything small pass through. That is what sits at the bottom of the optic disc. A small, tough, perforated plate, about as wide as the disc itself, pierced by openings that let the nerve fibre bundles through, and strong enough to carry the wall of the eye around them. Anatomy is full of poetic names given carelessly. This one is a photograph.

Host: One housekeeping question before we go further, because I know a certain kind of listener is already raising a hand. Is this the same lamina cribrosa that lives in the skull, up in the ethmoid bone? Same words, same thing?

Guide: Same words, different structure, and worth thirty seconds to keep the files separate. High in the nasal cavity, the ethmoid bone carries a plate with the same Latin name, pierced by tiny openings for the nerves of smell on their way up to the brain. Bone, up in the skull, serving the first cranial nerve. Ours is not bone at all. It is soft tissue, fibrous, made of the same collagen family as the sclera, and it sits at the back of the eye serving the second cranial nerve. The two plates even lie at opposite ends of the skull, one for smell and one for sight. So when you meet the name in a textbook, check the address before you file it. In this series, lamina cribrosa always means the optic nerve's sieve.

Host: Good, files separate. Now position. Where exactly does this plate sit, and what is it doing there? The sclera is a complete coat around the eye, is it not? So what happens at the posterior pole, where the optic nerve insists on leaving?

Guide: The sclera is indeed a complete coat, and the optic nerve needs a door through it. That door is the posterior scleral foramen, a round or slightly oval window at the posterior pole, measuring roughly one and a half to two millimetres across at the opening the lamina actually bridges. The lamina cribrosa is what fills that window, like a cork with holes in it. And here is the key idea, the one that makes everything else fall into place. It is not a foreign object dropped into the hole. It is modified sclera. The collagen of the sclera flows to the margin of the foramen and reorganises itself into this perforated plate, the way the deck of a bridge grows out of its abutments. You already met the collar where sclera meets the nerve head, the border tissue of Elschnig, back in episode seventy, Optic Disc. The lamina is the floor that hangs inside that collar.

Host: So the sieve and the wall are one continuous material, which I find quietly satisfying. Now take the mesh apart for me. When you say beams and pores, what exactly is a beam, and what exactly is a pore?

Guide: Think of the plate as woven from two elements that alternate with each other, solid and open, solid and open, all the way across. The beams are the solid part, straps of tough connective tissue, measured in tens of micrometres, many of them thinner than a human hair, crossing one another at every angle and welding themselves firmly to the sclera at the rim of the foramen. Between the beams lie the pores, the openings of the sieve. The human lamina carries in the region of three hundred pores, with counts from about two hundred to about four hundred reported from eye to eye, and each pore is occupied by a bundle of nerve fibres, so the sieve is fully woven, beam, then cable, then beam again. In the middle of the plate, one larger opening carries the central retinal artery and vein, whose wider course we mapped in episode seventy-one, Retinal Blood Supply. And behind the plate, the beams merge smoothly into the connective tissue septa that divide the nerve into bundles, a joining we will admire properly next week.

Host: Let us put numbers on the traffic, because that is where the awe lives. How many axons actually pass through this little window, and how crowded does that make the journey?

Guide: The standard figure is about one point two million axons, the outgoing fibres of the retinal ganglion cells, and we have quoted that number before in this series, back when we counted the disc's contents in episode seventy. Now push all of it through an opening about one and a half to two millimetres across, divided among roughly three hundred pores. That works out at bundles averaging several thousand fibres each, packed shoulder to shoulder, each bundle assigned to its own pore, like commuters sorted into separate doors. And remember that the plate is thin, only a few tenths of a millimetre from its upper surface to its under surface. By any honest measure this is the most crowded checkpoint in the eye, and every design feature of the lamina exists to serve that checkpoint.

Host: Which brings me to the beams themselves. You keep calling them straps of connective tissue. Walk me through the materials list. What are these beams actually made of?

Guide: The materials list is short and strong. First, collagen, several kinds of it, the same rope-like protein family that gives the sclera and the corneal stroma their tensile strength, laid down in dense bundles inside each beam. Second, elastic fibres, the springy component, which let the plate flex and return instead of cracking under load. Third, a ground substance of large sugar-protein molecules and adhesive proteins that binds the whole composite together. Then the cells. Fibroblasts maintain the connective tissue, laying down and renewing its fibres. And most distinctively, astrocytes, the supporting glial cells of the nervous system, line every pore and wrap each passing nerve bundle in a smooth cellular sleeve. So a pore is never a bare hole in collagen. It is a channel walled by living glia, with the cable running through the middle of it.

Host: You slipped something past me there, and I want to go back for it. Blood. Where do the vessels run in this plate, in the beams or in the pores?

Guide: In the beams, and this is one of the beautiful divisions of labour in the eye. The pores belong to the cables. The axon bundles have no vessels running inside their pores, because the pores are almost completely filled by the nerve fibres themselves, with no room left for plumbing. Instead, capillaries travel within the beams, embedded in the connective tissue, so that every bundle in every pore has a blood vessel lying alongside it, just across the glial sleeve. Nutrition does not have far to travel. Oxygen and glucose diffuse the short distance from a beam capillary, through the astrocyte lining, to the axons. So the finished design is a lattice of utilities wrapped around a lattice of cables, structure and plumbing in one material, wires in the other. Not one square micrometre of the plate is wasted.

Host: Then name the source. Which arteries feed those beam capillaries? I have a feeling this connects to territory we have already visited this season.

Guide: It connects directly. The feeders are the short posterior ciliary arteries, which we met properly in episode seventy-three, Choroidal Circulation. There are about ten to twenty of them, arising from the ophthalmic artery and running forward to the back of the eye, where many of them contribute to a small arterial ring embedded in the sclera around the nerve head, the circle of Zinn and Haller. From that ring, and directly from the short ciliary branches, small arterioles enter the lamina and run centripetally, inward toward the centre, travelling inside the beams themselves. Notice what that arrangement means. Each sector of the sieve is fed by its own little branch, so the plate has a segmental supply, sector by sector, like a building wired on separate circuits. Venous blood leaves mainly by the central retinal vein, travelling with the artery through the central opening of the plate.

Host: And the contrast with the zones we climbed through last week? In episode seventy-seven you said, almost in passing, that the very surface of the disc drinks from a different well. Refresh that contrast for me, because it completes the plumbing map.

Guide: Worth thirty seconds, yes, because the nerve head is a stack of three supply territories. The surface nerve fibre layer, the thin cap of the disc, is fed by small branches from the retinal circulation, the same arterioles that spread across the fundus. The prelaminar zone above the plate, and the laminar zone itself, are fed by the short posterior ciliary system we have just described, through the circle of Zinn and Haller. The retrolaminar zone below the plate is fed mainly by pial branches, vessels running in the sheath coverings of the nerve, with contributions from the central retinal artery in many eyes, and that story belongs to next week. So as you descend through the nerve head, you pass from retinal territory, into short posterior ciliary territory, into pial territory. Three supply systems stacked like floors in a building, meeting at the level of the sieve.

Host: Now the part of today I have been waiting for since you first said the word poles. Is this sieve uniform? The same beams everywhere, the same pores everywhere? Or does the plate vary from place to place?

Guide: It varies, and the variation is the most important structural fact about the lamina cribrosa. The classic teaching, backed by decades of careful microscopic study, goes like this. In the horizontal sectors, nasal and temporal, the beams are thick, densely cross-linked, and generously supplied with elastic tissue. At the superior and inferior poles, at twelve o'clock and six o'clock, the beams are fewer and thinner, they carry less elastin, the pores are larger, and the connective tissue is measurably sparser. The plate, in other words, is built like a bridge with two quiet corners, strongest through the middle of its span and thinnest at the top and bottom. And I want to underline this before anyone misfiles it. This is not a flaw found in unlucky eyes. It is the standard design of every normal lamina, present in healthy eyes everywhere.

Host: That is a strange thing for anatomy to do, though, is it not? Build a pressure-bearing sieve, and then thin it at two corners. Why would the poles be built weaker? Is there a reason, or is it just an accident of construction?

Guide: Anatomy does not design, it develops, so the honest answer is geometric rather than intentional, and it is a good geometry. The pores at the poles carry the arcuate fibre bundles, the great curved streams of axons that arise in the temporal retina and arch above and below the macula on their way to the disc, and those bundles are wide, so their pores run large. Where pores enlarge, the beams between them must thin, and where beams thin, less elastic tissue is laid down. Add the shape of the canal itself, and you get a plate whose support is least exactly where its span is widest. The full map of which retinal fibres pass through which pores is episode eighty's story, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, so I will hold the geography there. For today, keep the conclusion. The polar thinning is normal, reproducible, and universal.

Host: Connect this to the disc maps we already own. Episode seventy gave us the neuroretinal rim and its famous ordering. How does the floor map we just drew sit beside the rim map? Do the two agree?

Guide: Directly, provided you keep the two maps side by side and do not confuse them. Episode seventy, Optic Disc, gave us the rim map, the ISNT ordering, in which the neuroretinal rim, the ring of nerve tissue between the cup and the disc edge, is normally most generous inferiorly, then superiorly, then nasally, and most snug temporally. That map describes the tissue above the plate. Today's map describes the floor beneath it, and the floor is thinnest at the superior and inferior poles, exactly where the wide arcuate bundles funnel through their enlarged pores. So when you look at a disc, you are reading two storeys at once, a rim with its own normal geography, and a floor with another. Holding both maps in mind at the same time is precisely the anatomical habit this season has been quietly building.

Host: Here is a design question I cannot shake. Why holes at all? If the axons need protection and the plate needs strength, why not a solid sleeve with the bundles bundled inside it? What does the sieve format actually buy?

Guide: Three things, I think, and each worth naming. First, the obvious one. The axons physically must pass, all one point two million of them, so the plate must be traversable, and separate pores let the bundles travel in orderly, segregated files rather than one crushed crowd. Second, anchoring. The lamina is the union point between the nerve and the globe, the place where the cable is bolted to the wall, and beams radiating out to the scleral margin spread that anchor all around the foramen. Third, movement. The eye rotates constantly against its orbital surroundings, the nerve must follow without tearing, and a perforated plate flexes where a solid one would resist. The sieve, in short, is strong where strength is needed and compliant where movement is needed, and it manages both with the same material.

Host: You used the phrase pressure-bearing earlier, and I let it pass. I am not letting it pass now. What pressures does this plate actually separate?

Guide: Two different worlds. In front of the lamina is the inside of the eye, where the intraocular pressure normally sits at roughly ten to twenty millimetres of mercury. Behind it lies the orbit, where the tissue pressure is several millimetres of mercury lower. The lamina cribrosa is the dividing wall between those two numbers, the pressure-bearing floor of the eye, and a pressure difference acting on a flexible, perforated plate produces a very familiar mechanical result. The plate bows slightly backward, out toward the brain, and the beams are held under permanent tension, like the wires of a suspension bridge that never gets a day off. The collagen and elastin we catalogued earlier are the materials that carry that load, decade after decade. And a note of scope, because this series keeps its lanes. What happens to this plate when the pressures change, and how clinicians watch for it, belongs to disease discussions, not to today's anatomy.

Host: Understood, and thank you for holding the line. One more structural variable, then, and it is a familiar one from every waiting room. What happens to this sieve in a myopic eye, an eye that has been stretched long?

Guide: Stretching changes everything, because the lamina is made of the same collagen that stretches. In high myopia, where the axial length of the eye grows beyond about twenty-six millimetres, the posterior sclera remodels and thins, the scleral canal tends to enlarge, and the lamina cribrosa becomes thinner and more deeply bowed with it, its pores stretching and deforming as they go. This is also why myopic discs so often look tilted and oblique at the fundus, with the nerve entering the eye on a slant rather than straight on, and why the usual maps of the disc sit differently in those eyes. The sieve is not a separate organ that can hide from the wall around it. It is the wall, specialised, so whatever reshapes the sclera reshapes the plate.

Host: Here is the thought that keeps circling for me. Every visual message the brain will ever receive leaves the eye through this one plate. Back in Series 1 we followed the message itself, out of the eye and into the brain. Today we are standing at the doorway. Tell me how the two stories connect.

Guide: In Series 1, in an episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information, we treated the optic nerve as a cable and followed the traffic along it, from the retina toward the visual cortex. That was the right first story, the physiology of the message. Today we have been examining the doorway the cable must pass through, and the doorframe is astonishingly narrow. Every one of those one point two million fibres, carrying every grain of detail you will ever see, converges onto the disc, turns through a right angle, and files through the pores of this sieve, bundle by bundle, in the same ordered pattern in which the retina laid them down. And the physiological blind spot, which we placed on the field map in episode seventy-four, Photoreceptor Distribution, is simply the shadow of that doorway, the patch of retina where the plate occupies the ground and leaves no room for sensors.

Host: Now I want to see it. Shrink me down, put me on the plate, and walk me through the sieve from the inside, the way you would walk a student through a building on an open day.

Guide: Stand in the middle of the floor of the cup, and first notice the footing. Underfoot run the beams, pale and straplike, crossing at every angle like the cables of a suspension bridge seen from above, and tucked inside each strap, capillaries you cannot quite see. Toward one side, the twin channels of the central retinal artery and vein pass through the largest opening of the plate, one bright and one dark, the two rivers of the retina leaving home. Now walk toward the six o'clock edge, and feel the footing change. The beams thin beneath you, the pores widen, the tissue grows spare, like ice thinning as you leave the shore. The twelve o'clock corner is the same. And all around you, at every pore, the cables keep arriving from the retina, turning, and plunging through, one point two million of them, in patient file. That is the lamina cribrosa from the inside.

Host: Last practical question, and it is one the clinic will thank you for. When an optometrist looks into the eye with the ophthalmoscope, how much of this sieve can actually be seen?

Guide: A glimpse, and often a surprisingly good one. The lamina cribrosa is the floor of the cup, and in an eye with a deeper cup, more of the plate lies open to view at the bottom of the disc. What you see is a pale grey lattice, the beams catching the light and the pores sitting between them, a fine perforated texture at the base of the disc that many clinicians recognise on sight without always naming it. In some eyes, individual pores can even be made out as tiny grey openings across that floor. Two cautions keep the observation honest. The visible surface is only the upper face of the plate, not its full thickness, and how much of it you see depends on the depth and shape of the cup, which varies normally from eye to eye. But the anatomy is genuinely observable, not just diagram material.

Host: Then let us gather the episode into a pocket summary before we climb back out. If a student keeps only six facts about the lamina cribrosa from today, what should they be?

Guide: Six facts, in order. One, the lamina cribrosa is the sievelike, modified scleral plate that bridges the posterior scleral foramen, a window about one and a half to two millimetres across. Two, roughly three hundred pores, carrying bundles of the roughly one point two million ganglion cell axons, pass through a plate only a few tenths of a millimetre thick. Three, the beams are collagen and elastin, maintained by fibroblasts, sheathed around every pore by astrocytes, with capillaries running inside the beams and no vessels inside the pores. Four, the supply is segmental, from the short posterior ciliary arteries and the circle of Zinn and Haller, with drainage mainly by the central retinal vein. Five, the superior and inferior poles have the thinnest, least elastic beams and the largest pores, by normal design, and that is where the arcuate bundles pass. Six, the plate holds apart two pressures and bows under that duty, thinnest exactly where it must span widest. That is the whole machine.
