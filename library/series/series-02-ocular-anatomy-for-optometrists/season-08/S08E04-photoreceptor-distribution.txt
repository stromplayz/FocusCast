# Description: Where exactly do the retina's rods and cones live, and how many are there? In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we map both photoreceptor populations across the human retina, from the cone-packed, rod-free foveal centre to the rod-rich ring of the mid periphery and out to the sparse edge of the mosaic. You will hear the standard density numbers, learn why the optic disc is a true hole in the receptor sheet, and see how one anatomical map quietly explains the layout of the visual field. Made for optometrists and optometry students who want distribution anatomy, not phototransduction.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks. This is Series 2, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Season 8, Retina, Choroid and Optic Nerve, and this is episode four, Photoreceptor Distribution. Last time, in episode three, Choroidal Circulation, we walked the blood side of the outer retina, the short posterior ciliary arteries, the lobed carpet of choriocapillaris, and the four vortex veins that drain it. Today we stay at the back of the eye but change the instrument entirely. We put the blood away and take out a census sheet, because today we count cells. Where do the rods live? Where do the cones live? How tightly are they packed, and where does the mosaic simply stop? Back in Series 1, an episode called The Retina as the Sensory Layer introduced you to these two cell types as characters in the story of sight. Today is the anatomical sequel, the map edition. To guide us through it, my patient teacher is back. Welcome. Where do we begin?

Guide: We begin with the idea that there are two maps, not one. The retina carries two separate populations of light-catching cells, the rods and the cones, and each population has its own geography, its own capital city, its own empty countryside. They share one curved surface, they interleave in places, and yet their distributions are almost mirror images of each other. Our task today is topography, not chemistry. We are not asking how these cells turn light into a signal. We are asking where they are, how many there are, and what the pattern tells an optometrist looking at a living eye. By the end of this episode you will know the peak densities of both populations, the shape of their gradients across the fundus, and the one place where the map has a genuine hole, the blind spot. One habit to carry through the whole hour: every number tonight is a density, cells per square millimetre, not a total, and the two are different animals. Totals tell you the budget; densities tell you the map. We will move constantly between them, from whole-retina totals down to the tiniest patches at the centre of the fovea, and both scales matter.

Host: Before we can read the maps, sketch the two cells for me. Shapes and parts only. I do not need the chemistry, and I remember you promised we are not opening that door today.

Guide: Both cells are called photoreceptors, which simply means receptors tuned to light. Each one is a long neuron with two visible specializations stacked at one end. The inner segment is the engine room, crowded with mitochondria, and it carries the nucleus and the wiring that talks onward to the rest of the retina. The outer segment is the antenna, a modified cilium packed with flat stacks of membrane called discs, and it is the discs that hold the light-catching molecules. The rod outer segment is a slim cylinder, like a rolled poster, and the cone outer segment is shorter and tapers toward its tip, which is where the name cone comes from. That is all the anatomy we need tonight. The light-to-vision chemistry belongs to Series 1, in an episode we called From Light to Vision, and the interface these cells share with the pigment epithelium next door is next week's business.

Host: You called the rod a rolled poster and the cone a tapered one. How big are these cells, really? Give me a sense of scale before we start counting them by the million.

Guide: In width they are tiny and remarkably consistent. A rod holds a calibre of about two micrometres across, a fiftieth of a millimetre, and it keeps that slim figure wherever you find it in the retina. Cones are the flexible ones. At the foveal centre a cone is also only about two micrometres wide at its waist, but out in the far periphery a cone can run three or four times wider than its foveal cousin. In length they are impressive for such thin cells. A foveal cone, measured from the tip of its outer segment down to its synaptic wiring, can reach seventy or eighty micrometres, one of the longest cells in the retina, while rods run tens of micrometres in their outer segments. So picture the mosaic as a carpet of fine, closely woven threads, with the longest and finest threads woven into the very centre.

Host: Now, counting. Over a hundred million cells in a sheet thinner than paper. How does anyone actually know these numbers? Who counted them, and how?

Guide: Slowly, and with great patience. The classic counts come from human donor eyes. In nineteen thirty-five, a Danish investigator named Osterberg flattened pieces of human retina, stained them, and counted photoreceptors sample by sample, building the first serious density map. In nineteen ninety, Christine Curcio and colleagues repeated the project with modern methods and produced the reference maps that most textbooks still quote. The technique is called retinal flatmounting. You open the eye, spread the retina flat the way a globe is flattened into a wall map, sample small squares across the whole sheet, count the cells in each square under the microscope, and convert every count into a density, the number of cells per square millimetre of retinal surface. The human retina covers roughly a thousand square millimetres, so this is a huge survey with thousands of samples. Every number I give you tonight is a standard textbook value earned by someone counting cells one by one.

Host: Then give me the headline. How many photoreceptors are we carrying, and how do the two populations compare?

Guide: The classic census says about one hundred and twenty million rods and about six million cones. For every cone there are roughly twenty rods, a twenty to one ratio, and together they crowd into those thousand or so square millimetres, which makes the retina one of the densest neural sheets in the body. Hold that imbalance in your mind, because it shapes everything else tonight. The human retina is overwhelmingly a rod organ with a cone citadel built into one small region of it. Six million cones may sound like a fortune, but it is about five percent of the photoreceptor count. The other ninety-five percent, the great majority, are rods.

Host: Let us start with the cones then, the smaller population. Where exactly do they live, and where do they reach their greatest numbers?

Guide: Cones live where the finest work of seeing happens, which means they crowd into the posterior pole, the back-centre zone of the retina that holds the disc and the macula, and they reach their absolute peak at the very centre of the fovea. We walked the foveal pit itself back in Season 7, episode nine, Fovea and Foveola, so tonight I only need the floor of that pit. At its exact middle, the centre point called the umbo, the mosaic is cones and nothing else. From the centre out to a radius of about a third of a millimetre there are no rods at all. Zero. Not a few rods, no rods. That rod-free island is roughly a third of a millimetre across, which corresponds to about one degree of the visual field. Then, just beyond its rim, the first rods slip in between the cones, and they multiply fast as you walk outward.

Host: How tight is the cone packing at that peak? Give me the number, and then help me picture it.

Guide: The peak cone density at the foveal centre averages close to two hundred thousand cones per square millimetre. The classic mean figure is one hundred and ninety-nine thousand, and individual eyes ranged from about one hundred thousand to over three hundred thousand, so there is real human variation in this map. Now picture the space. A square millimetre is a pinhead-sized patch, and into it the foveal centre packs two hundred thousand cells. Each central cone is only about two micrometres across at its waist, and they are arranged in a hexagonal pattern, the way honeycomb tiles a plane, each cone ringed by six neighbours with the thinnest possible gaps between them. It is the tightest regular packing in the nervous system, and the payoff is direct. That centre point is where the retinal image is read with the finest grain the eye will ever possess.

Host: And the gradient away from the peak? Does cone density slide down gently, or does it fall?

Guide: It falls off a cliff. From the peak, cone density drops steeply within the first few degrees, so that by ten degrees out you have lost most of the height, and by the far periphery, near the ora serrata, the jagged front edge of the retina that we mapped in Season 7, episode six, Retina: Anatomical Overview, cone counts are down to a few thousand per square millimetre, thinner still at the extreme rim. Read that slope in reverse and you have the cone story in one sentence: nearly everything the cones have is concentrated in the last few degrees of the centre. But here is the misconception worth killing tonight, because even students who know the fovea well get this wrong. Cones never disappear completely. They are present across the entire retina, edge to edge, sparse in the periphery but always there. The periphery is not cone-free. It is cone-poor and rod-rich, and that difference in wording is the whole map.

Host: Which brings us to the rods, the majority population. Walk me across their map the same way. Where do they start, and where do they peak?

Guide: The rod map is almost the photographic negative of the cone map. At the foveal centre, rods are absent, as we said. Moving outward, rod density climbs steeply, faster than cone density falls, and it reaches its own peak not at the centre but in a ring around the centre, at an eccentricity of roughly fifteen to twenty degrees. Eccentricity is simply the word for angular distance from the foveal centre, so picture a donut of maximum rod density ringing the cone citadel. Peak rod density averages about one hundred and sixty thousand rods per square millimetre, with most eyes falling between roughly one hundred and thirty and one hundred and seventy thousand. From that ring, rod density declines slowly and gracefully all the way to the retinal edge, so the far periphery remains rod country, only thinner rod country. If the cone map is a great spike, the rod map is a crater with a raised rim.

Host: A crater with a raised rim. Why is the rod peak out at fifteen to twenty degrees instead of the rods simply packing in everywhere they can? What is the anatomical logic?

Guide: Partly real estate, partly design. The centre of the retina is already occupied by the cones, which own it completely, and the fovea is engineered around them, with the inner retinal layers swept aside so that light reaches those packed outer segments almost directly, the pit architecture we covered in Season 7, episode nine. The rods are excluded from the centre by simple crowding; there is no room left. But there is also a supply story worth connecting, because we laid the groundwork in this very season. Back in episode two, Retinal Circulation, we saw that the foveal centre is capillary-free, and in episode three, Choroidal Circulation, we saw the choriocapillaris lobules feeding the outer retina from behind. The central photoreceptors live on diffusion from that carpet. The rod ring, meanwhile, sits where the mosaic finally has room for long, bulky outer segments, with a generous supply underneath from both vascular beds. Anatomy rarely gives one reason. Here the geometry and the plumbing agree.

Host: Is the map perfectly symmetric around that ring, or do the quadrants differ from each other?

Guide: Not perfectly. The rod ring is not the same all the way around. The classic maps show the rod peak running a touch denser toward the superior and nasal side of the ring, and the whole ring is slightly elongated along the horizontal meridian, like a subtle streak, echoing the way the visual world is sampled. Cone counts also fall a little differently in different quadrants as you move away from the fovea. For working purposes, the textbook numbers I gave you are the honest averages, but it is worth knowing that the map has grain and drift in it, like any biological pattern, rather than being drawn with a compass.

Host: Before we leave the rods, I am curious how special this arrangement is. Is every retina in the animal kingdom built like ours, with a cone centre and a rod ring?

Guide: No, and the comparison sharpens the lesson. Distribution follows the life the animal lives. An owl, hunting in near darkness, carries a retina that is almost all rods, with hardly a fovea worth naming. An eagle, a daytime hunter, carries cone-rich retinas and famously has two foveas in each eye, one aimed forward for detail and one aimed sideways for the wide scan. The human design sits between those extremes and betrays our daylight ancestry: a cone citadel for sharp daylight work, and a twenty to one rod majority as the night shift. So the maps we are reading tonight are not arbitrary. They are the anatomical record of how our species sees, written by lifestyle, and our own lifestyle wrote both a citadel and a night shift.

Host: Let us make this clinical for a moment. An optometrist is not counting donor retinas at the slit lamp. Why does knowing this distribution matter when you are looking at a patient?

Guide: Because distribution is destiny for symptoms. When a patient reports that night vision is failing, or that they manage a bright room but struggle in a dim restaurant, the complaint localizes to rod real estate, the mid periphery where rods peak. When a patient says fine detail is fading in good light, the suspicion falls on the cone citadel. Series 1 did the experiential side of this in an episode called Peripheral Vision, and what anatomy adds tonight is the substrate underneath it. The sensitivity curve of the visual field, the way it falls from centre to edge, is in one sense the silhouette of these two density curves projected onto a bowl. The spike and the rim you now know as anatomy are the same spike and rim your field charts will draw. That is why I keep insisting the maps matter. They are the structure behind the numbers you order every week. And when that pattern is disturbed, the disturbance is worth listening to, because the photoreceptor mosaic is a fixed anatomical given, while the symptoms that walk through your door are its changing shadow.

Host: Now the hole in the map. Every anatomy student is told the eye has a blind spot, and most accept it as a quiz fact. Give me the anatomy behind it.

Guide: The hole is the optic disc, the place where the retina stops being a receptor surface and becomes a cable. Across the whole mosaic, the ganglion cells, the retina's output neurons, send their axons strung across the inner surface toward the back of the eye. There are roughly a million of these fibres, and every one of them has to leave the globe, yet they all leave through a single doorway. They converge on the disc, turn at right angles at its margin, and funnel out of the eye toward the brain. To make that doorway, the retina sacrifices its receptor sheet completely. There are no rods and no cones on the disc. Not sparse, absent. It is a true hole in the mosaic, and in field language it is an absolute scotoma, meaning an area where sensitivity is not merely reduced but gone. The seventeenth century scientist Edme Mariotte found it in the sixteen sixties, and the blind spot has quietly sat in every visual field since.

Host: Numbers and position, then. How big is this hole, where exactly does it sit relative to the fovea, and how large does it appear in the field?

Guide: Measure it on the retina first. The disc is an oval, slightly taller than it is wide, about one point nine millimetres vertically and about one and a half to one point seven millimetres horizontally, an area of roughly two and a half square millimetres. Its centre sits on the nasal retina about fifteen degrees from the fovea, which works out to roughly four to four and a half millimetres of surface distance, and it rides a little above the horizontal midline, by about one and a half degrees. Because the optics of the eye invert everything, that nasal position on the retina throws the hole into the temporal side of the visual field. In field terms, the physiological blind spot is an oval about five and a half degrees wide and seven and a half degrees tall, centred about fifteen and a half degrees temporal to fixation. So a two and a half square millimetre gap in the receptor sheet becomes a generous island of nothing in the field.

Host: Here is the everyday puzzle, then. There is a seven degree hole in each field, and yet nobody walks into a lamppost or notices a gap in the scenery. Why is the world seamless?

Guide: Two answers, one optical and one neural, and both are short. The optical answer is binocular overlap. The two eyes view the same world from slightly different angles, and the disc sits on the nasal retina of each eye, so the two blind spots land on different places in the field. What one eye misses, the other one sees, and we built that binocular story in Series 1 in an episode called Binocular Vision, so I will not re-pour that foundation. The neural answer is filling in, the visual system completing the background pattern across the gap, which is why the blind spot never presents itself as a black hole, only as a quiet continuation of whatever surrounds it. So keep the two layers separate in your mind. The anatomy is a genuine hole. The perception is a repair job running on top of it.

Host: That repair job is so good that people doubt the hole exists. Is there a practical way the hole proves itself, even inside a routine field test?

Guide: There is, and it is one of the elegant tricks built into modern field machines. Since the blind spot is an anatomical constant, perimetry uses it as a built-in check of fixation. The instrument flashes a bright stimulus exactly where the blind spot should be if the patient is looking where they were told. If the patient reports seeing it, the gaze has drifted, and the machine knows to distrust that moment. Series 1 walked the basics of the field chart in an episode called Basic Visual Field Testing, and now you know the anatomical secret the machine leans on. One bare oval, two and a half square millimetres of receptor-free retina, quietly working as the reference mark for every careful field printout.

Host: One caution flag before we close the disc. The rim, the cup, the disc margins, those are the daily bread of glaucoma assessment. Is that on tonight's menu?

Guide: Only at its edges, deliberately. The gross anatomy of the disc, the margins, the neuroretinal rim and the cup, we already handled at the end of last season, in Season 7, episode ten, Optic Disc. The deep structure of the nerve head is coming in this season, in episode seven, Optic Nerve Head, and the sieve-like plate the fibres squeeze through gets its whole episode in episode eight, Lamina Cribrosa. How the million fibres are bundled and arranged as they converge on the disc, the arcuate bundles and the papillomacular bundle, that is episode ten, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship. Tonight the disc matters to us purely as a hole in the receptor sheet, the one patch of fundus with nothing to catch light. Keep the frameworks separate, and each episode stays clean.

Host: One supply question to close the loop. The cone-only centre has no blood vessels over it, we learned that in episode two. So how do the most densely packed cells in the eye stay fed?

Guide: From underneath, by diffusion. Recall the arrangement from episode two and episode three. The retinal circulation stops short of the foveal centre, leaving that capillary-free zone, but the choriocapillaris, the dense carpet of fenestrated capillaries, fenestrated meaning riddled with tiny windows, lies directly behind the outer retina, separated from it only by the pigment epithelium and its basement membrane complex. The outer halves of the photoreceptors, including every one of those two hundred thousand foveal cones, sit bathed in fluid that crosses that interface. So the busiest real estate in the retina is fed from behind, across a barrier one cell thick. And that barrier, the retinal pigment epithelium, is exactly where the season goes next. Its structure is the bridge between the maps we drew tonight and the plumbing we did in the first three episodes.

Host: Let me try to hold the whole map in one picture. Can you narrate it for me in a single sweep, from the centre of the fovea out to the ora serrata?

Guide: Gladly. Stand first on the umbo, the exact centre. You are standing on nothing but cones, close to two hundred thousand per square millimetre, each one about two micrometres wide, honeycombed tight, with no rods among them and no vessels above them. Walk outward. Cones thin fast, rods appear within a few tenths of a millimetre and multiply, and by about fifteen degrees out you are on the crater rim, the rod peak, near one hundred and sixty thousand per square millimetre, running a little denser toward the upper and nasal side of the ring. Keep walking. Rods decline slowly, cones dwindle to a few thousand, and the mosaic grows coarse and sparse until the ora serrata ends it. And once on your walk you cross a bare oval about two and a half millimetres wide with no photoreceptors at all, the disc, where roughly a million fibres leave the eye. That is the whole map. Series 1 painted the experience of central seeing in an episode called The Macula and Central Vision; tonight you have met the cellular machinery underneath that experience.

Host: Recap time. Give me tonight's numbers one more time, quickly, like flashcards.

Guide: Here are the flashcards. One hundred and twenty million rods, six million cones, twenty to one. Foveal centre: cones only, rod-free for about a third of a millimetre, about one degree. Peak cone density: close to two hundred thousand per square millimetre, classic mean one hundred and ninety-nine thousand. Peak rod density: about one hundred and sixty thousand per square millimetre, in a ring at fifteen to twenty degrees eccentricity, slightly denser superior and nasal. Far periphery: cones down to a few thousand per square millimetre, rods still present but thinner. And the disc: no photoreceptors, about one point nine millimetres tall and one and a half to one point seven wide, sitting about fifteen degrees nasal and one and a half degrees above the midline, making a blind spot of about five and a half by seven and a half degrees, centred fifteen and a half degrees temporal to fixation. That is the census of the human retina.
