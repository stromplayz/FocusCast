# Description: How does one thin, dark coat feed half the retina, and where does all of its blood go? In this episode, Focus Cast by focuslinks walks the full choroidal circuit: the ten to twenty short posterior ciliary arteries ringing the optic nerve, the hexagonal lobules of the choriocapillaris with their arterial cores and venous rims, and the four vortex veins that carry the uveal circulation away by quadrants. Expect real textbook numbers, plain-language explanations, and the anatomical reasons optometrists care, from the equatorial watershed band to the oblique scleral canals that surgeons respect. This is episode three of the season on the retina, choroid, and optic nerve in the anatomy series for optometrists.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, series two, and we are deep in season eight, the season on the retina, choroid, and optic nerve. Over the last two episodes we followed the blood that feeds the innermost retina, first the central retinal artery and its arcades, then the fine capillary beds woven through the inner layers. Today we cross a border. Beneath the retina lies a second circulation, larger, faster, and stranger than anything we have met in this season so far, and it belongs to the choroid. We will meet the short posterior ciliary arteries, walk the lobular maze of the choriocapillaris, and end at the four vortex veins that drain the whole uveal tract by quadrants. As always, my guide is a patient teacher of anatomy. Welcome back.

Guide: Thank you, it is good to be here for this one, because this episode has been waiting a long time. Back in season five, episode nine, the episode we called simply Choroid, we mapped the coat's architecture: the suprachoroidea on the outside, the two vessel layers within, and the attachment running from the ora serrata at the front all the way to the optic nerve behind. At the end of that hour we deliberately stopped short of the plumbing. I promised that the circulation would get a day of its own, far enough ahead. Today is that day, episode seventy-three of the series. So bring the picture from that earlier hour back into your mind, a dark, spongy, thin-walled coat pressed against the inside of the sclera, and imagine it suddenly filling with blood. That filling is our journey today.

Host: Before we follow the pipes, place us. Not a re-lesson, just one breath to fix where the choroid sits among the coats of the eye.

Guide: One breath, then. Back in series one, in the episode called The Back of the Eye, we toured the rear of the globe and met the layers by name: retina, choroid, sclera, from inside out. In season five we gave the middle coat its true anatomy as part of the uveal tract, iris, ciliary body, and choroid, one continuous vascular sheet running from the front of the eye to the nerve at the back. Hold on to three facts only. The choroid is thin, about a fifth of a millimetre at the posterior pole. Its outer surface is separated from the sclera by the suprachoroidal space, a potential space, meaning a cleft whose two walls lie touching until fluid or blood forces them apart. And its innermost floor is a carpet of capillaries facing the retina. Today we trace where that blood enters, how it spreads, and how it leaves.

Host: Then start at the source. Where does all this choroidal blood actually come from?

Guide: From the ophthalmic artery, the first major branch of the internal carotid artery, the vessel we watched enter the orbit through the optic canal back in episode four of this series, Orbital Openings and Communications. Once inside, the ophthalmic artery sends a whole family of branches to the globe, and the branches that matter today are the ciliary arteries. There are two long posterior ciliary arteries, one medial and one lateral, and their long road forward to the iris and ciliary body we already walked in episode fifty, Complete Uveal Tract. The stars today are the short posterior ciliary arteries. Most textbooks count ten to twenty of them. They arise as one or two short trunks near where the ophthalmic artery crosses the optic nerve, then divide into that dozen or more branches, and they run a very short distance indeed before their work begins.

Host: Ten to twenty arteries is a crowd. Where exactly do they go, and why are they called short?

Guide: The name is simple geography. The long posterior ciliary arteries take the long road, forward along the inside of the sclera toward the front of the eye. The short ones take the short road. They cluster around the optic nerve and pierce the sclera obliquely within a few millimetres of it, in a tight ring of entries all the way around the nerve. Once through, they run forward in the perichoroidal space, that thin cleft between sclera and choroid we named in episode forty-nine, and from there they supply essentially the whole posterior choroid, the territory under and around the macula and the optic disc. Their branches reach forward toward the equator, where they eventually meet recurrent branches arriving from the anterior ciliary system, the frontier we will come back to. And notice, too, that like every ciliary vessel we will meet today, they enter through slanted tunnels in the sclera rather than straight holes, a small piece of engineering we will see again at the exits. So when you hear short posterior ciliary arteries, think posterior pole, think the back third of the eye.

Host: You said they crowd around the optic nerve. Do they do anything for the nerve itself at that meeting point?

Guide: They do, and it is one of the most elegant joins in the orbit. Just before or as they pierce the sclera around the nerve, several of the short posterior ciliary arteries give off side branches that link up with their neighbours and form a small circular anastomosis, a ring of joined streams, buried in the sclera around the optic nerve head. Anatomists call it the circle of Zinn-Haller, honouring two early describers. From that ring come vessels to the peripapillary choroid, meaning the choroid immediately surrounding the disc, and important feeders to the anterior part of the optic nerve itself. I will stop there, because the zones of the nerve head and their precise supply belong to episode seventy-seven, Optic Nerve Head. For us, the circle is a message: the short ciliary system is not a loose spray of twigs but an engineered network with junctions and ring mains.

Host: Is there ever a vessel that skips the border entirely, from this choroidal system straight into the retina?

Guide: There is, and you have probably photographed it. In roughly one eye in five, a branch of a short posterior ciliary artery, or of the circle of Zinn-Haller itself, sends a twig forward through the retina near the disc. That is a cilioretinal artery, a retinal-looking vessel with a ciliary pedigree. It behaves like a small retinal artery but it is fed by the choroidal system, not by the central retinal artery, and in some eyes it supplies a generous slice of retina between the disc and the fovea. Most of the time it is a harmless quirk that makes a fundus photograph distinctive. We will not chase the retinal circulation itself today; that was the business of episode seventy-one, Retinal Blood Supply, and episode seventy-two, Retinal Circulation. The point for today is that the short ciliary system is generous enough to lend an artery across the border.

Host: So the short ciliary arteries are in. Walk me through what happens once the blood is inside the wall of the eye.

Guide: The course is a funnel, wide at the outside and narrowing to its finest at the retina. The arteries run forward in the perichoroidal space, and as they go they send branches inward through the thickness of the choroid. The first floor they feed is the large vessel layer, Haller's layer, the one we named in episode forty-nine, full of thick-walled channels you could almost sew with. Their branches then climb to Sattler's layer, the middle stratum of vessels one size smaller. And finally each small arteriole turns inward and ends in the innermost carpet, the choriocapillaris. Each of those last feeders, one to a lobule, is narrow enough that a single plug can shut down the single hexagon it serves, which is a fact we will put to work in a moment. Picture three floors of plumbing stacked directly over one another, trunk arteries outside, medium vessels in the middle, capillaries against the retina, every floor fed from that ring of short ciliary entries around the nerve. The blood loses diameter at every step but gains surface area at every step as well.

Host: The choriocapillaris. Tell us what makes this capillary bed special, because it has a reputation.

Guide: Almost everything about it is exaggerated. A capillary anywhere else in the body has a lumen about eight to ten micrometres across, and a micrometre is a thousandth of a millimetre, so barely wider than a red blood cell. The capillaries of the choriocapillaris run twenty to fifty micrometres across, among the widest capillaries in the entire body, and beneath the macula they are at their widest and most crowded. They form a single flat sheet, one capillary thick, lying against Bruch's membrane. Their walls are paper thin and fenestrated, which means peppered with tiny round windows, each only some sixty to eighty nanometres across, a nanometre being a thousand times smaller than a micrometre. Those windows face inward, toward Bruch's membrane and the retinal pigment epithelium waiting on the other side. The design exists to let oxygen and nutrients flood outward toward the outer retina with almost no wall in the way. Bruch's membrane gets its own episode, number seventy-six, and the pigment epithelium sits at seventy-five.

Host: And the word in this episode's title, lobular. The choriocapillaris is not just a random net, is it?

Guide: No, and this is the heart of the episode. The sheet is built from units called lobules. At the posterior pole each lobule is a roughly hexagonal patch, about half a millimetre across, and it follows a fixed plan: a single feeding arteriole arrives at the centre of the hexagon and spreads blood outward, and a ring of venules collects that blood at the lobule's rim and carries it away toward the choroidal veins. Arterial core, venous rim, like a dartboard. Inject ink into the system and the posterior choroid stains as honeycomb, thousands of little hexagons, each one a perfusion territory with its own supply and its own exit. Toward the equator the pattern stretches and simplifies: the units elongate, and feeding arteries and draining veins alternate in sectors rather than holding a perfect core and rim. But the principle holds from disc to ora. The capillary sheet is a mosaic, not a single connected lake.

Host: Why build it that way? What does all that independence buy the tissue?

Guide: Two things worth carrying out of this room. First, a mosaic of semi-independent units means a blocked feeding arteriole starves one hexagon rather than the whole coat, and on a dye study an ischaemic patch maps as a distinct pale lobule. The architecture is written into the appearance of disease. Second, and this is the idea to keep, the lobular plan creates supply frontiers inside the choroid. The posterior lobules are fed by the short posterior ciliary arteries. The anterior choroid, near the ora serrata, the scalloped forward edge of the retina, is fed instead by recurrent branches coming back from the anterior ciliary system we met in episode fifty. Between those two territories runs a band around the equator fed strongly by neither, a watershed, literally a strip of high ground between two river systems where the sharing leaves it driest. The retina had its own watershed between the retinal and choroidal systems, which we covered in episode seventy-one. This one lies wholly inside the choroid, and it is the part of the coat most easily caught short when perfusion pressure drops.

Host: Numbers time, because this coat has a reputation. How much blood are we actually talking about?

Guide: The figures are among the most dramatic in the whole body. The choroid takes roughly eighty-five percent of all the blood the eye receives. The entire retina, every layer we spent two episodes on, runs on less than five percent. Per gram of tissue, the choroid may be the most richly perfused tissue we know, of the order of a litre of blood per minute for every hundred grams. The kidney, which is famous for its flow, manages roughly half that per gram, and the brain only a small fraction of it. And remember how thin the recipient is. All of that traffic squeezes through a coat about a fifth of a millimetre thick at the back, thinner still toward the front, a layer you could almost miss on a scan if you were not looking for it.

Host: Hold on. That much flow, into a layer that thin, sounds almost wasteful. Is the choroid really using it all?

Guide: Here is the beautiful part: no, and that is precisely the design. The choroid extracts remarkably little oxygen from the blood passing through it. When that blood leaves through the veins it is still nearly fully loaded, saturations around ninety percent, far brighter with oxygen than most venous blood anywhere in the body. The enormous flow is not there for the choroid's own appetite. It exists to hold the pressure and the oxygen level inside the choriocapillaris permanently high, so that a steep gradient pushes oxygen outward, across the capillary windows, across Bruch's membrane, through the pigment epithelium, into the photoreceptors. And recall from episode seventy-two that the very centre of the fovea contains no retinal vessels at all, so the densest, most oxygen-hungry tissue in the eye depends on this circulation alone, with no backup. How the photoreceptors spend that oxygen is the opening story of episode seventy-four.

Host: So it runs fast and generous on purpose. Now for the exit. Where does all this blood go once it leaves the lobules?

Guide: Outward and backward, into the venous half of the mosaic. Blood gathered at the rims of the lobules drains into wide, thin-walled, valveless veins occupying the large vessel layer, and those veins converge, quadrant by quadrant, toward four great collecting trunks, the vortex veins. Each vortex vein begins as a widening, a little flask called the ampulla, where the veins of its quadrant pour themselves together, and from the ampulla the trunk runs through an oblique canal in the sclera and out of the eye. Classically there are four vortex veins, one for each quadrant, though eyes vary and five or six are not rare. They are the exit route for the whole uveal circulation: venous blood from iris and ciliary body, having drained backward into the choroid, joins the same four streams. Everything the ciliary arteries of this series have been feeding since season five leaves through these doors.

Host: Does the retinal blood use these same doors? The central retinal vein must go somewhere too.

Guide: It does, but by its own road, and this is a distinction worth fixing. The central retinal vein, which collects the inner retina's blood and which we followed in episode seventy-one, leaves the eye inside the optic nerve and runs back through the orbit to the superior ophthalmic vein directly. It does not pass through the vortex veins. The vortex doors are reserved for uveal blood, choroid, ciliary body, iris, plus the sclera's own small venous drainage nearby. Two separate drainages, two separate plumbing systems, sharing one globe. That separation matters in the clinic, because it lets disease in one compartment announce itself without drowning the other: a congested uvea tells its story through the vortex system, while the retinal veins tell their own story at the disc, where you can watch them directly.

Host: Describe the exit properly, then. An oblique tunnel through the sclera sounds like a design detail worth understanding.

Guide: It is. The canal runs obliquely, angled backward as it passes outward, and the vortex vein pierces the sclera along it a few millimetres behind the equator, in practice somewhere between about fifteen and twenty-five millimetres from the limbus depending on the quadrant and on the individual. Two details matter. First, the tunnel is shared: one or two ciliary nerves, fine threads from the ciliary ganglion and the nasociliary nerve, run through the same canal alongside the vein, so every vortex vein doubles as a landmark for nerve fibres entering or leaving. Second, the oblique route acts like a safety valve. If the pressure inside the eye spikes, the walls of the slanted canal are pressed together and the vein partially collapses at the doorway instead of being torn off its anchor. The globe protects its own drainage with geometry. The same obliquity, incidentally, is why a vortex vein can be difficult to cauterise: it retracts into its tunnel rather than standing proud.

Host: Name the four, and follow them outward. Where does each quadrant's blood finally end up?

Guide: Name them by position and they name themselves: a superotemporal, a superonasal, an inferotemporal, and an inferonasal vortex vein. The two upper veins, superotemporal and superonasal, empty into the superior ophthalmic vein, the largest venous channel of the orbit. The two lower ones empty mainly into the inferior ophthalmic vein, with cross connections between the two systems being common. Both orbital veins then carry the flow back toward the apex of the orbit and through the superior orbital fissure, the great doorway from episode four, into the cavernous sinus, the venous crossroads sitting beside the pituitary gland inside the head. So a drop of blood entering a short posterior ciliary artery at the back of the eye washes the choroid, gathers at a lobule rim, collects in an ampulla, crosses the sclera, and reaches the cavernous sinus, all within a few centimetres and a few seconds. A complete circuit of one of the fastest organs in the body.

Host: A drainage question students always ask. With all this venous engineering, does the choroid have lymphatics as well?

Guide: The classic answer is no, and it is a striking one. Almost every organ in the body pairs its blood vessels with lymphatic channels, thin side networks that reclaim leaked fluid and return it to the circulation. The choroid, and the rest of the uveal tract, have no true lymphatic vessels. Fluid that escapes the leaky, fenestrated capillaries of the choriocapillaris is reclaimed on the retinal side by the pigment epithelium's pumping, and otherwise returns with the venous outflow through the vortex veins. That is one more reason the whole drainage story concentrates in four doors: there is no parallel lymphatic network standing by as a spare. Leaky walls, high flow, and a single venous exit per quadrant: hold that trio in mind and much of choroidal behaviour, including why congestion builds quickly, starts to make anatomical sense.

Host: And the neighbours? These canals must sit among the muscles and tendons of the orbit.

Guide: They do, and that is why vortex veins are landmarks that retinal surgeons respect. The inferotemporal vein exits close to the insertion of the inferior oblique muscle, and its ampulla is a favourite reference point in that quadrant. The superotemporal ampulla lies in the territory of the superior oblique tendon as it hooks around the upper outer quadrant. When a silicone band is passed around the globe to support a detached retina, the surgeon works deliberately around these veins, because cutting or strangling one of the uveal tract's only exits brings congestion, swelling, and raised pressure in the choroid behind the blockage. The ampullae themselves can be seen through the retina during surgery as dark, dome-shaped shadows arranged around the back of the eye, four quiet signs that say, in effect, do not cut here. For an optometrist interpreting a fundus photograph, knowing where the four sit explains the geography of many peripheral findings you will be asked about.

Host: One more system to ask about. Do these vessels listen to any nerves?

Guide: They do, and the wiring explains a lot of bedside behaviour. The choroidal vessels carry a strong sympathetic supply, vasoconstrictor fibres that set out from the superior cervical ganglion, ride the internal carotid plexus, and reach the eye chiefly with the long ciliary nerves. They also receive a parasympathetic, vasodilator supply from the pterygopalatine ganglion, and sensory fibres from the nasociliary nerve arrive with the ciliary nerves too, which is why an inflamed choroid can genuinely ache even though the coat itself is buried at the back of the eye. Now notice what is missing. The retinal vessels we met in episode seventy-one are autoregulators; they have the knack of holding their flow steady when blood pressure swings. The choroid autoregulates far less briskly. Its flow leans heavily on sympathetic tone and on the general perfusion pressure. Anatomically, the coat that feeds the photoreceptors is the coat most exposed to the body's blood pressure weather.

Host: Give us the outflow map in one glance, then. Where do the four ampullae sit on the clock face of the fundus?

Guide: Approximately at the two, five, seven, and ten o'clock stations, give or take an hour in any individual eye: superotemporal near two, inferotemporal near five, inferonasal near seven, superonasal near ten, all a few millimetres behind the equator. That ring of four is worth sketching from memory until you can draw it without hesitation, because so much posterior segment geography keys off it. The retinal arcades and the macula sit inside the ring, the vortex exits sit on the ring, and the peripheral fundus lies outside it. When a wide-field photograph shows you the dark oval shadows of the ampullae, you are instantly oriented: you know which quadrant you are viewing, roughly where the equator would fall, and where the no-cut zones for any future referral letter begin.

Host: Bring this back to the consulting chair for us. What should we see differently in a scan or a photograph with today's anatomy in mind?

Guide: Three pictures. On an OCT scan, look at the choroid under the fovea: in a healthy young adult it measures roughly a quarter of a millimetre, two hundred fifty to three hundred micrometres, and it thins with age and with the stretch of a longer, more myopic eye, so that number is quietly telling you the history of the globe it lives in. On a fluorescein angiogram, remember that the choroid lights up before the retinal arteries do, because the short ciliary system is fed closest to the ophthalmic artery, and it fills lobule by lobule, the mosaic made briefly visible. And behind any event that suddenly starves the outer retina, remember which circulation serves that tissue and where its weak frontier lies, at the equatorial watershed we mapped earlier. The fovea, as we said, has no retinal backup supply. Its entire oxygen bill is paid at the choriocapillaris window.

Host: Recap for us, then. If a listener walks away with three sentences from this hour, what are they?

Guide: First, the inflow: ten to twenty short posterior ciliary arteries ring the optic nerve, link up in the circle of Zinn-Haller, and funnel through Haller's layer and Sattler's layer down into the choriocapillaris. Second, the exchange: that capillary carpet is built as hexagonal lobules about half a millimetre wide, each with an arterial core and a venous rim, processing roughly eighty-five percent of the eye's blood while extracting very little of its oxygen, because the flow exists to bathe the outer retina, and the fovea alone, from above. Third, the outflow: four vortex veins, one per quadrant, gather at their flask-shaped ampullae and pierce the sclera obliquely a few millimetres behind the equator, sharing their canals with ciliary nerves, and hand the blood to the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins on the way to the cavernous sinus. Inflow, exchange, outflow. That is the entire circuit, and it is the promise we made in season five, kept at last.

Host: That is the whole map, and you have kept a promise on our behalf as well as the series. Before we let you go, leave our listeners with the one image they should carry into Monday morning.

Guide: One last image before we close. The next time you dilate a patient and see the dark, mottled, faintly orange background behind the retinal vessels, you are looking through a window at today's subject. That mottling is the choroid showing through: the great vessel layer, the medium vessels, and the lobulated carpet whose entrances and exits we walked today. Most patients will never know it is there. You now know it is the busiest plumbing in their body, and you know the four corners it drains by.
