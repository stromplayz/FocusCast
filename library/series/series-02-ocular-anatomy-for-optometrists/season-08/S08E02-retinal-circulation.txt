# Description: Two capillary networks lie woven through the inner retina, and in this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks we map them plane by plane. We trace where the superficial and deep capillary beds sit within the retinal layers, how they interconnect through the perifoveal anastomosis around the fovea, and why the very centre of the fovea is left completely free of capillaries. Along the way we meet pericytes, the inner blood-retinal barrier, the radial peripapillary capillaries, and the anatomical logic behind every branch and gap in the network. Pure anatomy of the retinal microcirculation, told for the ear, for optometry students and optometrists.

Host: Welcome back to Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, the anatomy series here on Focus Cast by focuslinks. This is Season 8, Retina, Choroid and Optic Nerve, and we are on episode two. Last week, in episode 71, Retinal Blood Supply, we walked the supply highways of the inner retina: the central retinal artery entering the eye deep inside the optic nerve, its division into superior and inferior branches, the four vascular arcades arching toward the nose and the temple, and the retina's dual supply, part retinal and part choroidal, with the watershed zone between the two territories. Today we leave the highways and descend into the streets. Our whole subject is the capillary network itself: where the two capillary beds sit, how they interconnect with one another, and the one small circle where the network deliberately refuses to go. My guide is back beside me. Walk us down off the arcades.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to every listener. Here is the map for today, and it has three destinations. First, the capillary beds themselves, because the retina carries not one bed but two, a superficial plexus and a deep plexus, resting at two different depths among the retinal layers. Second, the perifoveal anastomosis, a ring-shaped meeting point where those two planes merge around the fovea. And third, the capillary-free foveal centre, the famous empty circle at the middle of your sharpest vision. Before we set off, three plain definitions, because I promised plain words in this series. A capillary is the smallest kind of blood vessel, a tube so narrow that blood cells travel through it single file. A plexus simply means a network of interlacing vessels. And an anastomosis is a natural connection joining one vessel to another, a built-in shortcut. Say those three words to yourself once now. They are today's luggage.

Host: Let us pick up exactly where episode 71 left us. We are standing on one of the four arcades, a vessel you can see plainly with a viewing lens, running through the innermost layers of the retina. Somewhere ahead of us, that orderly, tree-like vessel becomes a mesh, a lacework that no direct view in the consulting room can really show. Walk me down the scale, step by step, from arcade to capillary.

Guide: The tapering happens in three stages, and the names matter. An arcade gives off branches, and those branches become arterioles, the smallest arteries, still carrying a muscular wall. Each arteriole then narrows into precapillary arterioles, and those finally open into the capillaries themselves. Numbers make this vivid. The central retinal artery is roughly two tenths of a millimetre wide at the disc, a major arteriole near the arcade is on the order of a tenth of a millimetre, and a retinal capillary has a lumen of only about five to ten micrometres. A micrometre is a thousandth of a millimetre. For comparison, a red blood cell is about seven to eight micrometres across, so inside these capillaries the cells travel literally in single file, bending as they squeeze through. That is how fine the plumbing becomes by the end of the taper, and everything we discuss today happens at that scale.

Host: So what is a capillary actually built from? I have a feeling the retinal ones are not just generic capillaries dropped into the eye. Give me the construction of the wall, and tell me what makes a retinal capillary different from a capillary in, say, your calf muscle.

Guide: Here is the construction, and it is beautifully economical. The inner lining is the endothelium, a single layer of flat cells joined edge to edge by tight junctions, which are molecular welds sealing the gaps between neighbouring cells. Outside that sits the basement membrane, a thin sheet of protein material that beds the tube down into the surrounding tissue. And wrapped around the outside, at intervals, sit the pericytes, small contractile cells that embrace the capillary from the outside. One more property, and it is decisive: the retinal capillary endothelium is continuous and non-fenestrated, meaning it has no little pores punched through it. Compare that with a capillary in the kidney, where letting fluid through is the entire point of the vessel. The retina builds its capillaries like sealed, reinforced pipes, with a living sleeve of supervision around every segment. And the next natural question is why it bothers to be so elaborate.

Host: Then let us slow down on the pericytes, because most listeners have never heard the word. What is a pericyte, what does it actually do all day, and why would the retina want so many of them? You said roughly one for every endothelial cell. In plain terms, is that a lot?

Guide: Think of a pericyte as a combination of a clamp and a caretaker. Pericytes contain contractile protein, so they can adjust the calibre of the capillary beneath them and help steer flow locally, and they also maintain the wall, talk chemically to the endothelium, and stabilize the whole tube. To your question about numbers: in skeletal muscle and in most other tissues you find roughly one pericyte for every three to ten endothelial cells. In the retina, the ratio approaches one to one, which is among the densest pericyte investment anywhere in the body. The retina, in other words, posts a supervisor to its smallest vessels at every turn, and pairs those supervisors with welded endothelial seams. Together they produce the single most important property of the retinal network, and that property deserves an answer of its own.

Host: Then give us that answer. You said sealed pipes, and pipes are sealed against something. What exactly is the barrier here, and what is it keeping in or out?

Guide: The barrier is keeping the blood itself out of the retinal tissue. The endothelial cells of the retinal capillaries are welded to each other by those tight junctions, and together with the pericytes and the basement membrane they form what anatomists call the inner blood-retinal barrier. Its job is to let oxygen and nutrients pass across to the retinal cells while refusing passage to the large proteins and the watery bulk of plasma. Now hold onto the anatomical reason this matters so much. The retina is an optical instrument. Light entering the eye must cross the inner retina, the very layers where the vessels live, to reach the photoreceptors deeper down. Any looseness in the wall, any seepage of plasma into the tissue, would fog that instrument from the inside. So the network is built watertight, because the tissue it feeds must stay transparent. Half of the barrier story, the outer half, belongs to the pigment epithelium, and that is a promise we make to episode 75.

Host: That is a lovely piece of engineering logic, and it sets up the question I have been waiting for all episode. Where exactly are these capillaries? The retina is famously layered. Which layers carry the beds, and which layers are left dry?

Guide: Cast your mind back to episode 67, Retinal Layers, where we walked the retina from inside to outside through its ten layers. Today only the inner layers concern us, because the retinal capillaries occupy two distinct planes. The first plane is the superficial capillary plexus, sitting in the nerve fibre layer and the ganglion cell layer, the innermost cellular layers just beneath the internal limiting membrane. That is the plane you see when you look at the fundus, and it is fed directly by the arcade arterioles. The second plane is the deep capillary plexus, sitting within the inner nuclear layer, the layer packed with the cell bodies of the bipolar, horizontal and amacrine cells. Modern scanning actually resolves the deep bed into two closely spaced planes, one near the inner plexiform layer and one near the outer plexiform layer, which is why you will also meet the term intermediate plexus. Classically, though, we teach two beds, and we will hold to that today.

Host: Two beds, at two depths, like a city with an upper road level and a lower road level. But a listener might reasonably ask, why bother with two planes at all? Why is one plane of capillaries not enough for the inner retina?

Guide: Because of distance, and the geometry is worth feeling in your bones. Capillaries feed tissue by diffusion, and diffusion punishes distance mercilessly. Double the distance from a cell to its nearest capillary and the delivery of oxygen falls off dramatically. The inner retina is a dense stack of living, firing nerve cells, ganglion cells and their fibres, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, all with high metabolic demand and all needing to sit within a few tens of micrometres of a vessel. A single plane could never put every inner neuron that close to a blood supply. By carrying capillaries at two depths, the anatomy ensures that almost every cell in the inner two thirds of the retina has a capillary either just above it or just below it, never more than a short drift away. Two planes are not a luxury. They are the minimum arrangement that keeps the whole inner thickness fed at once.

Host: Are the two beds identical in character, these two planes? Or do the upper road level and the lower road level actually look different from each other, if you could freeze them and study the geometry?

Guide: They have recognisably different personalities, and knowing them helps you read any picture of the network. The superficial plexus is the denser, more regular one, a fairly flat sheet of capillaries hanging directly beneath the arterioles that feed it. The deep plexus is fed partly by descending branches, little arteriolar twigs that dive down from the superficial plane, and partly by vertical connections reaching up from below. At the macula, the deep bed gathers into small whirls, capillaries spiralling around tiny hub vessels, giving it a lobular, vortex-like look that the superficial plane does not have. The two planes are not independent circuits either. They are stitched together by connecting capillaries running vertically between them, so blood can pass from the upper level to the lower level and across, which is precisely the interconnection we will need when we reach the ring around the fovea.

Host: Good, and that vertical stitching leads me to the inventory question. Which parts of the retina get their blood from these two planes, and, just as important, which parts get none of it at all?

Guide: Here is the classic division, and it is worth memorising as a fraction. The retinal circulation supplies roughly the inner two thirds of the retina: from the internal limiting membrane, down through the nerve fibre layer, the ganglion cell layer, the inner plexiform layer, and the inner nuclear layer, reaching as far out as the outer plexiform layer. The outer third receives no retinal capillaries whatsoever. The outer nuclear layer, where the photoreceptor cell bodies live, and the photoreceptor inner and outer segments beyond it, are completely free of retinal vessels. The photoreceptors are nourished from behind, by the choroidal circulation, and that entire story, the choroid and its remarkable plumbing, is next week's episode 73. Notice what this arrangement accomplishes optically. The layers that light must cross to reach the cones stay as clear of vessels as possible, while the busy neural machinery of the inner retina gets a private, generous supply of its own.

Host: Before we chase the choroid, there is a wrapping question I want to ask. Blood vessels do not sit in a vacuum inside the retina. What holds them there, and what is immediately around the outside of these capillary walls?

Guide: Glia, mainly, and it is a beautiful detail. Remember the Muller cell, the tall supporting glial cell that spans the whole thickness of the retina, which we met in episode 67. The retinal vessels, arterioles and capillaries alike, are embraced by the flattened endfeet of these Muller cells, and in the nerve fibre layer they are also wrapped in processes from astrocytes, the star-shaped glial cells that share that layer with the ganglion cell fibres. So the full wall reads like a sandwich: endothelium inside, basement membrane, pericytes outside, and then a continuous glial covering beyond that. This covering anchors the vessels in place, contributes to the barrier, and couples the plumbing to the tissue it serves. On the innermost surface, the internal limiting membrane forms the smooth boundary between all of this and the vitreous behind which, as we covered back in Season 7, the gel simply rests against the retina.

Host: Now, the disc region. You mentioned that the superficial plane is the one we see at the fundus. I have read that around the optic nerve head specifically, the capillaries arrange themselves in a distinctive way. What is special about the network near the disc?

Guide: That is the radial peripapillary capillary network, usually shortened to radial peripapillary capillaries, and it is one of the most elegant arrangements in the whole retina. In the nerve fibre layer around the optic disc, the capillaries run long, straight and parallel to one another, radiating outward from the disc margin and sweeping along the same arcuate paths that the nerve fibres themselves follow toward the disc. Unlike the rest of the capillary mesh, they form relatively few cross-connections between neighbouring channels. They are long, lonely roads rather than lace. Anatomically they belong to the superficial plexus, specialised for feeding the nerve fibre layer, which is the busiest cable room in the eye. Where those fibres converge and how the bundles are organised is a story we are deliberately saving for episode 80, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship. For today, hold the picture of straight, parallel capillaries combing the nerve fibre layer around the disc.

Host: Straight, parallel, and poorly cross-connected. That phrase makes me want to ask the general topology question about the network as a whole. Are the feeding vessels connected to each other at all? Do arterioles ever link up with other arterioles, the way ring roads around a city connect districts?

Guide: This is one of the most important anatomical facts of the episode, so let me state it plainly. Within the retina, the arterioles do not anastomose with one another. Each arteriole runs its own territory, feeding its own region of capillary bed, and there is no backup arterial channel running alongside it. That is why the central retinal artery is described as a functional end artery, an artery with no useful alternative route if it is blocked. The word functional is doing honest work here, because at the capillary level there is plenty of interconnection, as we will see in a moment, but between the feeding arterioles themselves, no. No arteriole-to-arteriole bridges, and no direct artery-to-vein shortcuts either. Every single drop of retinal blood must pass through a capillary bed before it can enter a venule. The system is a one-way street network with no bypasses at the arterial level, which is the anatomical price of keeping the walls sealed and the flow controlled.

Host: Which brings me naturally to the exit side of the story. Blood has come down the arterioles and through the beds. How does it get out, and back toward the ophthalmic vein? And is the drainage side built the same way as the supply side?

Guide: Symmetrically, with a few venous touches of its own. From the capillary beds, blood collects into postcapillary venules, the venous counterpart of the precapillary arterioles, and these merge into larger venules that run alongside the arterioles in the same two planes. The venules are a little wider than the arterioles, with thinner walls, and they converge on the superior and inferior venous arcades, mirror images of the arterial ones. From there the drainage follows the route we mapped in episode 71, ending in the central retinal vein, which exits the eye inside the optic nerve. I will not re-walk that course today, because we gave it a full tour last episode. What to notice anatomically is the pairing. In the retina, arteries and veins travel together in tidy pairs, arteriole and venule side by side in the nerve fibre layer, which is exactly the picture you see in every fundus photograph.

Host: Now for the destination I have been circling since the opening. The fovea. The centre of the macula, the spot where we resolve the finest detail the eye can deliver, is famously a hole in this network. Walk me to its edge.

Guide: We touched the edge of this in episode 69, Fovea and Foveola, when we studied the pit from the tissue side: the way the inner retinal layers are displaced sideways so that light falls almost directly onto the cones. Today we return to the same ground from the blood side, and the picture is just as dramatic. The centre of the fovea contains no capillaries at all. This central island is called the foveal avascular zone, and histologically it measures on average about half a millimetre across, with healthy eyes varying anywhere from roughly four hundred to six hundred micrometres in diameter. Think about how strange that is. Every other richly active tissue in the body is interlaced with vessels. Here, the most precious half square millimetre of the entire retina is deliberately left dry, and the capillary network simply flows around it like water around a stone.

Host: Deliberately left dry. I want to press on that word, deliberately. Why would the body deny blood supply to the very spot we use most? That sounds like a design flaw, unless you can convince me otherwise.

Guide: It is the opposite of a flaw, and the reasoning is optical before it is vascular. Two reasons, working together. First, transparency. Light entering the eye comes to its sharpest focus on that exact centre, and anything lying in front of the photoreceptors there, including capillaries, would sit inside the finest part of the image. A capillary is not invisible. Blood cells drifting through your point of sharpest detail would shimmer like dust on a lens. The foveal centre buys perfect clarity by refusing plumbing. Second, the trade is affordable, because of what episode 69 taught us: the inner layers have been displaced sideways, leaving the centre wonderfully thin, and the choroid lies immediately behind it. Oxygen and nutrients reach the foveal centre by simple diffusion across that short distance. Remember, the outer third of the retina has always depended on the choroid. The foveal centre simply leans on that same supplier a little more.

Host: So the empty circle is ringed by vessels, and I believe that ring has the name you promised at the start of the episode. Tell me about the perifoveal anastomosis. What is it, and what exactly is it doing there at the border?

Guide: This is today's centrepiece, so let us build it slowly. Throughout the posterior pole, remember, the capillaries travel in two planes, superficial and deep, stitched together vertically. But as the beds approach the fovea, those two planes converge and fuse into a single plane. Within that single plane, at the border of the foveal avascular zone, the capillaries anastomose freely with one another, side to side, forming a continuous ring of interconnected capillaries that encircles the foveal centre. This is the perifoveal anastomosis. It is the most distal part of the retinal network in that region, the last station at the end of the longest arterial journeys, and it marks the exact boundary between the vessel-rich retina and the empty centre. So the geometry, stated once, is this: two planes everywhere behind you, one merged plane at the ring, and zero planes inside the ring. Three states of the network within a single square millimetre.

Host: You said the two planes merge into one at the ring. Does the network perform this one-plane trick anywhere else in the retina, or is the perifovea unique in the whole body of the retina?

Guide: It happens at exactly one other place, and the symmetry is charming. At the far periphery of the retina, approaching the ora serrata, the scalloped front edge where the retina meets the ciliary epithelium, the capillaries also reduce to a single plane, and a sparser, thinner one than anywhere behind. So the classic teaching runs like this: the capillary network is double-layered through the posterior pole, and single-layered in two zones only, the perifoveal ring and the far periphery. Everywhere else, two planes, each fed and each drained. And notice where the mesh is densest of all: in the macular region between disc and ring, where the nerve fibre and ganglion cell layers are at their thickest and the metabolic demand peaks. Density of capillaries follows demand of tissue. That rule is one of the quiet laws of anatomy, and it explains almost every variation we have described today.

Host: I want to add a modern note, because students today meet these beds on machines rather than in jars. How do we actually visualise this network in the clinic, and do the scans agree with the classical anatomy?

Guide: The classical anatomy came from injected preparations, in which the vessels were filled with dye and the surrounding tissue was then cleared away, leaving the network as a standalone cast, plus a great deal of patient microscopy. Modern optical coherence tomography angiography, a scanning laser technique that detects the movement of blood without any injected dye, now images these beds in the living eye, and the correspondence is deeply satisfying. The scans show the superficial plexus, the deep and intermediate planes, and they outline the foveal avascular zone as a measurable shape, which is why avascular zone diameter has become a routine anatomical measurement in imaging. But keep the order straight when you teach it to yourself: the histology came first, and the machines had to catch up to it. The network on the screen is the same network anatomists were describing from dye casts a lifetime ago.

Host: One more detail about the empty circle itself, because listeners will be looking at these scans. Is the foveal avascular zone a perfect circle? And apart from curiosity, is there any anatomical reason clinicians pay attention to its exact shape and position?

Guide: It is not a perfect circle, no. On scans it usually appears slightly oval, a little wider side to side than top to bottom, and its exact size differs noticeably from one healthy person to the next, which is why measurements are compared against the individual's own baseline over time rather than against one magic number. Anatomically it is also the most reliable landmark for the true centre of the fovea, since the capillary ring hugs its border symmetrically. And one related corridor detail: immediately beside the largest vessels, the capillaries step back a little, leaving relatively vessel-free sleeves along the trunk routes. So the network keeps its distance twice over: a wide empty circle at the centre of vision, and narrow empty corridors along the largest pipes. Both gaps exist for the same anatomical reason, optical clarity where light concentrates and clean hydraulics where the flow is greatest.

Host: Let me now connect all of this to the consulting room chair. When I look into a patient's eye with my ophthalmoscope, what am I actually seeing of this network? I can see vessels, obviously. Am I seeing any of today's capillaries?

Guide: You are seeing the trunk roads, not the lace. What the direct view shows are the arterioles and venules, the arcade vessels and their major branches, because they are thick enough to reflect light and draw their familiar red lines across the fundus. The capillary beds themselves sit below the resolution of the naked viewing eye. Their presence shows indirectly, in the healthy, uniform transparency of the retina between the big vessels, and their absence from the foveal centre shows as that subtle darker glint at the middle of the macula. We made a promise back in Series 1, in the episode called The Back of the Eye, that examining the fundus means watching living blood vessels in a way no other organ of the body allows. Today you know that promise has two layers. The vessels you see are the highways, and the streets now have names you know: superficial bed, deep bed, perifoveal ring, and the silent circle at the centre.

Host: And that takes me back to another Series 1 episode, The Retina as the Sensory Layer, where we met the retina mostly as a camera film, a place where images are caught. Today it has been a plumbing project from start to finish, and I found the change of angle genuinely delightful. Before we close, give me the full journey. Follow one red blood cell from the heart to the foveal ring and back out.

Guide: With pleasure, because synthesis is where anatomy sticks. Our red blood cell leaves the heart, travels down the ophthalmic artery, and enters the optic nerve inside the central retinal artery, exactly as mapped in episode 71. At the disc it divides superior and inferior, then into the four arcades. From an arcade it is guided into an arteriole, squeezed through a precapillary arteriole, and released into the superficial capillary plexus of the nerve fibre and ganglion cell layers. It may drop vertically through a connecting capillary into the deep plexus of the inner nuclear layer, delivering oxygen along both levels. Then into a postcapillary venule, out through the collecting venules and the venous arcades, into the central retinal vein, and away. Meanwhile, spare a thought for its twin, the cell that skipped all of this and travelled around to the choroid instead, feeding the photoreceptors and the foveal centre from behind. That twin is next episode's protagonist.

Host: A twin circulation with two very different personalities, and that contrast hands us the perfect door into next week. Guide, tell our listeners where we are going.

Guide: Next week, in episode 73, Choroidal Circulation, we cross the pigment epithelium into the vascular coat behind the retina. We will follow the short posterior ciliary arteries as they arrive around the optic nerve, we will explore the choriocapillaris, a dense fenestrated capillary sheet built from independent lobules, each with its own feeder and its own drain, and we will map the four vortex vein quadrants that carry the entire choroidal outflow out of the eye. If the retina taught us sealed pipes and territorial arterioles, the choroid will show us the opposite philosophy: vast flow, leaky windows, and compartment after compartment. The two circulations together make one anatomical sentence, and next week we finish it.
