# Description: Where exactly does the vitreous grip the eye, and which of those grips can turn dangerous? In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we map every major attachment across the posterior segment, from the ring at the optic disc margin to the premacular patch, the vascular arcades, and the ora serrata. You will learn the real textbook numbers for each site, why posterior vitreous detachment tears where it does, and how the anatomy explains flashes, floaters, vitreous hemorrhage, and macular hole. A precise, listener friendly anatomy hour for optometry students and clinicians who want the posterior segment to finally make mechanical sense.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series Two, Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, where we walk through the eye structure by structure and season by season. This is Season Seven, Vitreous and Posterior Segment, episode four. If you have traveled with us through this season, you already know the ground. In episode one, Vitreous Body, we weighed and mapped the transparent gel that fills the globe. In episode two, Vitreous Cortex, we met the condensed outer shell of that gel, denser and more cellular than its quiet center. And in episode three, Vitreous Base, we stood on the strongest grip in the entire eye, where the shell seizes the peripheral retina at its forward edge. Today we complete the map. We will visit every major place where the vitreous anchors itself to the tissues around it, rank those anchors from permanent to flimsy, and then use the map to explain flashes, floaters, hemorrhages, and holes in the macula. It is one of those rare anatomy lessons where a single picture, held in the mind, explains a dozen bedside mysteries.

Guide: And I love teaching this one, because it is anatomy that pays rent immediately. Let me set the frame before we tour. The vitreous touches the retina across its whole back surface, but touching is not gripping. Grip happens only where the condensed outer shell, the cortex you met in episode two, weaves its collagen fibres directly into the innermost film of the retina. That film is the internal limiting membrane, a thin, glassy basement sheet that forms the retina's inner boundary, and it is manufactured by the retina's own supporting cells, the Muller cells, whose swollen endings, called footplates, spread along the inner surface like the soles of countless feet pressed against a windowpane. Where cortical collagen blends into that membrane, the gel and the retina become mechanically one tissue. Everywhere else there is only loose contact, the way a wet leaf lies on glass. So hold this master image for the hour. The posterior segment is a sea of loose contact, dotted with islands of firm adhesion. When we toured the back of the eye in Series One, in the episode The Back of the Eye, we read the fundus as a landscape. Today we learn how the jelly in front of that landscape is moored, island by island.

Host: Before we sail, one phrase has always made me hesitate, and I suspect it has bothered our listeners too. The posterior hyaloid face. Is that a separate structure from the cortex, or the same thing wearing a different name?

Guide: The same thing, viewed from the back, and knowing that saves real confusion. The cortex is the condensed shell that invests the whole periphery of the gel. Its posterior portion, the part lying face to face with the retina, is what textbooks and surgeons call the posterior hyaloid face, or simply the posterior vitreous cortex. The word hyaloid comes from the Greek for glass, the same root that gave the vitreous, the glassy body, its name. When you hear that a detachment lifted the posterior hyaloid, picture episode two's shell peeling off the inside of the eye. One tissue, two names. Every attachment we discuss today is a property of that face, because it is the face that touches, and grips, the retina.

Host: Then let us start with the one island we already own. Give us the vitreous base in a single breath, as a recap, and then steer us toward the sites we have not yet met.

Guide: One breath. The vitreous base straddles the ora serrata, the serrated, saw edge line where the sensory retina hands over to the ciliary epithelium, and it is the strongest attachment in the eye, so strong that it does not release even in the most complete detachment of old age. Its span, as we measured in episode three, extends about one and a half to two millimetres forward over the ciliary epithelium and two to three millimetres back onto the peripheral retina. That grip is why blunt trauma so often tears the peripheral retina at its border, and it is why the base is absent from every detachment story we tell today. It simply never lets go. But the base is an anterior landmark. The islands that fill the rest of our map sit across the posterior segment, and the first of them lies in a surprising place, right at the doorway where the optic nerve exits the eye.

Host: That doorway surprised me when I first read it. The disc is the busiest exit in the eye, packed with about a million nerve fibres funneling out, and yet the gel insists on anchoring itself precisely there. Walk us into that attachment.

Guide: At the margin of the optic disc, the cortical fibres converge into a dense, circular ring and lock into the tissues at the edge of the nerve head. The disc itself is small, about one and a half millimetres across on average, so this anchor is compact and tight. Anatomically, the collagen of the condensed cortex blends with the internal limiting membrane where it sweeps around the disc, and with the supporting glial tissue at the margin of the nerve head. In the young eye this ring is genuinely firm, one of the most dependable grips in the posterior segment, and although it softens with the decades, it remains among the last attachments to yield. Picture it as the hinge on the exit door. The gel is moored at the very point of departure of the nerve, and that geometry has consequences we will meet in a moment.

Host: Two questions in one, then. Does the grip take in the whole disc, including the central hollow, or only its rim? And when that ring lets go, does it leave evidence behind?

Guide: Only the margin, and this is worth saying carefully. The central hollow of the disc, the physiologic cup, lies loosely covered, with no firm grip at its floor. The adhesion is a ring at the rim, where nerve fibres and glial tissue crowd the edge. The cup and the rim, and their clinical measurement, belong to episode ten, Optic Disc, so we will not open that door wide today. What matters here is the ring itself, because when a detachment reaches the disc, traction concentrated on that ring can peel a cuff of cortical and glial tissue off the margin. Freed into the gel, it drifts, folds, and hovers in front of the retina as a small ring shaped opacity. Clinicians call it a Weiss ring, and it is one of the most satisfying confirmations in our field, because it tells you the separation has arrived at the disc. It is, quite literally, the footprint of this attachment. And a boundary note. In front of the disc lies a funnel shaped space, and from it a canal runs forward through the gel, remnants of the fetal circulation. That interior architecture is next episode's territory. Today we stay on the walls.

Host: Second island, then, and for anyone who sees patients every day, this is the one that matters most. The macula. Why would the gel anchor itself directly over the tissue we depend on for reading, faces, and fine print?

Guide: Over the macula, the posterior face forms a roughly circular adhesion about three to four millimetres across, centered on the fovea, the little pit at the macula's heart. To place it on our map, the fovea lies roughly four millimetres temporal to the disc, about two and a half disc diameters out toward the temple, and we will walk the macula's full geography properly in episode eight. In the young eye, this premacular patch is among the firmest grips anywhere outside the base, and the construction explains why. The cortical fibres here condense into a patch that blends with an internal limiting membrane which is distinctly thin at the fovea, so the bond is intimate. There is also a quiet functional logic. A firm, even contact helps hold the gel's optical pathway steady over the finest seeing tissue in the body. In Series One, in the episode The Macula and Central Vision, we explored what this patch of retina does for reading and detail. Today's point is how it is held, because how it is held decides exactly what happens when the gel begins to pull.

Host: And what does happen with age? Because everything else about the vitreous seems to loosen across a lifetime.

Guide: The premacular grip weakens with age, like every posterior attachment, and in the ordinary, uneventful detachment it releases cleanly, which is why most older eyes shed their vitreous from the macula without a trace of trouble. But this island has a stubborn center. Right at the foveola, the very middle of the pit, the grip can persist after the surrounding gel has separated, and then the geometry turns dangerous. The detached posterior face keeps pulling, and the forces run tangentially, sliding across the foveal surface in every direction while anchored at the middle. That state is vitreomacular traction, and in its fullest form it can split the tissue at the center into a macular hole. Notice the mechanism, because it is pure attachment anatomy. The hole is not punched by anything. It is torn outward by an anchor that should have released and did not. The pit's own architecture, and why it is so vulnerable, we reserve for episode nine, Fovea and Foveola. Here, hold only the anchor and the vector.

Host: Third island, and this one took me longest to picture. The retinal vessels. Take us along the arcades.

Guide: Four major trunks leave the disc, a superior and an inferior pair, each dividing into nasal and temporal branches, and they arch outward across the retina in the sweeping curves we call the vascular arcades. Along these major vessels, and especially around the thick trunks inside the arcades, the vitreous cortex forms firm, linear adhesions. The anatomical basis is elegant. The large vessels run in the nerve fibre layer, the innermost stratum of the retina, and the condensed cortical gel drapes over them, binding to the connective tissue that sheathes each vessel. Two classic events flow from this single fact. First, when the posterior face pulls free, traction concentrated along a vessel can rupture it, spilling blood into the gel, which is why a sudden shower of dark floaters so often means hemorrhage and not merely a stray fibre. Second, in an ischemic retina, one starved of adequate blood flow, new abnormal vessels grow forward out of the retina and along the posterior cortical face, and the fibrovascular membranes they build are moored to the very structure that will later pull on them. The attachment map explains both the bleeding and the pulling.

Host: Let me nail down a geometric point while we are among the vessels. When the gel pulls on these islands, in which directions do the forces run? I imagine direction changes everything.

Guide: Direction changes everything. There are two great vector families. The first is anteroposterior traction. When the gel liquefies and collapses, it swings forward with every eye movement, dragging the islands toward the lens. That is the vector that wrenches open horseshoe tears at the back edge of the base, and it is the vector that lifts and ruptures vessels. The second family is tangential traction, forces that run along the retinal surface rather than away from it. That is the premacular problem, sliding across the fovea and dragging the internal limiting membrane in circles, the mechanism we just blamed for macular holes. Every island suffers most from the vector it is angled to receive. The disc ring and the vessel grips are tormented by the front to back pull. The macula is tormented by the sliding pull. Same map, two directions.

Host: That traction brings me to the flashes people report. Why does pulling on the retina produce light?

Guide: Because the retina is a signaling tissue, and it is honest to a fault. Whatever stimulates it, it reports as light. Photons excite the photoreceptors, and mechanical stretch excites them too, which means traction during separation is perceived as brief flashes or shimmering arcs in the dark. The classic pattern even carries a name, Moore's lightning streaks, flickering arcs seen in dim surroundings, usually off toward the temporal periphery, often persisting for weeks as the detachment evolves. We met this territory from the patient's chair in Series One, in the episode Patient With Sudden Vision Change. Here is the anatomical takeaway for today. The flash is not the damage. It is the sensation of an adhesion being tested, and its location points you to an island that is still gripping. Only when the test succeeds, when traction completes a tear, does the symptom graduate from nuisance to emergency.

Host: And the sea between the islands? You promised us a map of the weak zones as well.

Guide: Between the islands, the contact is astonishingly weak. Across most of the posterior pole, away from the disc ring, the premacular patch, and the arcades, the cortex rests against the internal limiting membrane the way damp tissue paper rests on glass, barely attached, peeling at a touch. The equatorial cortex behind the base is variable, with scattered firmer points, and there is one exception to memorize. In lattice degeneration, the discrete patches of thinning that develop in the peripheral retina, the cortex at the edge of the patch is often firmly adherent, which makes those margins focal traction points of real consequence. We will meet lattice properly when we study the peripheral retina in a later season, but file the edge adhesion now. And so the master image stands. Four islands of firm grip, the base, the disc margin, the premacular patch, and the vascular arcades, set in a sea that gives way easily, with lattice edges as tiny rocky islets between.

Host: Now let us put time into the picture. The gel changes with the decades, and the balance of grip shifts with it. Walk us through how a detachment actually unfolds across this map.

Guide: Begin inside the gel. As we established in episode one, Vitreous Body, the vitreous is about ninety nine percent water held in a fine framework of collagen, and with the years that framework thins. Pockets of liquid form inside the gel, a process called syneresis, and the pockets coalesce until the gel loses its spring. Eventually the liquefied contents break through the thinned cortex, and the posterior face splits away from the retina. That event is posterior vitreous detachment, the peeling of the gel from its foundations, and the choreography follows our map exactly. Separation begins in the weak sea and stops at the strong islands, which is why the settling gel ends up tugging precisely at the disc, the vessels, and the macula. The numbers tell the demographic story. Posterior vitreous detachment is found in roughly twenty five percent of eyes in the sixties, rising to about sixty three percent by the eighties. And through every stage of it, the vitreous base holds on, exactly as it has since the eye was built.

Host: How fast does all of that unfold, once it starts? Gradually over years, or all at once?

Guide: Usually over days to weeks, not seconds, and not over years either. The acute, symptomatic detachment announces itself with the flashes and floaters we described, and the dangerous window is the early one, because the sea has already given way while the islands are still gripping. The great majority of retinal tears associated with an acute detachment declare themselves within the first six weeks of symptoms. That single anatomical fact is the reason behind the customary early re-examination of a patient after a first symptomatic event. The islands are strongest precisely when they are most dangerous, in the weeks while the sea around them is already gone.

Host: Let us give the floaters their own anatomy lesson. What exactly is the patient seeing when they complain of drifting specks and shadows?

Guide: A floater is simply something condensed enough to cast a shadow and mobile enough to drift. The candidates are all anatomical, and each maps to an attachment. Strands of collapsed cortical collagen, freed when the face split. The Weiss ring, lifted from the disc margin. Small tags of glial tissue avulsed from an adhesion point at a vessel or a lattice edge. And red blood cells from a ruptured vessel, which patients describe as smoke, a swarm, or a red haze. Because the gel around them has liquefied, these objects drift with every eye movement and settle slowly under gravity, which is why floaters dart away whenever the patient tries to look straight at them. Every floater has a tissue of origin, and the ones that matter most are the ones born at the islands.

Host: Can the shape of a floater tell you which tissue it came from? A burst of many small specks sounds different from one large cobweb.

Guide: It genuinely does. A sudden burst of many tiny dark specks points to red blood cells dispersed in the gel, which means a vessel has been torn at an adhesion point, and that finding deserves the same urgency as the flashes themselves. A single large, mobile veil or cobweb points to condensed cortical collagen or the Weiss ring itself, a structure big enough to be seen as one object. Numerous fine dots scattered evenly suggest the collapsed fibres of the cortex, settling like dust. The pattern is the anatomy speaking. Learn to translate the patient's description back into tissue, and the floater complaint stops being vague.

Host: Which brings us to the sharpest clinical edge of today's map. Retinal tears. Where do they form, and why there and nowhere else?

Guide: A tear needs two ingredients meeting at one place, a firm adhesion and a separating gel. When the sea gives way and an island holds, the entire pull concentrates at the island's border. The classic site is the posterior border of the vitreous base, where a rounded flap of peripheral retina can be dragged open into a horseshoe tear, hinged at the front, its apex pointing back toward the disc. The edges of lattice patches are the other classic site, for exactly the same reason. If the flap tears completely free, the floating fragment is called an operculum, a little plug of tissue adrift in the gel. And once a full thickness break exists, liquefied vitreous can pour through it and lift the sensory retina away from the pigment epithelium beneath. That is rhegmatogenous detachment, detachment through a hole, the most mechanically direct threat on today's map. This is why the anatomy itself, not mere convention, insists that new flashes and new floaters be examined with the pupil dilated and the periphery pressed into view.

Host: Does the map look different in a young eye? These attachments must behave very differently in a patient of twenty than in a patient of seventy.

Guide: Profoundly differently. In the young eye, every attachment is at its strongest and the gel is at its most rigid, so spontaneous detachment is rare, and the posterior islands, above all the premacular patch, grip fiercely. That is the protective face of the same anatomy. The dangerous face is trauma. A blunt injury suddenly indents the globe and yanks the cortex away from a firm attachment faster than the tissue can stretch, which is why the posterior border of the base is a classic tear site in the young patient after a sports injury or a fist. In the older eye, the situation inverts. The sea has thinned further, the gel has liquefied, and the islands are the only things still holding, so the islands become the focus of all the traction. Same anatomy, opposite risks, separated only by time.

Host: One modern note before we collect the map. When we want to see these attachments today, what does the clinic actually offer us? I suspect we are no longer limited to the ophthalmoscope.

Guide: We are not, and the change has been revolutionary. Optical coherence tomography, the cross sectional scan that most clinics now run daily, sections the gel retinal interface in micrometres and shows the premacular adhesion, the thin internal limiting membrane beneath it, and any persistent traction, resolved like a slice through a layered cake. It has turned vitreomacular traction from a textbook diagram into a picture on the screen. For the periphery and the base, wide field imaging and careful indirect viewing still carry the day. But whichever instrument you reach for, remember what it is confirming. The islands and the sea we mapped today are real, visible structures, not abstractions.

Host: Then let us collect the whole map in one place before we close. Strong against weak, island against sea.

Guide: Strong, in order. The vitreous base, straddling the ora serrata, one and a half to two millimetres forward and two to three millimetres back, permanent, and covered fully in episode three, Vitreous Base. The ring at the disc margin, about one and a half millimetres across, firm in youth, among the last to yield. The premacular patch, a circular adhesion three to four millimetres across, centered on the fovea. And the linear grips along the vascular arcades. Weak, everything between, the broad posterior sea and most of the equatorial cortex, with lattice edges as the scattered exceptions. If you keep one sentence from today, keep this one. The vitreous is firmly attached at four islands, the base, the disc, the macula, and the vessels, and nearly everywhere else it merely rests.

Host: And for the listener at the chair, how does this map change what they do with what they see? Convert it into judgment for us.

Guide: It converts findings into mechanisms. See a Weiss ring floating behind the lens, and you know the disc ring has released, so the separation has run its full posterior course. See vitreous hemorrhage with no visible source, and the vessel adhesions head your thinking, because traction there bleeds. See a patient reporting that straight edges bow, or that letters drop out at the center of a word, and suspect the premacular patch, clinging after everything around it let go. And see a horseshoe tear in the periphery, and you are looking at an island border that lost its argument with a collapsing gel. None of this requires a new instrument. It requires the map held in the mind while the light is in the eye.

Host: One more door before we finish. We have spent the hour on the walls the gel grips. What about the gel's own interior? I keep sensing there are rooms inside.

Guide: Then you will love next time, because we step inside the jelly itself. Within the gel runs a slender channel in a gentle S curve from the back of the lens to the front of the disc, the ghost of the fetal vessel that once fed the developing eye, widening over the disc into a funnel shaped space, while a small bursa hovers over the macula. The whole interior architecture, the Cloquet canal, the Berger space, and the prepapillary and premacular configurations, is the subject of episode five, Vitreous Chambers and Spaces. Today we mapped the grips on the walls. Next time we explore the rooms the jelly keeps within itself.

Host: A final pearl to carry home. Of everything we covered today, what is the one idea you most want our student listener to keep?

Guide: That strength and danger are not the same word. The base is the strongest attachment in the eye, but because it never releases, it rarely creates the dramas we fear. The posterior islands are weaker, and their danger is the opposite one. They hold on during a detachment precisely when the safest thing would be to let go, and every flash, every hemorrhage, every macular hole is that reluctance made visible. Attachment is protective across a lifetime of small movements, and hazardous in the rare moment of sudden separation. The clinician's craft is to know, for each site on the map, which moment that site is standing in.
