# Description: The retina is a wall built in ten named layers, and this episode walks them from the outermost pigment sheet to the innermost glassy ceiling. Learn which cells live in each nuclear layer, what actually happens on the two synaptic floors, why the external limiting membrane is not a true membrane, and how one radial glial cell scaffolds the whole wall. Focus Cast by focuslinks brings optometry students and clinicians a clear anatomy tour, with the macula, photoreceptor distribution and pigment epithelium saved for their own episodes.

Ten layers, walked outer to inner, one clear teaching home each. My calm expert guide is back in the studio. Welcome. Take us to the door.

Guide: Thank you, welcome back, and I will set the frame before we enter, because the retina deserves a moment of respect. It is the thinnest and most remarkable piece of nervous tissue in the body, a true outpost of the brain, pushed outward during development until it lines the inside of the eye like wallpaper lines a room. Two orientation rules before anything else, and they hold for the whole hour. When I say outer, I mean toward the choroid and the sclera, the back wall of the globe. When I say inner, I mean toward the vitreous, the clear gel we spent the first half of this season mapping. Now here is today's boundary, because this series works best when every story has one home. We walk all ten layers and say which cells live where. We do not map rod and cone densities across the retina, that belongs to episode 74, Photoreceptor Distribution. We do not take the pigment epithelium apart cell by cell, that is episode 75, Retinal Pigment Epithelium. And the macula's own geometry waits for next week. Today, the wall itself.

Host: Ten layers is a big count for a tissue thinner than a millimetre. Before we start the walk, tell me how histologists even arrived at ten. Where does the counting come from, and what are the names I should be listening for?

Guide: The counting comes from the light microscope, and the logic is beautiful once you see it. Stain a section of retina and you see three dark bands where cell bodies crowd together, like apartment blocks, separated by lighter bands where cells stretch out their fibres and talk to each other. The apartment blocks are called nuclear layers, because nuclei live there. The talking floors are called plexiform layers, from a word meaning woven, because that is exactly what a synapse-rich tangle looks like. Around those five bands sit boundary layers at each face of the wall, and two special residents at the outer edge. Count them with me, from the choroid side inward. One, the retinal pigment epithelium. Two, the layer of rods and cones. Three, the external limiting membrane. Four, the outer nuclear layer. Five, the outer plexiform layer. Six, the inner nuclear layer. Seven, the inner plexiform layer. Eight, the ganglion cell layer. Nine, the nerve fibre layer. Ten, the internal limiting membrane. Ten names, and by the end of this episode each one will have a face, a job, and an address. That is the promise of today.

Host: Then let us start at the very back, against the choroid we built earlier this series. Layer one. What is the pigment sheet, and why does every photograph of the living retina look dark because of it?

Guide: Layer one is the retinal pigment epithelium, and hear the fine print: it is technically not part of the sensory retina at all, it is the sheet the sensory retina leans on. A single layer of neat hexagonal cells, about four to six million of them per eye, joined edge to edge by tight junctions and sitting on the Bruch membrane we met with the choroid in episode 49, Choroid, and whose five-layer recipe gets its own episode, 76, Bruch's Membrane, in Season 8. Each pigment cell serves a small neighbourhood of photoreceptors, on average a few dozen, and does four jobs at once. Its melanin granules absorb stray light, like the black paint inside a camera body. Every morning it phagocytoses, meaning engulfs and digests, the tips of photoreceptor outer segments that were shed overnight, a daily housekeeping of astonishing scale. It recycles vitamin A so the visual pigment can be rebuilt. And its tight junctions form the outer blood-retinal barrier, deciding what may pass from choroid into retina. Hold those four jobs; the full cell-by-cell tour is episode 75's property. Today the sheet stays in one line.

Host: Here is the oddity that always bothered me as a student. The photoreceptors are the light catchers, yet they sit at the very back, so light has to slip past almost the whole wall before it is caught. Why would anyone build a camera backwards?

Guide: Because of the housekeeping, and this is one of the loveliest arguments in the eye. The outer segments of your photoreceptors are hardware under constant renewal, discs built, pushed outward, shed, digested. That factory service can only happen where the pigment epithelium can reach them, and the pigment epithelium must sit against the choriocapillaris, the capillary carpet of the choroid, because the outer retina is fed by diffusion across the Bruch membrane. So the whole assembly parks at the outer edge, receptors outermost, and the rest of the wall simply stays transparent. And it does stay transparent. Every layer between the light and the receptors is built of glassy, colourless cells and fibres, so the image arrives nearly undistorted. There is a price, of course, paid in scattered light and in the nerve fibres having to dive toward one exit point, but the trade has been judged worth it for four hundred million years. One more thing to hold, and it will organise the whole episode for you: light travels inward, from the internal limiting membrane toward the rods and cones, but the visual signal travels the opposite way, from the rods and cones out toward the ganglion cells. Two directions of traffic in one wall.

Host: Layer two then, the celebrities, the layer with the retina's most famous residents. What exactly is sitting in the layer of rods and cones, and what is the difference between the two cell types?

Guide: Layer two holds the photoreceptors themselves, but hear the address precisely: it holds their working ends, the outer segments and the inner segments, not their nuclei. A rod is a slender cylinder, about one hundred and twenty million of them across the retina, and its outer segment is a stacked pile of six hundred to a thousand flattened membrane discs, each loaded with the pigment rhodopsin, the molecule that changes shape when a single photon strikes it. A cone is shorter and tapers like a flask, about six million of them, and their pigments come in three wavelength classes, so the same basic design serves dim-light sensitivity in rods and fine detail and colour in cones. Beneath the outer segment sits the inner segment, crowded with mitochondria, the power plant that manufactures the proteins and energy the outer segment burns through. This layer is where light stops being light. The chemical change in a disc begins the electrical whisper that becomes a nerve signal, which is why this thin strip is the true birthplace of sight. How many of each cell live at any given point of the retina, and where the blind spot comes from, is the density map of episode 74. Today, one clear line: this is where light becomes electricity.

Host: Now there is a line between those working ends and the nuclei behind them, and it has the word membrane in its name, which I suspect is a small historical lie. Layer three?

Guide: Layer three is the external limiting membrane, and you are right, it is not a true membrane at all. Under the old light microscope it looked like a thin line, so the early histologists gave it a membrane's name. We now know it is a belt of junctions, little rivets called zonulae adherentes, stitching every photoreceptor body to the glial cell that cradles it. Which brings in the most underrated character in this whole story: the Muller cell, the retina's own support glia. Each Muller cell is a tall, thin radial scaffold, spanning almost the entire wall from the internal limiting membrane on the inner face up to these junctions at the external limiting membrane, and every layer in between is threaded through its branches. It holds the wall together, mops up ions and water to keep the tissue clear, and clears used transmitter from the synapses. The external limiting membrane is simply the inner edge of the receptor zone: the working ends live beyond the rivets, toward the pigment sheet, and the nuclei live behind them.

Host: Layer four, then. Behind the rivets, the dormitory. Who sleeps there?

Guide: Layer four is the outer nuclear layer, and its residents are exactly who you would expect after that last line: the cell bodies, the nuclei, of the rods and the cones. This is one of the most crowded dormitories in the body, holding the nuclei of roughly one hundred and twenty-six million cells. In the periphery the rod nuclei pile several rows deep, and the layer swells and thins across the retina according to how the receptors are distributed. Every one of those bodies sends a single thin fibre inward, back through the riveted belt, toward the synapse floor we are about to reach, so the layer above and the layer below are really one continuous cell seen at two heights. One gross note, because the map of layer thickness belongs to last week's episode: the outer nuclear layer is among the layers that swell dramatically toward the posterior pole, and the reason for that is pure macular geometry, which we will explain properly in episode 69, Fovea and Foveola.

Host: Layer five, then. The first of the two talking floors. Who is talking to whom down there, and what do they say?

Guide: Layer five is the outer plexiform layer, synapse city number one, and the conversation has a precise guest list. The rods and cones end here in little terminals: each rod ends in a tiny rounded button called a spherule, and each cone ends in a bulkier triangular platform called a pedicle. Pressed into those terminals are the fine dendrites of bipolar cells, the honest reporters of the retina, whose job is to carry the photoreceptor's message one reliable step inward. And threading sideways between all of them are the flat processes of horizontal cells, which connect neighbours to neighbours, so that a strongly lit receptor can quieten the ones around it, the trick called lateral inhibition that sharpens edges before the brain ever sees them. Around the macula, the receptor fibres arriving at this floor run obliquely sideways for a distance before they synapse, a band called Henle's fibre layer, and the reason for that strange detour is foveal geometry, a story with a one-home rule: episode 69. Today, the address. Outer plexiform layer: photoreceptor terminals, bipolar dendrites, horizontal cell cross talk.

Host: Layer six is the middle of the middle, and I have heard it called the processing department. Who lives there, and why do we need so many different cells between a photoreceptor and the optic nerve?

Guide: Layer six is the inner nuclear layer, and it is the busiest apartment block in the wall, because four different families share the address. The bipolars are there, and they are not one cell type but a whole staff: midget bipolars that connect to a single cone for fine detail, diffuse bipolars that pool many receptors, and rod bipolars dedicated to the rod pathway, each kind coming in an on flavour and an off flavour that respond to light increasing or decreasing. The horizontal cells keep their bodies here, two kinds in the human retina, though their branches work downstairs in the outer plexiform layer. The amacrine cells are here too, and they are the artists of the retina, dozens of types, commonly counted at around thirty, each shaping some aspect of the message: motion, direction, change, suddenness. Their branches work upstairs in the inner plexiform layer. And finally the nuclei of the Muller cells themselves sit in this layer, which tells you where the great scaffold keeps its own control room. Why so many families? Because nothing reaches the brain raw. Everything you will ever see is edited first inside this wall, and most of that editing is staffed from layer six.

Host: Layer seven, then. The second talking floor. What changes between the two plexiform layers?

Guide: Layer seven is the inner plexiform layer, synapse city number two, and here the cast rotates. The axons of the bipolar cells arrive and end in tidy little terminals. The branching processes of the amacrine cells flood the floor, weaving between everything. And the dendrites of the ganglion cells reach down from above to collect the finished report. The remarkable thing is that this floor is stratified, built in thin shelves like a switchboard with decks. In the outer half of the layer, called sublamina a, the off signals do their business, and in the inner half, called sublamina b, the on signals gather, and particular bipolar and amacrine types stick to their own decks with great discipline. This is where the message acquires its final shape: contrast made crisper, edges emphasised, motion flagged, change detected. A useful way to hold the pair of floors together: the outer plexiform layer is where the retina takes dictation from the photoreceptors, and the inner plexiform layer is where it drafts the letter it will actually send to the brain.

Host: Layer eight. This is where the wall finally produces its output cells, the ones whose fibres we will eventually follow to the brain. Who are they, and how many of them are there?

Guide: Layer eight is the ganglion cell layer, home of the retinal ganglion cells, the only true output neurons of the retina, the cells whose axons become the optic nerve. Their number is worth memorising because it is the number the whole system is built around: about one million of them per eye, often quoted up to one point two million, matching the roughly one point two million fibres counted in a healthy optic nerve. Notice the arithmetic hiding in that figure. Over one hundred million photoreceptors funnel down to about a million output cells, so the wall is a colossal convergence machine, pooling and compressing before anything leaves the eye. Two names will carry you far: the midget ganglion cells, small and numerous, serving fine detail and colour in the centre, and the parasol cells, larger, serving motion and broad sweeps. Some displaced amacrine cells also rent space in this layer, a small technical footnote. Where ganglion cells crowd most thickly, and what that means for the geography of the posterior pole, is the opening story of next week's episode. And we followed what their fibres become in Series 1, in The Optic Nerve and Visual Information.

Host: Layer nine, then. Once a ganglion cell has decided, its message has to travel to one exit door. What does that gathering of wires look like from inside the wall?

Guide: Layer nine is the nerve fibre layer, and it is exactly what it sounds like: a sheet of parallel wires. The axons of the ganglion cells run across the inner face of the retina toward the optic disc, and they follow a disciplined map. Fibres from the temporal side arch above and below the macula in curved bundles called arcuate bundles, sweeping around the centre like tributaries of a river, while the fibres from the macula itself run almost straight to the disc as the papillomacular bundle, taking the shortest route. Two anatomical facts about this layer repay the memory. First, these fibres are unmyelinated inside the eye; they are bare cables, wrapped in no fatty sheath, because a myelin coat would be cloudy and the wall must stay transparent. Myelination begins only at the lamina cribrosa, the sieve-like plate in the nerve head, precisely where the nerve leaves the eye's territory. Second, this layer is thickest at the disc margin, where everything converges. This is the layer optometrists measure, as retinal nerve fibre thickness, when they watch glaucoma year by year. The disc itself, the exit door with its rim and cup, is the season finale, episode 70, and its deep interior belongs to Season 8, episode 77.

Host: Which brings us to layer ten, the innermost face, the part of the wall that actually touches the vitreous gel we spent five episodes with. What is the internal limiting membrane?

Guide: Layer ten is the internal limiting membrane, and it is the retina's smooth glassy ceiling, the boundary the vitreous rests against. Like the external limiting membrane it is not a cellular layer but a product: a basement membrane, a thin sheet of connective tissue material laid down mostly by the expanded footplates, the little feet, of the Muller cells. Recall that those scaffold cells span the whole wall; the internal limiting membrane is literally their floor mat on the inner face, mirroring the rivets of the external limiting membrane on the outer face. Its surface is remarkably smooth, which is what lets the vitreous glide against the retina, and its adhesion to that gel is not uniform: it grips firmly at the disc margin and at the macula, and most tightly of all at the vitreous base up front. We mapped every one of those attachment sites in episode 64, Vitreous Attachments, so I will not re-draw the map, but hold this: the internal limiting membrane is the meeting line between the two great stories of this season, the gel and the wall. It is also, for retinal surgeons, a natural peeling plane, which tells you how real it is despite being thinner than a red blood cell is wide.

Host: Let me try to hold the whole wall at once, because I can feel ten names slipping around. Light enters from the vitreous side and passes through which layers first, and in which order does the signal travel back out? Walk the circuit slowly.

Guide: Slowly, then, in two passes. First the light. A photon enters through the pupil, crosses the vitreous, and passes, in this order, through the internal limiting membrane, the nerve fibre layer, the ganglion cell layer, the inner plexiform layer, the inner nuclear layer, the outer plexiform layer, the outer nuclear layer, and through the riveted belt of the external limiting membrane, before it is finally caught in the discs of a rod or cone outer segment, pressed against the pigment epithelium. Almost the whole wall, and it stays transparent precisely so this is possible. Now the signal, travelling the other way. The photoreceptor converts the catch into an electrical whisper and hands it, at the outer plexiform layer, to a bipolar cell, edited sideways by horizontal cells. The bipolar carries it up to the inner plexiform layer, where amacrine cells and ganglion dendrites shape it into a finished report. The ganglion cell fires, and its axon joins the river of fibres in the nerve fibre layer, flows to the disc, and leaves the eye for the brain. Threading through all ten floors the whole time, the Muller cells hold the wall up, keep it clear, and tidy the chemistry. Light in one direction, signal in the other, one scaffold through the middle. That is the retina.

Host: That circuit deserves to be seen, not just heard. When an optometrist looks at a scan of a living retina, do these ten layers actually show up? And while you are there, remind me whose blood feeds which floors, because I remember there were two plumbers.

Guide: On both counts, yes. Optical coherence tomography, the light-based scanner you will use daily in practice, slices the living retina into a cross section, and the ten layers appear as alternating bright and dark bands, because each floor reflects light differently: the nerve fibre layer, the two plexiform floors, the nuclear blocks, even the junction belts, all sit exactly where our histology put them. Modern machines can measure the thickness of the ganglion cell complex or the nerve fibre layer in micrometres and follow it visit by visit. As for the plumbers, the wall is fed from two sides, and the border between them runs near the outer plexiform layer. The inner two thirds, roughly from the nerve fibre layer down to that floor, are fed by the central retinal artery and its branches, which you saw entering with the optic nerve. The outer third, the photoreceptors and the pigment epithelium, have no vessels at all inside the eye; they are fed by diffusion from the choriocapillaris across the Bruch membrane, which is exactly why the pigment sheet and the receptors had to park against the choroid. The arteries, the two capillary beds, and the watershed strip between the two systems each get their episode in Season 8, episodes 71 to 73.

Host: Before we close, test me. I am going to recite the ten, outer to inner, from memory, and you tell me if the wall stands. Retinal pigment epithelium. Rods and cones. External limiting membrane. Outer nuclear layer. Outer plexiform layer. Inner nuclear layer. Inner plexiform layer. Ganglion cell layer. Nerve fibre layer. Internal limiting membrane. How did I do?

Guide: The wall stands, and that recitation is the whole episode in ten breaths. Let me hand each name one final plain word, the way I hope you will remember them. The pigment sheet, the retina's dark landlord that renews the hardware. The receptor carpet, where light becomes electricity. The riveted belt that is not a membrane. The receptor dormitory, one hundred and twenty-six million nuclei deep. The first talking floor, where the wall takes dictation from the light. The processing department, four families of editors under one roof. The second talking floor, where the finished letter is drafted in shelves. The dispatch office of a million ganglion cells. The wiring duct, bare fibres arcing to one exit. And the glassy ceiling where the gel meets the wall. Three floors of cell bodies, two floors of conversations, two boundary membranes, one pigment sheet, one receptor carpet, one wiring sheet. If you can rebuild that sentence, you can rebuild the entire list in order, outer to inner, from memory, in an examination hall or a clinic corridor.

Host: One last question, then I will let you go. If a listener keeps only one sentence from today, the sentence that makes all ten layers click into place, what should it be?

Guide: It should be the two directions. The retina is a wall with two traffic flows running through it in opposite senses. Light flows inward, through the glassy ceiling, the wiring, the dispatch office, both talking floors, both dormitories, until it dies in a photoreceptor disc at the back and is reborn as a signal. The signal flows outward, from that disc through the bipolar, edited by the horizontal and amacrine staff, into a ganglion cell, down its bare axon, and out of the eye toward the brain, a million fibres strong. Around it all sits the pigment sheet that renews the light catchers every single day, and through it all runs the Muller cell, the quiet scaffold that keeps the wall transparent enough for the first flow to work at all. Keep those two arrows in your head and you will never lose your place in the ten layers again. And keep the home rule too: densities in episode 74, pigment epithelium in episode 75, and next week, the centre of it all.
