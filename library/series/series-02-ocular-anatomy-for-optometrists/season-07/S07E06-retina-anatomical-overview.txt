# Description: Where does the retina begin and end, and what are its named regions? In this anatomy episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, our Guide maps the innermost coat of the eye from the optic disc to the scalloped ora serrata, names the posterior pole, the equatorial band and the far periphery, and walks the thickness ramp from a tenth of a millimetre at the forward edge to about half a millimetre at the back. A geography lesson for optometry students, practising optometrists and curious listeners, setting up the retinal layers that arrive next episode.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library that takes one topic at a time and gives it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, series two, and we are deep in season seven, Vitreous and Posterior Segment, at episode six. For five episodes we have been living inside the vitreous gel. Episode one, Vitreous Body, introduced the gel itself. Episode two, Vitreous Cortex, showed its condensed outer rind. Episode three, Vitreous Base, found its strongest grip. Episode four, Vitreous Attachments, mapped every adhesion site across the back of the eye. And in episode five, Vitreous Chambers and Spaces, we threaded the Cloquet canal through the gel to its quiet landing place near the disc. Today we finally step off the gel and onto the wall it cradles: the retina. And because a wall this important should never be toured blind, today is map day. Walk us in.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to every listener. Today is pure geography, and I want to be honest about what that means, because an overview is not a lesser episode. It is the frame that keeps every later episode honest. We will answer three questions. First, extent: exactly how far does the retina reach, and where does it begin and end. Second, regions: what are the official names on the fundus map, from the posterior pole to the far periphery. Third, thickness: how thick is this sheet as you travel from its forward edge back toward the pole. And I will tell you what today is not. Episode seven, Retinal Layers, walks the ten layers one by one. Episode eight, Macula, measures the central patch and its zones. Episode nine, Fovea and Foveola, descends into the pit. Episode ten, Optic Disc, gives the nerve head its due. In series one we had an episode called The Retina as the Sensory Layer, and there we asked what the retina does. Today we ask where it lies, how far it reaches, and how the sheet is organised - the anatomy underneath that story.

Host: Then let us pin the sheet into place before we draw its borders. Remind us where the retina sits among the coats of the eye, because we have spent whole seasons on those neighbours. Give us the three of them in order, from the outside in, and tell me exactly where our sheet lives in that stack.

Guide: Gladly, because the neighbourhood explains the organ. The globe wears three coats, one inside the other. The outer coat is the fibrous tunic: the tough white sclera, which we mapped in season three, episode eight, Scleral Structure, and the clear cornea bulging forward from it. The middle coat is the vascular tunic, the uvea: iris, ciliary body and choroid, the territory we walked through season five, ending at the episode called Choroid. And the innermost coat is our subject today, the nervous tunic: the retina. It is a thin, soft, nearly transparent membrane lining the inner surface of the back of the eye, and anatomically it is the most remarkable of the three, because it is not really a wall at all. It is neural tissue - a piece of the brain, developmentally speaking, pressed against the inside of the globe. Hold that thought as we work. Today it is a sheet with borders, and we are drawing them.

Host: Every map needs anchors, two at least. You said the retina stretches from the optic disc to the ora serrata. Take me to the rear anchor first. What exactly is the optic disc, and what is it doing for our sheet?

Guide: The optic disc is where the sheet is pinned to the exit door. Everything the retina gathers across its whole extent has to leave the eye, and it leaves at one round opening in the back wall, where the nerve fibres funnel together and become the optic nerve. The disc measures about one and a half millimetres across, side to side, and is a little taller than it is wide. Here is why it matters to a map maker. It is fixed, round and famously easy to identify, so optometrists use the disc as the built in ruler of the fundus: when we describe a finding we say it sits so many disc diameters from something, and each of those diameters is about one and a half millimetres. The rim, the cup and everything else about the disc itself get their full treatment in episode ten, Optic Disc. For today it is simply our rear anchor.

Host: And now the other coordinate of the back of the fundus: the fovea. Give me the geometry between the disc and the fovea, because that little relationship is one of the first things an examiner learns to find, and I want the numbers exactly right.

Guide: Here is the relationship. The disc sits on the nasal side of the posterior pole, and the fovea sits temporal to it. The centre of the fovea lies roughly four millimetres temporal to the centre of the disc, and a shade below the level of the disc centre - an offset so small that beginners routinely miss it, and so consistent that once you have seen it you never lose it again. Between the two runs the busiest bundle of nerve fibres in the entire sheet, the traffic corridor from the centre to the exit door; its fibre geography waits for season eight, episode ten, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship. One more anatomical fact for the road. The disc contains no photoreceptors at all - it is pure wiring, no sensors - which is why it is the blind spot you met back in series one. The full census of receptors across the sheet belongs to season eight, episode four, Photoreceptor Distribution.

Host: That is the rear anchor settled, then. Now take me forward, all the way to the other end of the sheet. What is the ora serrata, where exactly do we find it, and why does it carry such a strange, jagged sounding name?

Guide: The ora serrata is the forward edge of the retina, the line where the nervous sheet proper comes to an end. On the horizontal meridian it sits about six and a half millimetres behind the limbus on the nasal side, and about eight millimetres behind the limbus on the temporal side, because the ciliary body is a touch wider on the temporal side of the eye. And here is the beautiful part. The retina does not stop dead at this line like cloth cut with scissors. The nervous tissue ends, but its epithelial continuation sails on: the sheet changes costume and continues forward as the non-pigmented epithelium that roofs the ciliary body - the territory of season five, episode seven, Ciliary Body - and then onward onto the back surface of the iris. As for the name, ora means edge or border, and serrata means notched, like a saw. You will see why in a moment.

Host: Then let us look closely at that edge, because the name promises jagged scenery. If I could float forward along the inner surface of the retina and arrive at the ora serrata, what would I actually see? Describe the border for us, tooth by tooth if you can.

Guide: Picture a scalloped shoreline. The retina pushes forward little pointed tongues of tissue called dentate processes, the teeth of the saw, and between them the edge recedes in rounded gaps called oral bays, the bays between the headlands. The pattern is not the same all the way around. Nasally, the teeth are long and the serrations are bold and obvious. Temporally, the border is much more restrained - the edge often runs almost straight, with weak, shallow serrations or none at all. Occasionally you find a meridional fold, a straight ridge of retinal tissue running forward toward a dentate process, like a pier jutting from the shore. And one number to carry with you: this is the thinnest stretch of the entire sheet. At the ora the retina is about a tenth of a millimetre thick - a damp sheet of paper. Remember that number, because our third question today is the thickness map, and this is where it starts.

Host: Now I want the clinical honesty that keeps this series grounded. Why does an optometrist care so much about this thin, scalloped, faraway edge of the sheet? It is a long way from the lights of the consulting room, and it never gets to complain.

Guide: It cares for a beautifully simple anatomical reason. Come back with me to episode three, Vitreous Base, where we found the strongest attachment in the whole vitreous: a band of gel gripping the eye in a ring that straddles this very line, holding the forward few millimetres of retina and the ciliary epithelium behind and ahead of the ora. So at the ora serrata you have the thinnest retina in the eye under the firmest grip of the gel, with traction possible whenever the gel pulls. Add that this border is also the hardest region to see, because the periphery curves out of view and often needs gentle indentation to bring it into the line of sight, and you have the anatomical recipe that makes the far periphery a zone optometrists examine with real respect. Structure sets the agenda. We stay with anatomy today, but you can already hear the clinical echo.

Host: Let me zoom out for a definition that has been floating around our whole season, because everyone keeps saying posterior segment as though everyone agrees. Where does the posterior segment actually begin and end, and how does our sheet fit inside it?

Guide: The clean anatomical answer: the posterior segment is everything behind the lens. In front of the lens lies the anterior segment, with its chambers and its angle, the territory of season five. Behind it you have the vitreous gel, our host for five episodes, the retina lining the wall, the choroid beneath the retina, and the optic nerve leaving through the back. So the retina is the innermost lining of the posterior segment - the wallpaper of the back room, if you like, though wallpaper is far too stiff a word for tissue a tenth of a millimetre thick in places. That is the frame we are working in this season: gel inside, retina at the wall, and the disc as the exit. Now that the frame is set, we can walk the regions of the fundus from back to front, and the names will finally have somewhere to sit.

Host: Then let us walk the map from the back forward. Start at the very back of the fundus and give me the official regional names, one by one, in the order I would meet them if I set out from the pole and travelled toward the ora.

Guide: At the very back is the posterior pole, the working heart of the fundus. It is the region containing the two structures every examination visits: the optic disc, our rear anchor, and the macula beside it. A practical way to bound it: the posterior pole is roughly the territory framed by the temporal vascular arcades, the pairs of blood vessels that leave the disc above and below and arch outward around the central region. Vessels make honest fence posts, because they are always visible. The full story of those vessels - where they come from, how they divide, and how the retina shares its feeding with the choroid - belongs to season eight, episode one, Retinal Blood Supply. For today they are simply the frame around the back room. So: posterior pole, disc on the nasal side, macula on the temporal side, arcades arcing above and below. That is the heart of the fundus.

Host: You have now said the word macula twice, and I am conscious we are only waving at it. Tell us what the macula is as a region on this map - just its geography, I take it - and then be honest with me about what we are deliberately postponing.

Guide: As a region, the macula is the central patch of the posterior pole, centred on the fovea and lying temporal to the disc - the zone of the retina that handles the middle of your vision. That is the whole geographic statement for today, and it is enough for a map. What we are postponing is everything else, and there is a lot of it: the ring by ring definitions and diameters, the yellow pigment that gives the region its name and its faint colour in the living eye, and the crowding of cells that makes it special. All of that gets its own episode in just two weeks: episode eight, Macula. And remember, in series one we already learned what this region buys a person, in an episode called The Macula and Central Vision. Today we place it on the map; episode eight will furnish the room.

Host: Moving forward from the pole, then. Somewhere along our journey toward the ora there is a landmark part way, and I have heard it called the equator. What is the equator of the retina, where does it sit, and what do we call the fundus regions on either side of it?

Guide: The equator is the widest girth of the globe, the line where the eye is fattest, and on the fundus map it sits roughly six millimetres behind the ora serrata. Around it lies a band of retina a few millimetres wide on either side, and that band is what retina specialists call the equatorial fundus, or the mid-periphery. Forward of the band, from it to the ora serrata, is the far periphery - the oral region, the last strip before the edge. Backward of the band, from it to the posterior pole, the fundus is described as mid-peripheral as it approaches the pole. These are working names, agreed by convention so that when one clinician writes equatorial and another reads the note, the same patch of retina comes to mind. A map is only useful if we all agree on the place names, and now we do.

Host: Which raises the practical matter of addresses. When an optometrist finds something on the fundus during an examination - an unusual patch, a small lesion - how is its position actually written down? Give me the coordinate system we use at the chair, in real records.

Guide: Three devices, used together. First, the quadrant: the fundus is divided by a vertical and a horizontal line through the centre, giving superior and inferior, nasal and temporal quadrants, so a finding can be assigned to, say, the superior nasal quadrant. Second, clock hours: you imagine the face of a clock laid over the fundus, twelve at the top, and a finding can sit from one to three o'clock, which is finer than a quadrant. Third, distance in disc diameters: how far the finding lies from a named landmark - the disc, the fovea or the ora - measured with the disc as the ruler. Put them together and you get a sentence like this: a small pale patch, two o'clock, about two disc diameters behind the ora serrata. Any colleague who reads that note can steer straight to it. This is anatomy in its most practical costume, and it keeps faith with the recording discipline series one taught in an episode called Recording the Examination.

Host: And now to the third question of the day, the one you planted at the very start. Take me on the thickness journey of the retina, from the ora serrata back to the posterior pole, and give me the numbers at the milestones as we go.

Guide: The journey is a steady ramp. We start at the ora serrata, the thinnest point of the entire sheet, about a tenth of a millimetre - a thin, damp sheet of paper. As we travel backward through the far periphery and the equatorial band, the sheet quietly thickens. By the time we reach the posterior pole it approaches half a millimetre, and it is thickest of all in the ring of tissue around the back, near the disc and around the macular region. That pair of numbers - a tenth of a millimetre at the forward edge, about half a millimetre at the back - is the classic textbook summary of retinal thickness. And it is not just textbook. Modern scanning, optical coherence tomography, measures this ramp in living eyes every day and confirms the same gradient, edge to pole. The map we are drawing is one you can see in clinic.

Host: A ramp like that demands an explanation, because we are still talking about one continuous sheet of tissue. Why should the forward edge be paper thin while the back is five times thicker? What is the retina quietly stacking up as it travels backward?

Guide: Wiring. The retina is nervous tissue, and nervous tissue is not distributed evenly. Toward the back of the sheet, the processing machinery stacks up - the cells that receive, relay and begin the work of vision, and the fibres that will carry the result to the disc. The farther back you go, the more of that machinery lies in the column of tissue you are standing in, and the thicker the sheet. Toward the ora, almost all of that is left behind, and you are left with the thinnest possible remnant of the same fabric. Two later episodes will finish this thought properly: episode seven, Retinal Layers, takes the census of cells and the ten layers they build, and season eight, episode ten, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, follows the fibres as they converge and bundle toward the disc. Today we simply register the consequence: the sheet thickens as its wiring accumulates.

Host: Before we close the thickness map, I want to catch something in your wording. You said the sheet is thickest around the back, near the disc and macula - but I have also heard that the very centre of the back is a thin spot. Is there a dip hiding in the middle of our thickest region?

Guide: Well caught. There is a dip, and it is one of the most elegant facts in the whole fundus. At the centre of the macula, the inner part of the sheet - the wiring we just spoke of - is displaced sideways, to let light reach the very centre with the fewest obstacles. The result is a shallow pit at the middle of the thickest region of the retina: a thin point, ringed by the thickest tissue in the eye. So the thickness map has two extremes, not one: the thinnest edge at the ora serrata, and a local thin point at the centre of the posterior pole, sitting inside the broadest, thickest surround. The architecture of that pit - how it forms, what it costs, and why it repays the cost - is exactly the story of episode nine, Fovea and Foveola. Today it is a dip on our map, and it belongs there.

Host: One border of the sheet we have not mentioned at all today, and it may be the most intimate one: the outside. A paper-thin nervous sheet cannot just float. What does the retina lean against, and what lies on the far side of it?

Guide: It leans on its partner. Against the outer face of the nervous retina sits the retinal pigment epithelium, a single sheet of dark, brick-like pigmented cells, and behind that a thin basement membrane complex called Bruch membrane, and behind that the choriocapillaris, the rich vascular carpet that is the deepest layer of the choroid. You have met the choroid before, in season five, episode nine, Choroid. The pigment epithelium and Bruch membrane get their own full episodes early in season eight: Retinal Pigment Epithelium, and then Bruch's Membrane. For today, the geography is what matters: the nervous retina is a thin sheet pressed against this supporting complex, and that partnership is why the retina can be so thin and still be fed. The two blood systems - the one inside the retina and the one beneath it - and the watershed between them are held for season eight, episode one and its neighbours.

Host: Here is a question I have been saving since the beginning of this episode. The retina is nervous tissue, and nervous tissue, everywhere else in the body, is opaque greyness. Yet the books all describe the retina as transparent. How does a living sheet of nerve tissue manage to be see-through?

Guide: Because it is thin, orderly and wet with perfectly aligned structure. Transparency is what thin, regular tissue does; cloudiness is what happens when order is lost. The living retina is so transparent that when an optometrist looks into the eye, they look straight through this nervous sheet as if it were glass, to see the pigment epithelium and choroid beneath it and the vessels running within the sheet itself. Its deeper layout - the way light must actually cross the inner wiring before it reaches the receptors tucked against the pigment epithelium - is precisely the story of episode seven, Retinal Layers, and I will not spoil it. But hold the anatomical point: the transparency depends on the fine structure staying intact and in place. When the sheet is lifted from its bed, as in a detachment, it loses that transparency and turns grey and opaque. The retina is living architecture, not wallpaper, and its optical behaviour is a property of its anatomy.

Host: That is the whole tour, and it deserves a recap before we let it settle. Walk me through the map one last time, slowly, from the rear anchor to the far edge, with the numbers attached, so I can carry it out of this episode in one piece.

Guide: Here is the map. The retina is the innermost coat of the eye, a thin, nearly transparent nervous sheet. Behind, it is anchored at the optic disc, about one and a half millimetres across, sitting roughly four millimetres nasal to the fovea, which itself lies a shade below the level of the disc centre. In front, it ends at the ora serrata, about six and a half millimetres behind the nasal limbus and about eight behind the temporal limbus, its edge scalloped into dentate processes and oral bays, boldest nasally, and there it changes costume into the ciliary epithelium. Between the anchors lie the regions: the posterior pole framed by the temporal arcades, the equatorial band about six millimetres behind the ora, and the far periphery between the band and the edge. And the thickness runs as a ramp: a tenth of a millimetre at the ora, approaching half a millimetre at the pole, with one dip at the foveal centre. Regions, borders, numbers. That is the map.

Host: It is a good map, and I notice how quietly useful it already is - the addresses we give to findings in records, the respect the far periphery deserves, the ramp a scanner traces in a living eye. But that leaves the question our listeners always ask at this point in the season. What arrives next week?

Guide: Next week the sheet stops being a blank outline and gets its machinery. When you finish it, the thickness ramp will have faces and names attached to it, the transparency question will be answered properly, and the dip at the fovea will begin to make architectural sense. Bring this map with you. We are going to furnish every room on it, one layer at a time.
