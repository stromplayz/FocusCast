# Description: Inside the gel that fills most of the eye there are quiet, liquid-lined rooms: a canal that runs the whole length of the vitreous, a space at the back of the lens, a funnel over the optic disc, and a pocket resting in front of the macula. In this episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks traces the Cloquet canal from the Berger space to the area of Martegiano, uncovers its origin as the ghost of the fetal hyaloid artery, and maps the prepapillary and premacular configurations of the gel. Expect real dimensions, real eponyms, and the anatomical leftovers every optometrist should recognise at the slit lamp. This is part five of ten in our season on the vitreous and posterior segment.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, the audio library where we take one topic at a time and give it the room it deserves. This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series together, and we are five episodes into season seven, which is devoted to the vitreous and the posterior segment of the eye. Here is how far we have travelled. In episode one, Vitreous Body, we met the gel itself, about four millilitres of it, ninety-nine percent water held in a delicate framework, filling the globe from the lens to the retina. In episode two, Vitreous Cortex, we studied its condensed outer shell. In episode three, Vitreous Base, we visited the strongest anchor of them all, straddling the ora serrata. And in episode four, Vitreous Attachments, we mapped every place the gel grips the back of the eye. Today we go somewhere that sounds like a contradiction. Today we explore the empty rooms inside the gel, its chambers and spaces, from a long curved canal running through its heart, to a quiet pocket resting over the macula. And if you have been with us since the beginning, you may remember that all the way back in Series 1, an episode called The Back of the Eye introduced this gel simply as the transparent jelly that fills the eye. Today, that jelly gets its floor plan. Our guide is with me as always. Welcome.

Guide: Thank you, and welcome to every listener. Let me begin with an image, because it will carry the whole episode. Think of the vitreous not as a shapeless blob of jam, but as a building. It has walls, which we have spent four episodes studying. It has a facade against the lens and a long back wall against the retina. And like any good building, it has interior architecture. Rooms. Corridors. A canal that runs through the entire structure from one end to the other, a small round space at the back of the lens, a funnel at the optic nerve head, and a shallow pocket floating in front of the macula. These are the vitreous chambers and spaces, and they are far from being trivia. They mark where the gel was built, where its blood supply once ran, and where it holds on loosely. Today I will name every one of them, give you their dimensions, and tell you the remarkable developmental story that explains why they exist at all. But before we can walk through the rooms, we need one definition, because there is a puzzle hiding in the very title of this episode.

Host: I am glad you said that, because I felt the puzzle immediately. The vitreous is, as you told us in episode one, about ninety-nine percent water. It sounds as if it is already almost entirely space. So what is left over to be a space inside something that is basically water?

Guide: A fair challenge, and answering it properly will make the rest of the episode easy. Remember the recipe of the vitreous. It is water, yes, but the water is not loose. It is held in a three dimensional scaffold made of collagen fibrils, collagen being the body's rope protein, woven into threads far thinner than a wavelength of light, all of it swollen with hyaluronic acid, a sugar molecule that drinks water and keeps the whole structure inflated. That combination makes a gel, a state of matter in which liquid is trapped inside a net. Now, a space inside the vitreous is simply a region where the net thins out or disappears altogether. The fibrils part company, and the trapped water stands free as liquid vitreous, a watery pocket living quietly inside the jelly. Two cautions before we go further, because both will matter today. First, these spaces are not lined by any membrane. There is no skin around them, no wall of cells, nothing you could lift away with forceps. A wall exists only where fibrils end and liquid begins. Second, in the living young eye most of them are potential spaces, their sides lying collapsed against each other like a folded umbrella, ready to open when liquid gathers. Keep both ideas in hand, because every room we visit today obeys these two rules.

Host: Then let us take the rooms in order of drama, and the winner is clearly the corridor you teased in your list, the one that runs the whole length of the gel. What is it called, where does it begin, and where does it end?

Guide: It is called the Cloquet canal, and anatomy also knows it by its older name, the hyaloid canal. Picture a curved tunnel with its back end at the optic disc and its front end at the posterior pole of the lens, the very centre of the back surface of the lens. It runs the entire length of the vitreous cavity, and it is not straight. It curves gently twice, an S-shaped course, bending one way and then the other as it threads through the gel. At each end it opens out like a funnel, wide and roomy, and near the middle of the vitreous it narrows to its tightest waist. Inside it there is no gel to speak of. The canal is a zone where the collagen fibrils are sparse or absent, so liquid vitreous fills it. And I must stress the word zone, because the canal has no true wall. Nothing encloses it. It is simply the place where the fibrils of the surrounding gel turn away and leave a liquid corridor behind. The name honours the anatomist Jules Germain Cloquet, who described it in the early nineteenth century, and the older name, hyaloid canal, comes from a Greek word for glass, the same root that gives us the word hyaline all over ocular anatomy. Of all the spaces inside the gel, it is the largest and the most constant.

Host: Here is what I want to know before anything else, because it seems like strange design. The eye is a masterpiece of tight packing. Why on earth would it leave a tunnel running through its most delicate interior? Was there always a corridor there? Did something used to travel through it?

Guide: You have put your finger on the single most beautiful fact in this episode. The canal is a ghost. It is the dried-up riverbed of a river that flowed before we were born. Let me tell that story properly, because it explains the shape as well as the existence of the canal. In the early weeks of development, the eye grows as a bubble of the brain, and the space between the budding lens and the future retina is filled by the first generation of vitreous, which anatomists call the primary vitreous. Around the fifth or sixth week, the hyaloid artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery, the main arterial trunk of the eye, grows backward into that space through the optic stalk, the embryonic stem of the optic nerve. It runs through the primary vitreous, straight to the back of the lens, and there it blossoms into a fine capillary net that wraps the lens like a basket. That net is the tunica vasculosa lentis, the vascular coat of the lens, and it is the lens's food supply while it grows, because a developing lens cannot yet feed itself. Meanwhile, around this vascular primary vitreous, the eye lays down the permanent, clear, avascular gel that will serve for life, the secondary vitreous. And then, as birth approaches, in the last months of pregnancy, the whole vascular system withers away, strand by strand. The lens learns to feed from the aqueous instead, a change of kitchen we described back in Season 6, episode seven, Lens Nutrition. The artery vanishes almost without a trace. But the corridor it occupied, the core of the old primary vitreous, remains embedded in the permanent gel for the rest of our lives. That corridor is the Cloquet canal.

Host: So the tunnel is a kind of birthmark. Give me its dimensions then, in the adult eye. How long is this corridor, how wide, and what shape does it hold?

Guide: Take it end to end. From the optic disc to the back of the lens is, in an adult eye, roughly sixteen to seventeen millimetres along the visual axis, and the canal spans that whole distance. In shape it is two funnels joined tip to tip, a wide mouth at each end and the narrowest part of the channel close to the middle of the gel. In a child or a young adult the canal is usually a potential space, its walls lying collapsed against each other, because the surrounding gel is firm and full. As the years pass, more and more of the gel turns from jelly to liquid, and the canal, being liquid already, simply gains room, so in older eyes it opens out and becomes genuinely visible on scanning. And here is the practical point that organises the rest of our tour. Each funnel of the canal, the one at the lens and the one at the disc, is important enough to carry its own name. So let us take the ends one at a time, starting at the front, at the lens.

Host: Front end first then. The canal arrives at the very centre of the back surface of the lens. What does it find waiting for it there, and what does anatomy call that meeting place?

Guide: Turn the lens around in your mind and look at its back surface. The posterior pole of the lens does not press flat against the vitreous. Instead, the front face of the vitreous, the anterior hyaloid face, carries a shallow hollow that cradles the lens like a ball sitting in a cup. That hollow is the patellar fossa, from the Latin for a little dish. Now, just behind the lens, the anterior face of the vitreous is stitched to the lens capsule by a ring-shaped ligament called the hyaloideocapsular ligament of Wieger, a circular adhesion about eight to nine millimetres across. But a ring has a middle, and in the middle of Wieger's ring the lens capsule and the hyaloid face are not joined at all. There, at the centre of the posterior pole of the lens, lies a small round gap about one to two millimetres wide. That is the Berger space. It is the anterior mouth of the Cloquet canal, the place where the canal's front funnel opens against the lens, and it corresponds exactly to the spot where the hyaloid artery once pushed against the lens it was feeding. So the geography is tidy and worth saying twice. Wieger's ring grips the rim, eight to nine millimetres across, and inside the ring, one to two millimetres wide, the Berger space holds the doorway.

Host: I like that we can bolt this onto season six, because we gave the lens ten whole episodes and I remember the lens being described as suspended, held from every side. Where does this ring, Wieger's ring, fit into that suspension story? Is it part of the same scaffolding as the zonules?

Guide: It is the innermost handshake of that whole suspension system. In Season 6, episode five, Lens Zonules, we followed the zonular fibres, those astonishingly fine threads that sling the lens from the ciliary body, and in the following episode we mapped where they land. The lens hangs from those fibres in front and at its equator, and behind, it simply rests in the patellar fossa of the vitreous. Wieger's hyaloideocapsular ligament is the backmost stitch of the arrangement, the place where lens capsule and vitreous face are sewn together around the Berger space. There is even an embryological family tie between the two tissues. Anatomists describe three generations of vitreous, primary, secondary and tertiary, and the tertiary vitreous, the last-born of the three, is precisely the tissue that condenses into the zonules. So lens and vitreous are relatives, not merely neighbours. One flag before we leave the lens behind, because the border of our scope matters. Out beyond Wieger's ring, between the zonular sheets and around the back of the lens, anatomy names a few other small spaces, Petit's canal and Hannover's canal among them, but they belong to the lens and posterior chamber side of the map. We walked that territory in Season 5, episode two, Posterior Chamber Anatomy, so today we stay inside the gel proper.

Host: Now the back end. The canal's other funnel arrives at the optic disc, the blind spot we have circled all season without landing on. What does that end of the corridor look like, and what is its name?

Guide: At the disc, the canal opens into the most elegant of the vitreous spaces, the area of Martegiano. To place it, remember from episode two that the vitreous wears a condensed outer coat, the cortex, a shell of densely packed fibrils. Over most of the back of the eye, that shell lies flat against the inner surface of the retina. But over the optic disc, it does something remarkable. It stops. The cortex has a round defect there, a genuine hole in the shell, called the prepapillary hole, and around its rim the fibrils gather into a stout, distinct ring. That hole is no accident and no flaw. The disc is the exit door of the eye, the spot where the nerve fibres, well over a million of them, turn and leave the globe as the optic nerve, and the surface of the disc itself measures only about one and a half millimetres across. There is no sensory tissue over the centre of the disc for a cortex sheet to sit on, so the shell parts around it. Seen from the side, the hole is not a simple round window either. It deepens into a funnel, wide at the front, narrowing backward, its tip centred over the disc like a trumpet lying on its side. That funnel-shaped space is the area of Martegiano, its mouth roughly four millimetres across, and the posterior funnel of the Cloquet canal opens straight into it.

Host: Then let me join the dots carefully, because the picture suddenly clicks into one piece. The Berger space at the lens, the canal through the middle, the Martegiano funnel at the disc. Are we describing one single, continuous corridor from the front of the gel to the back?

Guide: Exactly one corridor. A single liquid pathway runs from the centre of the back of the lens, through the waist of the gel, and out into the funnel over the disc. In the young eye it is mostly a potential space, walls apposed, and that is why a student dissecting a fresh eye may struggle to find it. Give it reason to open, and it declares itself. With age, as liquid collects inside the gel, the corridor widens and becomes traceable on optical coherence tomography, the scanning technique so many of us use every working day. And there is one classic moment when the disc-end anatomy shows itself directly to the clinician. When the gel finally separates from the disc, the stout ring of fibrils around the prepapillary hole comes away with it and is seen floating behind the lens as a small, translucent ring, which clinicians call a Weiss ring. I mention it only as anatomy made visible, a cast of the very hole we have been describing. The wider story around that separation is a tale for another day. Today it is simply proof that the room exists.

Host: You called the canal a ghost of an artery, and a dried river usually leaves debris along its banks. The artery touched the eye at two places, the disc where it entered and the lens where it fed. Are there leftovers at either end, things we might actually bump into in the clinic?

Guide: There are two calling cards, one at each end, and both are favourites in the examination room. At the lens, look for the Mittendorf dot, a tiny, round, whitish fleck sitting on the posterior lens capsule, usually just to the nasal side of the posterior pole, the very point where the hyaloid artery tugged at the lens before it vanished. It is a fixed, harmless landmark, and patients carry it through life without ever knowing. At the disc, look for the Bergmeister papilla, a small tuft or veil of fibrous glial tissue standing proud of the disc surface, all that remains of the glial sheath that wrapped the artery where it entered the nerve. The word papilla simply means a small nipple-shaped projection, and that is exactly the shape. Neither remnant is a disease, and neither usually disturbs vision, though both are classic normal findings that a student must learn to recognise, to name, and then to leave alone. And notice how neatly they complete the story we began. The canal is the riverbed, the Berger space is the mouth of the river at the lake it fed, and these two are the pebbles the river left behind on its way out.

Host: That is the corridor's whole biography, from development to leftovers. Now let me ask the practical question, honestly. When does an optometrist ever see these spaces? They are collapsed, they have no walls, and they sit inside a perfectly clear gel.

Guide: Through three windows, each worth knowing. The first is imaging. On optical coherence tomography, the premacular pocket shows up as a dark, fluid-filled crescent in a good number of adult eyes, and the walls of the canal can sometimes be traced as faint parallel lines running through the gel. The second is the slit lamp. Dilate a young patient, swing a good beam behind the lens, and the anterior funnel of the canal and the cup of the patellar fossa can be glimpsed, while the Mittendorf dot is a routine catch on the posterior capsule. The third window we have already met, the moment the gel separates from the disc and the floating Weiss ring announces the prepapillary hole. The clinical wisdom here is recognition, not intervention. Every one of these structures is normal anatomy, quietly present in almost every eye, and knowing the floor plan is precisely what stops a normal dot, ring or crescent from being misread as something sinister. Anatomy first, always. That has been the motto of this whole series.

Host: Which brings us to the room you promised and have not yet delivered, the one with the best address in the entire eye. You said there is a pocket sitting directly in front of the macula, the patch of retina that does our sharpest, most precious seeing. Tell me about that one.

Guide: The premacular configuration, and it deserves careful handling. The posterior cortex of the vitreous is not the same thickness everywhere. Over the macula, it is at its thinnest, stretched over our most important retina like fine silk. And just in front of that thinned cortex lies a shallow pocket of liquid vitreous, a few millimetres across, sitting over the macula like a lens of water laid against the wall. The classical name is the bursa premacularis, the premacular bursa, bursa being the Latin word for a purse or pouch. In the scanning era it has become famous under a newer name, the posterior precortical vitreous pocket, mapped in exquisite detail by the Japanese researchers Kishi and Shimizu late in the twentieth century. Its boundaries are worth memorising. In front lies the thin premacular cortex. Behind lies the inner limiting membrane, the smooth, glassy basement membrane that the retina lays down along its innermost surface, the true lining between retina and gel, whose place among the retinal layers we will take up properly in episode seven. Between the two sits the pocket. It is the same kind of space as the canal, fibril-poor, membraneless, a potential room, but pressed flat against the wall of the eye rather than running free through the middle of the gel.

Host: Let me push on the logic for a moment, because it seems completely backwards. Of all the places to keep a slippery pocket of water, why would the eye choose the one spot where vision is sharpest? Surely the gel should hold that area most firmly of all?

Guide: The design is subtler than it first appears, and the answer is motion. The gel is a mass with weight, and every eye movement swings it, gently, hundreds of thousands of times a day. Over the macula, the eye solves that problem with a glide plane. The pocket of liquid lets the bulk of the gel slide smoothly over the macula rather than dragging on it, a built-in slip layer between two surfaces that must not grip each other carelessly. And now step back with me, because a pattern emerges across the whole episode. Where does the vitreous hold on firmly? In rings. Wieger's ring at the lens, a hoop eight to nine millimetres wide. The fibrillar ring around the prepapillary hole at the disc. As episode four showed, the gel's holds are discrete hoops rather than broad sheets, and inside every hoop the gel leaves the centre free. The rooms we have toured today are the interiors of those hoops, the quiet centres the gel deliberately does not fill. As for the macula itself, its zones, its yellow pigment, its dense crowd of ganglion cells, those are stories with their own episodes later this season, so today the wall stays closed and we admire the room in front of it.

Host: One more architectural question, and then I promise we will walk out of the building. Do these rooms connect with each other, or are they separate pockets of quiet, each with its own locked door?

Guide: In many eyes they communicate, and the route is exactly where you would now guess. The premacular pocket drains backward through a narrow channel toward the prepapillary hole, so liquid can pass from the bursa over the macula into the funnel of Martegiano, and from there down the Cloquet canal to the Berger space behind the lens. Picture one connected waterway threading the gel from front to back, with a side branch resting over the macula. The exact plumbing varies from eye to eye, and in the young eye most of it lies collapsed, but the principle holds firm. The gel is not a sealed jelly. It carries a liquid route through its centre, and that route is the fossil of the artery that built the eye in the first place.

Host: This has been a lot of names for one episode, and I want to carry all of them out of the room with me. Have you got a trick for holding the whole map at once, without the card index?

Guide: Use the river, because the river remembers itself. Imagine a river that once ran through the gel, rising at the disc, where the hyaloid artery entered, and flowing forward to the lake behind the lens, which it fed. Then imagine the river drying up before birth. The dry bed across the gel is the Cloquet canal, sixteen to seventeen millimetres of ghost corridor. The lake at its front end, one to two millimetres wide, ringed by Wieger's eight to nine millimetre hoop, is the Berger space. The delta where the river rose, the funnel roughly four millimetres across over the disc, is the area of Martegiano. Beside the bed, a puddle rests over the macula, the premacular bursa, our posterior precortical vitreous pocket. And along the bed lie two pebbles, the Mittendorf dot on the back of the lens and the Bergmeister papilla at the disc. Say it once more, slowly. Spring at the disc, lake at the lens, bed between them, puddle over the macula, two pebbles left behind. That single image is the entire episode, and it will still be with you in the examination room.
