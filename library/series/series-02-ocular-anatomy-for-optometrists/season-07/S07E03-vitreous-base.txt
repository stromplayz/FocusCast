# Description: In episode three of the Vitreous and Posterior Segment season, Focus Cast by focuslinks zooms in on the vitreous base, the strongest grip the gel of the eye has anywhere inside the globe. Learn where this three to four millimetre ribbon sits across the saw-toothed ora serrata, how its collagen fibrils are stitched into the inner limiting membrane of the peripheral retina and the basement membrane of the ciliary epithelium, and why the grip never loosens with age. The Guide explains what retinal dialysis, giant tears and vitreous base avulsion have to do with blunt trauma, why the collapsing gel of posterior vitreous detachment spares the base, and how the pars plana provides the safe surgical corridor for vitrectomy. A clear, anatomy-first episode for optometry students, optometrists and eye care teams.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to season seven of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, the season in which we walk the vitreous and the back of the eye, one structure at a time. Last episode, episode two, Vitreous Cortex, took us along the condensed outer shell of the gel, where the collagen gathers densely, and where those quiet resident cells, the hyalocytes, sit like park keepers on the edge of the estate. Today we stay in the same neighbourhood, but we stop at the one spot where this gel absolutely refuses to let go. Of every surface the vitreous touches inside the eye, there is a single zone where its grip is fiercest, a narrow ribbon that encircles the eye like a belt. It has a name, it has millimetres attached to it, and it leaves consequences all over clinic and theatre. Guide, set the scene for us. What is the vitreous base?

Guide: Thank you, and let me open with the one-sentence version. The vitreous base is the zone in which the cortex of the vitreous, that condensed outer layer we met last episode, becomes welded to the tissues beneath it, and it does so more firmly than anywhere else in the eye. Every attachment the vitreous has, and it has several, is graded on a scale of grip, and this one sits at the very top. It also sits at a very specific address. It straddles the ora serrata, the saw-toothed front edge of the sensory retina, so that the zone lies partly on the retina behind the ora and partly on the ciliary body in front of it. Here is my plan for the hour in miniature. We will find the address, take the measurements, open the mechanism of the grip, and then follow the consequences, because a structure this strong leaves its fingerprints in trauma, in detachment, and in surgery.

Host: Let us take the address first, then. You said the zone straddles something called the ora serrata, and I want to unpack that name before we go anywhere else. I can picture the retina as a thin sheet lining the back of the eye, but I have never really pictured it ending. Where is this ora serrata, and what exactly am I looking at when I stand there?

Guide: Picture the sensory retina, the paper-thin, light-catching sheet, stretched as a lining over the inner back of the eye. Now walk forward along it, away from the optic disc, out toward the front of the eye. The retina does not march all the way to the ciliary body and stop politely at a border. It stops with a jagged edge, a scalloped coastline of tiny forward-pointing teeth separated by small bays, and anatomists named it with perfect honesty, the ora serrata, the saw-toothed mouth. So just behind the teeth, the sensory retina ends. In front of the teeth begins the flat, quiet back strip of the ciliary body, called the pars plana, roughly four millimetres wide, and in front of that lies the pleated, wrinkled part, the pars plicata, with its ciliary processes, another two millimetres or so. That flat strip is going to matter enormously later in our story, so hold on to it. And one promise about boundaries today. The full journey from disc to teeth, with the whole coastline of the retinal periphery, is episode six, Retina: Anatomical Overview. We are standing on one landmark only.

Host: Something you just said caught my ear, and I want to pull the thread. You were careful with your words, that the sensory retina ends behind the teeth. Careful how? Does something of the retina continue forward past the ora, into the ciliary body, or does the tissue simply stop and hand over to something completely different?

Guide: It continues, and this is one of the elegant facts hiding at this address. What stops at the ora serrata is the sensory machinery, the layered photoreceptor part of the retina. But the retina and the ciliary body are built from one continuous sheet of neuroectoderm, the embryonic tissue from which the inner eye is fashioned. So when the sensory layers stop, the innermost epithelium simply carries on forward as the nonpigmented ciliary epithelium that lines the pars plana. Same sheet, new job, no seam in the tissue itself, only a change of costume. That continuity is not a piece of trivia here. It is the anatomical reason the vitreous base can grip both sides of the ora with a single mechanism, because the surface it holds onto, a basement membrane, the smooth supporting sheet on which such cellular layers rest, runs across the zone without interruption.

Host: Then give me the definition cleanly. If a student had to write one sentence in an examination under the heading vitreous base, what should that sentence say, and which numbers should it carry?

Guide: The vitreous base is a circumferential zone of the vitreous cortex, three to four millimetres wide, centred on the ora serrata, within which the gel is anchored to the underlying retina and ciliary epithelium by collagen fibrils that pass into their basement membranes. Say the numbers with me, because they are the ones that get asked in examinations and quoted in theatre. The zone extends about two millimetres in front of the ora, which places it over the back part of the pars plana, and about two millimetres behind the ora, which places it over the peripheral retina. Three to four millimetres all told. Some texts round the two sides slightly differently, a little more than one and a half here, a little less than two and a half there, but the band of three to four is the standard textbook figure, and it is the one to carry. And the direction of the ribbon matters as much as its width, because it runs in a full circle around the eye, all three hundred and sixty degrees, without gaps and without a preferred quadrant. It is the widest firm attachment the vitreous has. Every other grip the gel owns is an island. This one is a coastline.

Host: A belt rather than a button, then, and I suppose it must be the same all the way around? Or does the width wander, wider here, narrower there? And does the zone hold its size through a lifetime, or does it grow, shrink, or drift as the eye ages?

Guide: It is fairly uniform in width as you travel around, but its back border, where firm anchoring fades into the ordinary weak coupling of cortex to retina, is not a clean drafted line. It is irregular, gently scalloped, a fade rather than a fence. And the zone is not frozen in time either. In the infant the base is at its narrowest, and across the decades the posterior border creeps slowly backward, so the base gains a little ground on the peripheral retina with age. It is one more reason the peripheral retina of a seventy year old is never quite the same organ as the peripheral retina of a child. But here is the headline property, and I want to plant it now. What does not fade with age is the strength of the grip itself. Hold that thought, because it organises everything clinical that follows.

Host: Then let us open the mechanism, because a welded jelly is not an image that explains itself. Last episode gave me a picture of the cortex as condensed collagen at the rim of the gel. What actually changes at the base? How does a transparent gel grip living tissue hard enough to earn the word welded?

Guide: The trick is that here the cortical collagen does not merely lie against the surface. It penetrates it. At the base, bundles of the vitreous collagen, fibrils built around type II collagen, turn out of their wandering course and run roughly at right angles to the surface, plunging into the two anchor sheets. Behind the ora they pass into the internal limiting membrane of the peripheral retina, the thin, smooth basement membrane that forms the innermost boundary of the retina, laid down on its inner surface by its supporting glial cells, the Muller cells. We will meet that membrane in its rightful place among the ten layers in episode seven, Retinal Layers, so I will not pre-walk them now. In front of the ora, the very same fibrils pass into the basement membrane beneath the nonpigmented ciliary epithelium of the pars plana. So the true picture is thousands upon thousands of microscopic anchors, fibrils stitching the gel into two membrane sheets that face each other across the ora. The gel is not glued to the tissue. It is sewn into it.

Host: Sewn into it. And you called this the firmest attachment in the eye, so let me press on the ranking. The gel also hugs the optic disc, and I believe it grips over the macula too. Where does the base actually stand against those, and how do we even speak about stronger and weaker?

Guide: We speak exactly that way, because adhesion here is graded, and the grading is knowledge you will use. Across most of the posterior segment the cortex couples to the retina weakly, so weakly you could almost peel it with a thought. At the optic disc and over the macula the adhesion is firmer, and along the retinal vessels it firms up again in patches. But the vitreous base outranks all of them. It is the strongest attachment of the vitreous to any structure in the eye, full stop. And here is the property that makes it famous. The posterior attachments weaken across the decades, which is a story with its own consequences, but the base does not. Its grip is essentially as firm in the eighth decade of life as in the second. The complete tour of the adhesion sites, disc, macula, vessels and ora compared side by side, belongs to next episode, Vitreous Attachments, so today we stay on our ribbon.

Host: You slipped in a fact there that sounds almost impossible, so let me chase it properly. If everything behind the base loosens with age, what happens to the gel as a whole? I have heard the phrase posterior vitreous detachment used around the clinic. What is that event, in the picture we are building today?

Guide: Back in episode one, Vitreous Body, we established what this gel actually is, about four millilitres of it filling most of the globe, ninety-nine percent water held in a fine framework of collagen and hyaluronic acid. With age that framework slackens. Pockets of liquid open inside the gel, the collapse gathers, and eventually the cortex separates from the retina behind it. That separation is a posterior vitreous detachment, one of the commonest events in the aging eye, and one that awaits essentially everyone. The collapse usually announces itself with new floaters and the occasional flash of light, but the anatomy of where it stops is the part that matters to us today. But watch what the collapse can and cannot do. It can peel the cortex off the disc, off the macula, off the vessels, because those attachments have loosened. It cannot peel the cortex off the vitreous base. A complete posterior vitreous detachment is, by definition, a separation that stops at the base. The gel detaches everywhere behind our ribbon and hangs from it, like a curtain that has torn away from the wall along its whole width except the one edge that was nailed down.

Host: And that is where I start to hear alarm bells. If a curtain tears away from everything except its nailed edge, then every time it moves, all the pulling concentrates at exactly the places that still hold. What does that concentrated pulling do to the retina at the edges of the base?

Guide: You have just described the geometry of most traction events in this part of the eye. When the gel moves, force reaches the retina only where the two are still bound, and the boundary between a stubborn attachment and a surrendered one becomes a stress line. That is why fresh tears after a posterior vitreous detachment cluster around the posterior edge of the base and around the other firm islands. Pulled at right angles, the retina can lift into a flap. Pulled along the surface, it can rip. The base itself, being essentially unbreakable from the gel side, usually wins its contest, but the retina along its borders does not always. I want to keep the finer map of tearing points for episode four, because that is the adhesion story in full. Think of pulling a strip of sticky tape from paper. Where the tape lifts easily, nothing happens. Where it hesitates, the paper tears, and the retina behaves like that paper, with the border of the base as the hesitating line. Today, hold only the principle. Strong bonds concentrate force, and the retina pays the bill at the boundaries.

Host: Then let us take the dramatic version, because the slow collapse of age is one thing, but there is a faster teacher out there. A cricket ball, a squash ball, an elbow at a football match. The eye is suddenly compressed and rebounds in a fraction of a second. What does that violence do to a ribbon this strong, in a young patient whose gel has not liquefied at all?

Guide: Now the base meets its honest rival, which is sudden deformation. In blunt trauma the globe is compressed along one axis and stretched along the axis at right angles, and the peripheral tissues at the base are yanked violently in that instant. Three named injuries belong to this moment. The first is a retinal dialysis, a tear of the peripheral retina right at the ora, where the retina is at its thinnest, classically in the lower outer quadrant after a fist or a ball, and treacherously quiet, because it may not disturb sight until months or even years after the injury, which is exactly why a story of a significant knock to the eye, however old, belongs in the notes of every peripheral examination. The second is a giant tear, a rip of peripheral retina sweeping a quarter of the ring or more, and it is characteristically a young-eye injury, precisely because the young base refuses to separate, so it drags the retina with it instead. The third is avulsion of the vitreous base itself, when a strip of basal gel tears free, carries its ribbon of ciliary epithelium with it, and floats inside the eye as a scalloped, pigmented fringe. That fringe is called a bucket handle, and it is regarded as a fingerprint of previous blunt trauma, sometimes spotted years after the injury everyone forgot.

Host: A fingerprint of old violence, floating in the gel. That raises the practical question for the listener in the consulting room. Can we actually see any of this? Where does the vitreous base sit in the everyday working examination of an optometrist, with the instruments we actually own?

Guide: It sits in the part of the examination that many students approach with dread, the far periphery. The ora and the zone around it are not visible in an ordinary view of the fundus. They are brought into sight by indentation, by gently pressing on the outside of the eye with a small support while you look in, a technique called scleral depression. The indentation pushes that very region into your line of sight, and the ridge you raise and survey is, in effect, the neighbourhood of the vitreous base laid out in front of you. When you depress, you are quite literally pressing on tissue overlying the strongest grip in the eye. Learn to read the picture. The dentate processes show as pale ridges along the indented crest. A pigmented, scalloped band floating above the periphery is that bucket handle from an old injury. A dialysis, by contrast, shows as a deep red edge where the retina has stepped back from its coastline, and it is easiest to miss in the patient you never depress at all. And this same peripheral survey is what you owe any patient with a story of significant blunt trauma, even months later, precisely because dialysis and base avulsion are quiet injuries that keep their secrets.

Host: Now back to the millimetres, because you promised they carried surgical weight. I have heard that eye surgeons enter the back of the eye through the pars plana, through that flat strip in front of the ora. Connect the anatomy to that corridor for me, and tell me where the danger lines come from.

Guide: The connection is one of the loveliest examples of anatomy dictating incisions. If you need instruments inside the vitreous cavity, you want a doorway that lies behind the lens and its zonules, the fine guy ropes that hold the lens in place, and in front of the retina, so that entering the eye cuts neither of them. The pars plana is the only strip in the eye that satisfies both conditions, which is why its four millimetres are precious. So the standard ports of a vitrectomy are placed three and a half to four millimetres behind the corneal edge, safely within pars plana. Now notice what sets the danger lines on both sides of that corridor. Come too far forward and you threaten lens and zonules. Slip back past the pars plana and you are under the posterior part of the vitreous base, where retina lies directly beneath the gel, and every entrance becomes a retinal entry wound. The base and its numbers are the reason the corridor exists exactly where it does.

Host: And once the surgeon is inside through that corridor, I gather the base becomes a problem of a different kind. Detached retinas, membranes, and one stubborn ribbon that refuses to separate from anything. What is the surgical relationship with the base?

Guide: The rule of thumb is simple to say and demanding to perform. If the retina is detached, any gel left in the base is ammunition for the enemy. The base is where the contracting membranes of proliferative vitreoretinopathy, the scar-like sheets that grow on a detached retina, tend to anchor and to shorten, and contraction there drags the peripheral retina back off the wall and can undo an otherwise perfect operation. So in detachment surgery the base is deliberately dissected, an approach surgeons call shaving the base, removing basal gel as completely as the instruments allow. It is the anatomical proof of everything we have said today. Nowhere else does the gel demand that kind of dedicated surgery, because nowhere else does it hold that hard. And the same logic reaches advanced diabetes, where membranes can grow forward onto the base and pull from the front, so once again the base is worked over with special care.

Host: Before we close the anatomy, one more property. You told me the base never lets go with age. Does it ever let go in disease, in inflammation, in anything short of the trauma we described? Or is its grip genuinely for life?

Guide: Almost never on its own, and that near-infallibility works as a diagnostic in reverse. When a posterior vitreous detachment is complete, the base stays intact and attached, and you read the separation as stopping at the ora zone. If the basal gel is absent, or the base is visibly disrupted, you do not blame age. You blame trauma, or a surgeon's instruments, or a pathological process that has eaten through the zone. Severe inflammation and the contractile membranes we discussed can distort and shorten the base, and extreme disease can scar it, but the everyday shorthand stands firm. An intact base is the default state of every aging eye. A violated base is a story with a cause, and the cause is usually injury or the operating theatre.

Host: Let me try to place today in the wider journey, because it strikes me that we have now spent three episodes in a part of the eye that most patients never hear mentioned. Back in Series 1 there was an episode called The Back of the Eye, the wide establishing shot of the fundus. Today we have been living inside a ribbon four millimetres wide. Help the listener feel why anatomy spends its time out here, in the ignored geography.

Guide: Because the statistics invert out here. The centre of the fundus, disc and macula, earns the attention and the referrals, and rightly so. But the peripheral retina, the zone our ribbon dominates, is where the everyday retinal tears announce themselves, where the trauma injuries of young eyes hide, and where the surgical corridor lives. A clinician who has never thought about the vitreous base examines the periphery as a chore to be finished. A clinician who knows the base sees the periphery as the contact patch of the gel, the place where the mechanics of detachment actually happen. Three episodes in, you now own the whole layer cake at this address. The gel and its volume, the cortex as its condensed skin, and the base as its anchor belt. It is the same shift of view we asked of you back in Series 1, when an episode called The Back of the Eye turned the fundus from a red glow into a map, and the base is that map's most important margin. From that belt, almost everything that follows, tears, detachments, membrane surgery, can be reasoned out rather than memorised.

Host: Then give us the sentence to carry out of the room. If a listener remembers exactly one line about the vitreous base when this episode ends, what should that line be?

Guide: Here is the sentence. The vitreous base is a three to four millimetre ribbon of firm anchorage straddling the ora serrata, about two millimetres on the pars plana and two on the peripheral retina, where cortical collagen fibrils are stitched into the basement membranes of the retina and the ciliary epithelium, and it is the strongest attachment in the eye, one that does not loosen with age. The surgeon's doorway lies just in front of it, and the surgeon's hardest hours are spent clearing it. One structure, one sentence, and a career of clinic and theatre decisions hanging on four millimetres.
