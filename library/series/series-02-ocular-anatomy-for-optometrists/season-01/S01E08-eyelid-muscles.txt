# Description: Two thin sheets of muscle stand guard over every eye you will ever examine. In this episode of Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, Focus Cast by focuslinks dissects the eyelid muscles: the orbicularis oculi with its pretarsal, preseptal, orbital and lacrimal parts, and the lid retractors, the levator aponeurosis and the smooth Muller muscle, that hold the eye open for a lifetime of looking. Learn the attachments, the directions of pull, and the anatomical logic behind blinks, lid creases and the classic causes of ptosis. A precision anatomy session for optometry students and practising optometrists.

This is Ocular Anatomy for Optometrists, our second series, and we are deep in Season 1, the Orbit and Ocular Adnexa. Over the last two episodes we built the eyelid like craftsmen. In episode six, Eyelid Layers, we stacked the lid from skin in front to conjunctiva behind, and in episode seven, Tarsal Plates and Meibomian Glands, we fitted the lid with its skeleton. But a skeleton does not move. Today we add the engine. This is episode eight, Eyelid Muscles, the hour we meet the fastest moving muscles a patient owns. To guide us, welcome back our patient teacher. Where shall we begin?

Guide: Thank you, as always. Let me frame the hour so you know its shape. The eyelid is run by two opposing teams. Team one closes the lid, and it is a single muscle, the orbicularis oculi, a purse string of muscle wrapped around the opening between the lids. Team two opens the lid, and it is a system rather than a single muscle: a striated elevator called the levator palpebrae superioris, which ends in a flat, wide tendon sheet called the aponeurosis, helped by a small strip of smooth muscle called the superior tarsal muscle, or Muller muscle. Today is pure anatomy: origins, insertions, directions of pull, and the anatomical why behind blinks, lid creases and droopy lids. The detailed wiring, the nerves and the blood vessels, is episode nine. And the levator has a second life inside the eye movement system, which we deliberately set aside for Season 9, Episode 88. Today it works only as a lid opener.

Host: Before we name a single attachment, help me answer the listener who is thinking, it is a flap of skin, how much anatomy can there be? Why do these muscles deserve a full hour from an optometrist?

Guide: Because they are never off duty and never out of sight. Every patient you seat at the slit lamp opens and closes their lids in front of you, and the lid margin is a landmark you read every single day. Back in Series 1, an episode called The Front of the Eye introduced the cornea and the tear film, and we learned that the cornea, the most exquisitely sensitive surface in the body, has no blood supply of its own and depends entirely on that tear film. The lids are the wipers and the windscreen in one. The muscles inside them spread the tear film evenly in a fraction of a second, protect the cornea from a flying fragment faster than any conscious reaction, and hold the lid margin exactly where it should sit against the eye. When the closer weakens, the cornea dries and scars. When the opener weakens, the lid droops, and in a child the visual system can be starved of light. Anatomy here is not decoration. It is diagnosis.

Host: Then let us meet the closing team first. Walk me up to the orbicularis oculi as if I had never seen it. What kind of muscle is it, and where does it sit?

Guide: The orbicularis oculi is the sphincter of the eyelids. A sphincter is a ring of muscle that closes an opening by tightening around it, like the drawstring of a bag, and this one wraps in flat, concentric loops around the palpebral fissure, the almond shaped opening between the lid margins. It belongs to the family of muscles of facial expression, so its fibres sit in the dermis and the loose tissue just under the skin, not on bone the way a limb muscle does. That is why the lids wrinkle and pleat when it squeezes. It is supplied by the facial nerve, the seventh cranial nerve, and I will simply plant that flag now, because the full motor wiring is episode nine's territory. Functionally, its continuous resting tone is one of the quiet miracles of the body: a gentle, constant squeeze that keeps the lid margins apposed to the globe and stops the lids from falling open under gravity. Every time you look at a patient's quietly closed eyes, you are watching that tone.

Host: You said it wraps in loops, but I suspect it is not one uniform loop. How do anatomists divide it?

Guide: They divide it by geography, and the geography is the function. The outermost ring is the orbital part, which sweeps beyond the lids onto the forehead, the temple and the cheek. The inner rings live inside the lids themselves and together form the palpebral part, and that part is subdivided by one structure we already know from episode six: the orbital septum, the thin fibrous barrier between lid and orbit. The fibres lying in front of the septum are called preseptal, and the fibres lying directly on the tarsal plates, the rigid plates we shaped in episode seven, are called pretarsal. Hidden behind the nose of the orbit there is also a fourth, secret piece, the lacrimal part, which most students never see in a dissection but every patient uses at every blink. Four territories, one muscle, four different jobs. Let us take them in the order you will use them clinically: palpebral first, then orbital, then the lacrimal piece.

Host: Start with the palpebral part then. What exactly do the pretarsal and preseptal fibres do?

Guide: They are the everyday blink. The pretarsal fibres, the ones lying directly on the tarsal plates, pull the lid margins together and press them against the globe, so they are the fibres that actually close the door. Medially, they take origin from the medial palpebral ligament, the tough band anchoring the tarsal plates to the medial orbital wall, and laterally they weave into the lateral palpebral raphe, a fibrous seam where the fibres of the two lids meet over the lateral rim. The preseptal fibres, sitting on the orbital septum, contract with them and draw the lids slightly toward the nose as they close. A spontaneous blink uses almost exclusively this palpebral part, and its numbers are worth saying aloud. We blink about fifteen times a minute while awake, something like fifteen thousand blinks in a waking day, and a single blink lasts around a quarter of a second. In that quarter of a second the lid sweeps the tear film into a fresh, even layer. No other muscle in the body repeats one movement so many times, which is why its anatomy deserves respect.

Host: Fifteen thousand times a day puts a different weight on every attachment you just named. Now the outer ring. What does the orbital part add?

Guide: Power. The orbital part is the wide outer loop that reaches up onto the forehead, out to the temple and down onto the cheek, and it is recruited only for forceful, voluntary or protective closure. Its fibres arise from the nasal part of the frontal bone above, from the frontal process of the maxilla below, and from the medial palpebral ligament in between, and then sweep in a big ellipse around the orbit. When it fires, you screw the eye shut: the lids close, the skin bunches into folds radiating from the outer corner, and the whole ring pulls toward the medial corner like a purse string being yanked. Those radiating folds at the outer corner are the lines patients call crow's feet, and they are literally the imprint of this muscle on the skin. Here is a clinical pearl that falls straight out of the anatomy. When you ask a patient to squeeze the eyes shut, you are testing orbital fibres, but the gentle, quiet blink that wets the eye uses only palpebral fibres. That is why a patient can look perfectly normal while chatting and still be unable to screw the eye shut after certain facial injuries. Two territories, two tests.

Host: And the secret fourth piece, the lacrimal part. I like that you called it secret. Where is it hiding?

Guide: Behind the lacrimal sac, on the wall of the nose of the orbit. Some books call it Horner's muscle. Its fibres arise from the posterior lacrimal crest, the little ridge of bone behind the lacrimal sac on the lacrimal bone, and they pass backward around the sac to reach the tarsal plates. This position is the whole point. When the palpebral fibres fire a blink, the lacrimal fibres fire with them, and as the lids close, the muscle shortens over the sac. That flick of contraction expands the sac for an instant, and then, as the fibres relax and the lids open, the sac refills, drawing the fresh tear lake in from the lid margin and pushing older tears down the nasolacrimal duct toward the nose. Anatomists call this the lacrimal pump. If the pump is lazy, tears spill over the lid instead of draining, and the patient complains of watering eyes even though the tear film is perfectly normal. So the orbicularis oculi is not only a closer. It is also the engine of tear drainage.

Host: Let me flip the muscle off entirely. A patient with a facial nerve palsy on one side. What does the anatomy predict, and how bad is that for the eye?

Guide: The anatomy predicts a limp sphincter, and the consequences follow beautifully from the parts. Without palpebral tone the lower lid sags away from the globe, the blink disappears, and the eye cannot be fully closed even in sleep. That inability to close is called lagophthalmos, from the Greek for hare eye, because hares were thought to sleep with their eyes open. The exposed strip of cornea and conjunctiva dries every night, and the resulting surface damage, exposure keratopathy, is one of the most important reasons an optometrist sees urgency in a facial palsy. Add the lost lacrimal pump and the eye floods with reflex tears it cannot drain, which looks paradoxical, a wet eye drying out. One more protective detail belongs here, because it is pure design. When the lids close, the eyes swing upward and outward, tucking the most precious surface, the cornea, up beneath the sheltering upper lid. This reflex is called Bell's phenomenon, and in a facial palsy you see it clearly: ask the patient to attempt closure, lift the lid, and the globe has already looked skyward. The muscle is gone, but the brain's exit plan remains.

Host: That image will stick with me. All right, closing team complete. Now the opening team. Who lifts the lid, and why does it take a system rather than one muscle?

Guide: Because closing is easy and opening is active. The orbicularis closes against almost nothing, but holding a lid open all day against gravity, and lifting it through fifteen millimetres of travel from downgaze to upgaze, needs power on tap. The system has three players. The prime mover is the levator palpebrae superioris, a striated muscle you can command, which delivers its pull through the levator aponeurosis. Its silent partner is the superior tarsal muscle, the Muller muscle, a strip of smooth muscle you cannot command, which supplies a constant background lift of about two millimetres. And the accessory player stands outside the lid altogether: the frontalis, the forehead muscle, which hoists the brow and with it the lid, and which every ptosis patient recruits without knowing. Let us take the prime mover first, from its origin at the back of the orbit to its insertion at the lid margin, because that journey explains everything about lid position and lid crease.

Host: Origin then. Where does the levator palpebrae superioris begin, and what route does it take through the orbit?

Guide: It begins at the apex of the orbit, on the underside of the lesser wing of the sphenoid bone, just above and in front of the optic canal. You remember that doorway; episode four, Orbital Openings and Communications, introduced it and everything that passes through it. The levator's origin sits on the roofward side of that region, just above the common tendinous ring from which the rectus muscles arise. From there the muscle runs forward, almost horizontally, through the anterior orbit, lying immediately above the superior rectus muscle, and the two share fascial connections so that gaze and lid position stay coupled. The belly is about forty millimetres long, and near the front of the orbit something surprising happens. Instead of inserting as a compact tendon the way a limb muscle does, it spreads into a wide, thin sheet, the levator aponeurosis, which keeps travelling downward to the lid. Between muscle and aponeurosis, the whole apparatus threads through a sling of connective tissue called Whitnall's ligament, and that sling is the key to the whole mechanism.

Host: A sling. Tell me about Whitnall's ligament, because I have heard the name but I could not say what it does.

Guide: Picture a pulley. Whitnall's ligament, also called the superior transverse ligament, is a condensation of the levator's own fascia stretched across the front of the orbit, attached medially near the trochlea, the little pulley of the superior oblique muscle, and laterally to a small bump on the inner face of the lateral orbital rim called Whitnall's tubercle. The levator passes through it at the junction of its muscular and aponeurotic portions. Behind the sling the muscle pulls forward; in front of it the aponeurosis must pull downward onto the lid. So Whitnall's ligament converts a horizontal force into a vertical one, exactly like a curtain ring turning the pull on a curtain cord. It is also a suspension bridge for the upper orbit: the lacrimal gland, sitting in its fossa up there, drapes over the sling and is divided by it into a larger orbital lobe behind and a smaller palpebral lobe in front. One structure, three jobs: fulcrum for the elevator, hanger for the gland, and landmark for the surgeon searching for the aponeurosis.

Host: So the aponeurosis comes off the sling heading down. Where does this sheet actually anchor, and what does each anchoring do?

Guide: It fans out like a sail, and every corner of the sail matters. The main body descends about fifteen millimetres, and its lower fibres insert onto the anterior surface of the tarsal plate, concentrated over the lower third of the tarsus, a few millimetres above the lid margin. That is the anchor that lifts the whole plate and with it the lid margin. Second, fine slips pass forward through the orbicularis muscle and attach to the dermis of the lid skin about eight to ten millimetres above the margin, and that attachment is what pulls the skin inward when the lid opens, creating the upper lid crease. Third, the sheet sprouts two horns: the medial horn runs back to the region of the medial palpebral ligament, and the lateral horn sweeps through the substance of the lacrimal gland, helping divide it into those two lobes, before anchoring at the lateral tubercle. And remember from episode six that between the aponeurosis behind and the orbital septum in front lies the preaponeurotic fat pad, the yellow fat that pads the upper lid. Septum in front, fat in the middle, aponeurosis behind: that is the stack you will one day see in a lid dissection.

Host: You slipped in the crease there, eight to ten millimetres, and I suspect that little number explains a great deal of clinic. Why does the crease matter so much?

Guide: Because the crease is the aponeurosis made visible. In a young, healthy lid, the levator pulls the tarsus up and simultaneously dents the skin at its dermal slips, so crease and lid margin move together in lock step. Now let the years pass. The aponeurosis is the one component that degenerates with age: it thins, stretches, and can slip off its tarsal insertion entirely. When that happens the margin drops, because the anchor is gone, while the crease rides higher, because the slack sheet now folds at a higher level, and the preaponeurotic fat slides forward and pouts under the skin. That triad, a droopy margin, a high or doubled crease, and a fullness above it, is the classic picture of involutional, aponeurotic ptosis, one of the commonest lid malpositions an optometrist meets. The measurements you will use come straight from this anatomy. A normal palpebral fissure between the margins measures about ten millimetres. The margin reflex distance, from the lid margin to the corneal light reflex, sits around four millimetres. And levator function, the excursion of the margin from extreme downgaze to extreme upgaze, is normally fifteen millimetres or more. Low function points to a muscle problem; normal function with a droop points to the aponeurosis.

Host: Before we leave the prime mover, there is the quiet partner, the Muller muscle. Smooth muscle in the eyelid still surprises people. Take it apart for us.

Guide: The superior tarsal muscle is a strip of smooth muscle, involuntary, unstriated, and under the control of the sympathetic nervous system, not the facial or oculomotor nerves you might guess. It takes origin from the undersurface of the levator close to the junction where muscle becomes aponeurosis, and it descends behind the aponeurosis for about ten millimetres to insert on the upper border of the tarsal plate. Its job is background lift: while the levator does the commanded lifting, the Muller muscle adds a steady two millimetres or so of elevation, keeping the lid wide when you are alert, interested or startled. You have felt this muscle work. When adrenaline floods the system and the pupils dilate, the lid fissure widens with them, the startle stare, and that is the Muller muscle firing on sympathetic drive. The anatomy also gives you the signature of its failure. In Horner syndrome, where the sympathetic supply to the head is interrupted, the ptosis is mild, one to two millimetres, exactly the size of the Muller contribution, and it comes with a small pupil, miosis, because the same sympathetic pathway serves the pupillary dilator. A two millimetre droop with miosis points straight at a smooth muscle short of its fuel.

Host: One to two millimetres sounds tiny, but you said the fissure is only ten. Proportionally that is a fifth of the doorway. Does the lower lid have any of this machinery too?

Guide: It has, in a smaller, quieter form, and knowing the analogy saves you confusion forever. The lower lid's answer to the levator aponeurosis is the capsulopalpebral fascia, a fibrous sheet that arises indirectly from the sheath of the inferior rectus muscle and rises to the lower tarsus. Its smooth muscle partner is the inferior tarsal muscle, the Muller muscle of the lower lid. You see the coupling at work every day: when a patient looks down, the lower lid drops a few millimetres with the gaze, because gaze and lower lid share that fascial link, the same coupling we noted between levator and superior rectus. But the symmetry ends there. The lower lid has no powerful elevator of its own; it is opened passively, pushed up by the globe and held between the canthal tendons, and its real muscular work is done on the way down. So when the lower lid malpositions, sagging out or rolling in, think of stretched canthal anchors and lax retractors rather than a failing levator, and keep the deep dive for episode ten, which is the clinical hour of this lid run.

Host: Let us put the two teams in the same room now. A blink happens in a quarter of a second. How do closer and openers coordinate, and who is in charge?

Guide: The brain choreographs them as mutual opposites, and the anatomy makes the choreography economical. At the start of a blink, the levator's drive shuts off instantly, and the pretarsal and preseptal orbicularis fibres fire together, sweeping the margins down and medially. Bell's phenomenon takes the globe up and out, the lacrimal part squeezes the pump, and the tear film is renewed. To open, the sequence simply reverses: the orbicularis relaxes, the levator fires, its aponeurosis glides through Whitnall's sling, and the tarsus rises until the Muller muscle's two millimetres hold the lid wide at the top. When the load increases, the frontalis joins in from outside, raising the brow, and you can see it in every heavy ptosis patient, the furrowed forehead doing the work the retractor has lost. And if the demand is protective, a fly at the cornea, a hand at the face, the orbital part fires in one massive loop and slams the door. One player is still missing from this picture: the wiring diagram itself, which nerves command each team and through which branches. That is precisely episode nine, so I will stop at the door of that room.

Host: That choreography explains a lot of what we see at the slit lamp. Now give me the failure modes of the opening side, framed the way you framed the closing side, from the anatomy outward.

Guide: Sort them by the part that fails. Failure of the aponeurosis we met in the crease discussion: the common, elderly, usually bilateral, high crease droop. Failure of the muscle itself, myogenic ptosis, gives a weak levator function reading, fifteen millimetres falling to five or less, and the congenital form, where the muscle is partly replaced by fibrous tissue from before birth, shows a lid that barely climbs and, tellingly, does not drop properly in downgaze, the classic lid lag of a stiff muscle. Failure of the wiring, neurogenic ptosis, splits by nerve: interrupt the sympathetic path and you get the gentle one to two millimetre Horner droop; interrupt the third nerve, the levator's own supply, and the lid falls across the whole fissure, which is one of the true emergency presentations in eye care. Failure by load, mechanical ptosis, means the muscle is fine but the lid is weighted down, by swelling, by a mass, by redundant tissue. And the opposite pole is possible too: a sphincter in spasm. Blepharospasm is involuntary, forceful contraction of the orbital and palpebral parts, functionally blinding for seconds at a time even though the eye itself is healthy, and it reminds you that closing too well can be as disabling as opening too little.

Host: One more everyday thing patients mention, that fluttering eyelid that drives people to search the internet at midnight. Is that in our muscle at all?

Guide: It is, and it has a name, myokymia. It is a fine, rippling fasciculation of the orbicularis fibres, usually of the lower lid, a tiny involuntary twitch of muscle bundles brought on by fatigue, stress and caffeine; it is benign and it fades as lifestyle eases. I mention it because it is the perfect little demonstration that the orbicularis is voluntary muscle living in the skin: you can both feel and see it twitching under the surface, and it obeys the general rules of muscle health. Distinguish it from blepharospasm by its subtlety: the lid margin does not move, the vision is untouched, and the patient is more bothered than harmed. Most of what patients call eye twitching is this, and a calm sentence of explanation from you is the whole treatment.

Host: I can feel the hour closing, fittingly. Before we sign off, would you compress this anatomy into one breath, the closer and the opener, so it can settle?

Guide: Gladly. The closer is one sphincter, the orbicularis oculi, facial nerve territory, with four territories of its own: pretarsal fibres pressing the margins shut on the tarsal plates, preseptal fibres drawing the lids together over the septum, orbital fibres screwing the eye shut in protection, and the hidden lacrimal part pumping tears behind the sac. The opener is a system: the levator palpebrae superioris from the lesser wing of the sphenoid, threading through Whitnall's sling, its aponeurosis anchored to the lower third of the tarsus and to the skin to make the crease, backed by the sympathetic Muller muscle's two millimetre lift, and assisted from above by the frontalis. Closer and opener meet at a lid margin that should rest about ten millimetres apart, and each failure mode of each fibre writes its own signature in the position of that margin. That is the anatomy. Everything else, the vessels, the nerves, the clinic, belongs to the next two episodes.

Host: You mentioned earlier that the levator has a second life, and I want to make sure listeners know we are not teasing carelessly. Where does that story go?

Guide: It goes to the movement season, deliberately. In Season 9, Episode 88, the episode called Levator Palpebrae, we return to this same muscle from the other side, as a member of the extraocular family: its partnership with the superior rectus, the way lid position tracks gaze, and the synergy that keeps the lid rising with the eye. Today we treated the levator strictly as a lid opener, an actuator in the lid's machinery. In that episode we will treat it strictly as part of the movement system, and the two portraits together will finally look like one muscle. Anatomy, like the lid itself, opens in layers.
