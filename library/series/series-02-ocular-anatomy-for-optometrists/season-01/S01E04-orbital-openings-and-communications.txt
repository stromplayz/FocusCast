# Description: Every door into the orbit has a name, a size, and a passenger list. In this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks, we walk to the orbital apex and map the optic canal, the superior and inferior orbital fissures, and the smaller foramina that carry nerves and vessels in and out of the socket. Learn exactly what passes through each opening, how the annulus of Zinn organizes the traffic, and how the orbit talks to the middle cranial fossa, the pterygopalatine fossa, the nasal cavity, and the face. Essential anatomy listening for optometry students, clinicians, and anyone who wants the orbit to finally make sense.

Host: Last time, in episode three, Orbital Walls, we walked around the orbit panel by panel, roof, medial wall, floor, and lateral wall. The time before that, in episode two, Orbital Bones and Their Clinical Importance, we met the seven bones that build this small room of bone. But a room with walls and no doors is a vault, and the orbit is not a vault. It is one of the busiest little workshops in the body, with nerves, vessels, and muscle anchors pouring in and out of it every second of your life. So today we go looking for the doors. Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to episode four of our opening season, called Orbital Openings and Communications. As always, I am joined by our patient guide through ocular anatomy. Before we open a single door, set the map in front of us. What is on today's walk?

Guide: Thank you, and welcome back to every listener. Here is our route. We start at the apex, the pointed back corner of the orbit, because that is where the big doorways cluster. Then we visit them one at a time. First the optic canal, the private tunnel of the optic nerve and its companion artery. Next the superior orbital fissure, the grand gateway at the apex, and we will take its passenger list slowly, because it is long. Then the inferior orbital fissure, which leads the orbit downward and backward toward the spaces behind the cheek. After the three great openings we pick up the smaller foramina, the humble holes on the rim and walls that students often skip and clinicians regret skipping. And we finish with the reward question. If these are the doors, where does each door actually lead? That is the communications half of our title.

Host: Before we get technical, let me ask the fair student question. Could we not have folded the openings into the walls episode? Why do the holes deserve a whole hour of their own?

Guide: Because the openings are where anatomy starts doing work. A wall tells you what the orbit is. An opening tells you what travels where, and that is the information you will actually use. When a patient sits in front of you with a drooping lid, a numb forehead, a paralyzed eye, a numb cheek, or a red eye with corkscrew vessels, the lesion is sitting in one of these doorways, and the pattern of findings is the address. The openings are also the highways for disease. Infection from the nose, tumors from the sinuses, blood from an injured artery, all of them travel by these routes. So the holes are not leftovers between the bones. They are the functional border of the orbit, and they repay study many times over.

Host: Then let us set the scene properly. Where exactly is this doorway cluster we keep calling the apex?

Guide: Picture the orbit as the four-sided pyramid we drew back in episode one, with its base at the rim in front and its point pushed far back and slightly inward, toward the nose. That point is the apex, and it sits roughly forty millimetres behind the rim, in the posterior third of the orbit. Almost everything here is made by one bone, the sphenoid, the butterfly-shaped bone wedged across the base of the skull. The lesser wings of the sphenoid form the upper, medial part of the apex and carry the optic canal. The greater wings form much of the lateral apex and help bound the superior orbital fissure. And one small landmark keeps them apart. A ridge of the lesser wing called the optic strut separates the optic canal from the superior orbital fissure. So the two biggest doorways are close neighbors with a wall between them, and each has its own strictly limited guest list.

Host: Good, because I was about to ask how anyone keeps those two openings apart in their head. Now, the most famous doorway first. Describe the optic canal.

Guide: The optic canal is a tunnel through the lesser wing of the sphenoid, running from the middle cranial fossa, the floor of the brain cavity, forward, laterally, and slightly downward to open at the orbital apex. It is a true canal, a covered tunnel, about nine millimetres long, and its orbital opening is a small oval roughly six and a half millimetres tall and five millimetres wide. Two flanges of bone, the upper and lower roots of the lesser wing, wrap around it, which is why the canal is so complete and so narrow. Because it is narrow and ringed in bone, whatever passes through it cannot dodge. Hold on to that sentence, because it becomes the whole clinical story of this doorway. It is the most tightly packaged opening in the orbit, and the most precious cargo in the orbit travels through it. Nothing about the optic canal is roomy, and that is precisely why it appears in examination rooms.

Host: Who exactly is in the tunnel? Give me the full passenger list.

Guide: Two travelers, and only two, which is worth savoring. First comes the optic nerve, and not just the naked cable. Here the nerve wears all three of its coverings, the meninges, meaning the tough outer dura, the gauzy arachnoid, and the innermost pia, because the dura of the nerve is continuous with the lining of the brain and becomes fused with the periorbita, the tough membrane that clothes the inside of the bony orbit. In plain words, at this doorway the nerve is glued to the hole it passes through. The second traveler is the ophthalmic artery, the first major branch of the internal carotid artery as that vessel emerges from the cavernous sinus. The artery runs below and to the outer side of the nerve inside the canal, then usually hooks over the top of the nerve as it enters the orbit. A fine sprinkling of sympathetic fibers, the hair-thin fibers of the carotid plexus, ride along with the artery. That is the entire list. One nerve, one artery, and a few autonomic hitchhikers.

Host: We met the optic nerve's working life back in Series 1, in an episode called The Optic Nerve and Visual Information, where we followed the image from the retina toward the brain. But the cable itself, and this particular stretch of it, feels like a different story. Why does the tunnel segment matter so much clinically?

Guide: Because it is the trapped segment. Everywhere else along its twenty-five millimetre orbital course, the optic nerve has slack, a little winding room, space to be displaced by swelling or movement. In the canal it is cemented down, dura fused to bone, sharing a passage about nine millimetres long with an artery. So when trouble comes here, the nerve cannot move away from it. A sharp blow to the brow or temple can shear the delicate nerve fibers inside this rigid tunnel even when no fracture shows on a scan, which is the anatomy behind traumatic optic neuropathy. Tumors can wrap around the sheath here and choke the nerve slowly. And remember the neighbor. The sphenoid sinus lies immediately below and medial to the canal, sometimes separated from it by paper-thin bone, so disease in that sinus can reach the nerve, and sinus surgeons can navigate to this canal through the nose. When a compressed nerve is surgically decompressed, the surgeon is thinning the roof of the sphenoid sinus. One little tunnel, and three specialties care about it.

Host: Give me the numbers for the whole nerve while we are here. Students always want the lengths.

Guide: Happily, and they are worth memorizing because they tell a story. The optic nerve runs about twenty-five millimetres inside the orbit, about nine millimetres through the canal, and about ten millimetres inside the skull before the two nerves join hands at the optic chiasm. Add it up and you get roughly forty-five millimetres of cable for a journey that is considerably shorter in a straight line. That surplus is not waste. It is safety slack, a gentle redundancy of length that lets the eye move, and even lurch forward in swelling, without the cable going tight. It is also why the canal segment, the one stretch with no slack at all, is the first part of the nerve to suffer when the orbit is suddenly crowded. The slack is a savings account, and the canal is where the account holds nothing.

Host: And now for the bigger neighbor. The superior orbital fissure. First, vocabulary. We have said canal and foramen and now fissure. Are those three genuinely different things?

Guide: They are, and the difference is simple enough to carry forever. A foramen is a hole in a plate of bone. A canal is a tunnel, a hole with a roof, a floor, and real length. A fissure is a cleft, a gap between two bones that never quite closed, a slit rather than a hole. The superior orbital fissure is a slit between the greater and lesser wings of the sphenoid, lying just below and to the outer side of the optic canal at the apex. It is the largest communication between the orbit and the middle cranial fossa, a comma-shaped cleft roughly twenty-two millimetres long, widening toward its outer end. Through it, the orbit talks directly to the depth of the skull, where the cavernous sinus, that flat web of thin-walled veins flanking the pituitary gland, sits waiting with the internal carotid artery running through it. Keep the word valveless in your pocket for later, because those veins will close our episode.

Host: And its passenger list is the famous long one. Take it slowly for us, and tell us how anyone organizes so many structures inside one slit.

Guide: The organizing trick is a ring. Bridging the upper, narrower part of the fissure, and crossing the optic canal too, lies the annulus of Zinn, also called the common tendinous ring, a hoop of fibrous tissue that anchors the origins of the four rectus muscles. Think of the ring as a customs gate drawn across part of the fissure, because everything that must reach the eyeball's deep muscle cone passes inside it. Through this ringed portion run the superior and inferior divisions of the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve, which drives most of the eye's movers; the abducens nerve, the sixth cranial nerve, heading for the muscle that pulls the eye outward; the nasociliary nerve, the deep sensory branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve; and the sympathetic root of the ciliary ganglion. Outside the ring, above it, slip through the smaller travelers: the trochlear nerve, the fourth cranial nerve, bound for the muscle that works through a pulley; the frontal nerve, the large sensory branch to forehead and upper lid; the lacrimal nerve, sensory to the outer corner of the lid; and the superior ophthalmic vein, the main venous drain of the orbit. Below the ring, the inferior ophthalmic vein finds its own way through. One slit, one ring, three zones.

Host: Students love an ordered list for this one. Is there a way to walk the fissure from side to side and catch every nerve in sequence?

Guide: There is, and teachers have recited it for generations. Walk the fissure from lateral to medial, from the temple side toward the nose, and above the ring you meet, in order, the lacrimal nerve, the frontal nerve, and the trochlear nerve. Students remember that trio with the phrase lazy French tarts. Then at the ring the order continues: the superior ophthalmic vein, the superior division of the third nerve, the nasociliary nerve, the inferior division of the third nerve, the abducens nerve, and finally the sympathetic fibers. A popular sentence for that sequence begins with the words sit naked in anticipation, and I will leave the rest to your anatomy course. Say it a few times and you own the apex. More importantly, do not let the list float free of meaning. Each name has a job. The two divisions of the third nerve carry the lifeline to most eye movers and to the lid lifter. The sixth nerve reaches the outward mover. The fourth reaches the pulley muscle. The frontal and lacrimal nerves carry feeling from the forehead, the lid, and the outer corner. The nasociliary carries feeling from the eye itself. And the veins carry blood away to the cavernous sinus.

Host: While we are at the apex, there is a tiny structure hiding nearby that you mentioned indirectly, the ciliary ganglion. Where does it fit into this doorway geography?

Guide: It is the apex's little relay station, and it earns its place because all three of its roots arrive through these doorways. The ciliary ganglion is a pinhead-sized collection of nerve cells, about two millimetres across, sitting between the optic nerve and the muscle of the lateral wall, a little in front of the apex. Three fine threads enter it. The parasympathetic root arrives from the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve, carrying fibers destined to constrict the pupil and to focus the lens for near work. The sympathetic root arrives by way of the carotid plexus, and those fibers pass straight through the ganglion without stopping, headed for the dilator of the pupil. And the sensory root comes in from the nasociliary nerve, carrying feeling from the eyeball itself. Out of the ganglion run the short ciliary nerves, six to ten fine threads that fan forward around the optic nerve to the back of the eye. So the fissure is not merely a motor and sensory gateway. It is also the doorway through which the eye's autonomic wiring reaches its final relay before the globe.

Host: Now the clinical question that hangs over a doorway this crowded. What does a patient look like when something goes wrong in the superior orbital fissure?

Guide: The pattern is unforgettable once you connect it to the map. If disease blocks the fissure, everything that ran through it fails. The eye cannot move in any direction, a state called ophthalmoplegia, because the third, fourth, and sixth nerves are all down. The lid droops, because fibers of the third nerve lift it. The forehead and the cornea lose sensation, because the first division of the trigeminal nerve is interrupted. The eye may sit slightly forward because venous blood backs up behind the blockage. That bundle of findings is called superior orbital fissure syndrome. Now slide one centimetre inward, catch the optic canal as well, and vision itself collapses too, and that fuller picture is called orbital apex syndrome. The difference between the two is a single doorway, and the difference in the examination room is sight itself. One more name for your shelf: Tolosa-Hunt syndrome, a painful ophthalmoplegia caused by inflammation at the cavernous sinus and the fissure, famous because steroid treatment can switch it off. The openings are tiny. The syndromes that live in them are loud.

Host: Down from the apex now, along the line where the lateral wall meets the floor. Tell us about the inferior orbital fissure.

Guide: The inferior orbital fissure is a long, slanting cleft, about twenty millimetres, running forward and outward between the greater wing of the sphenoid behind and the maxilla and palatine bones in front. Where the superior fissure is a gateway into the skull cavity, the inferior fissure is a gateway to the spaces below and behind the cheek. At its back end it opens into the pterygopalatine fossa, a small crossroads space tucked behind the back of the maxilla, where the maxillary nerve and the pterygopalatine ganglion wait like stationmasters. Toward its outer end it leads toward the infratemporal fossa, the space behind and below the cheekbone where the chewing muscles live. And here is a detail students rarely expect. The lower part of this fissure is not left gaping. It is bridged by the periorbita and by a small sheet of smooth muscle called the orbitalis, fed by sympathetic fibers. Classical teaching holds that when that sympathetic supply fails, as in Horner syndrome, the eye sinks back just a little, a slight enophthalmos. A door with a muscle shutter, and even the shutter has a nerve.

Host: What actually passes through the inferior orbital fissure?

Guide: The maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve dominates, or rather its branches do. The infraorbital nerve, the great sensory nerve of the midface, passes through the fissure at its back end and immediately slips into the groove that will carry it along the floor, and with it travel the infraorbital vessels. The zygomatic nerve, a smaller branch of the same division, enters through the fissure and hugs the lateral wall. Then come the quieter passengers. Fine branches of the pterygopalatine ganglion cross the fissure carrying parasympathetic fibers, some of them destined for the tear gland. And one or two communicating veins link the inferior ophthalmic vein to the pterygoid plexus, the venous net spread over the muscles below the skull base. So the inferior fissure is sensory, autonomic, and venous at once, and each job explains a different sign someday, from a numb cheek to a backed-up orbit.

Host: You said the zygomatic nerve carries something destined for the tear gland. That sounds like a detour worth telling properly. Walk us through it.

Guide: It is one of the loveliest detours in head and neck anatomy. The zygomatic nerve enters the orbit through the inferior fissure, turns outward along the lateral wall, and enters a small hole in the zygomatic bone. There it splits into two skin nerves, the zygomaticofacial nerve to the prominence of the cheek, and the zygomaticotemporal nerve toward the temple. But the zygomaticotemporal branch is carrying secret passengers. Riding with it are parasympathetic fibers that left the pterygopalatine ganglion, crossed the inferior orbital fissure, and are now climbing toward the tear gland. In the lateral wall that branch sends a tiny communicating fiber up to the lacrimal nerve, and the passengers transfer trains one last time to reach the gland that waters the eye. So when you someday hear the tear gland's full story later in this series, remember that part of its secretomotor wiring runs beneath the lateral wall inside a humble skin nerve. The gland itself we will save for its own season. The wiring, you now know, passes through this fissure.

Host: And the infraorbital nerve, the one that slips into the groove. Follow it for us all the way out.

Guide: Its course is the biography of the floor. The infraorbital groove begins at the back of the floor, at the inferior orbital fissure, runs forward in the maxilla, and then the groove's roof closes over it, turning it into the infraorbital canal, a tunnel set in the roof of the maxillary sinus. Near the rim it turns upward and opens on the face as the infraorbital foramen, roughly a centimetre below the rim, in line with the pupil. Out of it come the nerve and vessels for the skin of the lower lid, the cheek, the side of the nose, and the upper lip, and along the way the nerve drops branches to the upper teeth. Two practical notes. First, that foramen is a landmark every examiner knows, the spot for numbing the midface. Second, recall that the orbital floor is thin and easily fractured, as episode three emphasized. When a blunt object strikes the eye and the floor buckles into that roof of the sinus, the infraorbital canal rides in the collapsing ceiling, and the classic complaint afterward is a numb cheek and lip. One tunnel, one very common clinical sign.

Host: Now the smaller openings. Which ones matter most, on the rim and along the walls?

Guide: Start on the rim. At the upper border, a finger can feel the supraorbital notch, sometimes a complete foramen, at the junction of the inner and middle thirds. Through it pass the supraorbital nerve, the terminal branch of the frontal nerve we met above the ring, and the supraorbital vessels, climbing to the forehead and scalp. Then move onto the medial wall, to the seam between the ethmoid and frontal bones, and you find two small holes in a line. The anterior ethmoidal foramen lies about twenty-four millimetres behind the rim, and the posterior ethmoidal foramen about thirty-six millimetres behind it. Through the first pass the anterior ethmoidal nerve and vessels, running out of the orbit into the skull cavity and then down into the nose. Through the second, the posterior ethmoidal nerve and vessels. And here is the pearl that makes these worth knowing. The orbital opening of the optic canal sits about forty-two millimetres behind the rim. Twenty-four, thirty-six, forty-two: sinus surgeons chant those three numbers, because the posterior ethmoidal foramen is their warning marker that they are nearing the optic nerve. The same holes are also an infection route, since the ethmoid air cells lie just on the far side of the paper-thin lamina papyracea we honored in episode three, and ethmoiditis in children is a classic road by which infection marches into the orbit.

Host: One more opening before we do the grand tour. The one the tears use.

Guide: Yes, and we will touch it gently and move on, because it owns a whole season of this series later. At the front of the medial wall, in the shallow lacrimal fossa, the nasolacrimal canal begins. It is a downward tunnel, roughly a centimetre and a half long, formed by three bones cooperating: the maxilla, the lacrimal bone, and the inferior nasal concha. It opens into the nose beneath the inferior turbinate, at the spot called the inferior meatus. Functionally it is the orbit's plumbing exit, the route by which tears leave the eye for the nose, which is why crying makes the nose run. The full architecture of that drainage system, the puncta, the sac, and the duct, waits for the lacrimal season, around episodes sixteen to twenty of this series. Today it simply counts as one more communication, orbit to nasal cavity.

Host: Then let us do the thing the title promises. Assemble the whole communication map for me. If the orbit is a room, which rooms does it talk to, and through which doors?

Guide: Five neighbors, five directions, one map. First, backward through the optic canal and the superior orbital fissure, the orbit talks to the middle cranial fossa, and through the fissure its veins drain onward to the cavernous sinus. Second, downward and backward through the inferior orbital fissure, it talks to the pterygopalatine fossa, and by that little crossroads onward to the infratemporal fossa and the deep spaces of the face. Third, inward through the ethmoidal foramina, it talks to the ethmoid air cells and the nasal cavity, and through those canals to the anterior skull base as well. Fourth, downward and inward through the nasolacrimal canal, it talks directly to the nose. Fifth, forward through the supraorbital notch, the infraorbital canal, and the zygomatic foramina, it talks to the skin of the face. And the veins deserve their own sentence. The ophthalmic veins are valveless, so blood, and with it infection or air, can travel in either direction, from the face into the orbit and onward to the cavernous sinus, or from deep disease back toward the surface. The orbit is a room with five doors, and none of the doors have locks.

Host: Those valveless veins. Say more about why they matter at the chair, because I can tell you have been saving it.

Guide: Because they turn a distant disease into a visible sign. Follow the geography. The angular vein at the inner corner of the eye connects with the ophthalmic veins, and the ophthalmic veins drain into the cavernous sinus beside the brain. No valves anywhere on that road. So an infected pimple on the nose or upper lip, in the so-called danger area of the face, can send bacteria backward into the orbit and the cavernous sinus, which is why those little infections are treated with real respect. In the other direction, if the wall of the carotid artery weakens and the artery ruptures into the cavernous sinus, a carotid-cavernous fistula, arterial blood floods into the ophthalmic veins. The eye bulges, the episcleral veins on the eye's surface twist into corkscrews, the pressure inside the eye can rise, and sometimes an examiner hears a bruit, a soft whooshing sound, over the closed lid. None of that is managed at the chair, but every piece of it is recognized there, and recognition begins with knowing which doors connect where.

Host: Let us lock it in. Take me back through the doors one more time, quickly, before you go.

Guide: Quickly, then. The optic canal, through the lesser wing of the sphenoid, about nine millimetres of tunnel: the optic nerve in all three sheaths and the ophthalmic artery, and nothing else. The superior orbital fissure, the twenty-two millimetre cleft between the greater and lesser wings: the third, fourth, and sixth nerves, the frontal, lacrimal, and nasociliary nerves, the sympathetic roots, and the ophthalmic veins, all sorted into zones by the annulus of Zinn. The inferior orbital fissure, about twenty millimetres slanting toward the pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae: the infraorbital and zygomatic nerves, branches of the pterygopalatine ganglion, and communicating veins, bridged below by the little orbitalis muscle. Then the small doors: the supraorbital notch, the anterior and posterior ethmoidal foramina at twenty-four and thirty-six millimetres behind the rim, the infraorbital groove and canal with its foramen a centimetre below the rim, the zygomatic foramen, and the nasolacrimal canal. Five communications, five neighbors: the middle cranial fossa, the pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae, the ethmoids and the nose, and the face. That is the whole freight map of the orbit.

Host: A perfect map to end on, and it sets up what comes next beautifully. What is waiting for us in episode five?

Guide: Next time, in Extraocular Spaces, we step inside the orbit and stop treating it as one open room. The orbit is really layered into compartments: the subperiosteal space between bone and periorbita, the peripheral space outside the muscle cone, the central space inside it, and the episcleral space hugging the eyeball. Each compartment has its own borders, its own contents, and its own way of containing or spreading disease, and those boundaries explain why an abscess in one layer behaves so differently from swelling in another. If today was about the doors and where they lead, episode five is about the rooms inside. It is some of the most clinically elastic anatomy in the whole season.
