If you have ever sat at a slit lamp studying a lid margin, you have been staring at today's anatomy without knowing its names. Welcome, Guide. Take us in.

Guide: Thank you, it is good to be back. In episode six, Eyelid Layers, we walked the full stack of the lid: skin, the orbicularis layer, the orbital septum, the fat pockets, the retractors, and the palpebral conjunctiva at the back. One structure in that stack got only a passing nod, and today it gets the full hour. The tarsal plates are the two firm shelves that give the lids their shape, and the meibomian glands are buried inside them like seeds in a pod. By the end of this episode you will know the exact dimensions of each plate, what tissue it is truly made of, how its ends anchor to the orbital bones, the landmarks of the lid margin from cilia to posterior border, and the architecture of every meibomian gland from acinus to orifice. And you will see that this architecture, more than anything else, decides whether a patient's lid margin stays quiet or fills with lumps and plugs.

Host: Then let us start with the name itself. The tarsal plate. Where exactly is it, and what does it do for the lid?

Guide: Each lid contains one tarsus, so we speak of the superior tarsal plate in the upper lid and the inferior tarsal plate in the lower lid. The word tarsus is an old Greek word for a flat plank or frame, and that is exactly what you should picture: a long, narrow, gently curved sheet of stiff tissue, bent so that its surface conforms to the front of the globe, sitting just behind the lid margin and just in front of the conjunctiva. It is the skeleton of the eyelid. Everything else in the lid drapes around it: the orbicularis muscle in front, the conjunctiva behind, the retractors above and below. And here is the key spatial fact. The free lower edge of the superior tarsal plate actually forms the rounded margin of the upper lid itself, that firm little ridge you see and feel at the edge of a healthy lid. Without the plate, the lid would be a soft curtain of skin and muscle with no defined edge, and blinking would be a shapeless flop instead of a smooth sweep of a firm little shutter.

Host: You said stiff, and I suspect many students immediately think cartilage. Is the tarsal plate cartilage?

Guide: That is the classic error, and it is worth killing early, because the real answer is more elegant. The tarsal plate is not cartilage. It is dense fibrous connective tissue, rich in elastic fibers, with the meibomian glands embedded throughout its substance. Cartilage would be far too rigid for the job. A blink bends the lid around the curve of the eye, and the tarsus must flex slightly, spring back, and keep its shape for decades. Dense connective tissue loaded with elastic fibers gives you exactly that combination: stiffness to hold the margin as a straight, stable rail, and flexibility to bend a little on every one of the many thousands of blinks a day. The same logic appears all over the body. Structure follows demand. So when a textbook calls the tarsus rigid, read it as stiff but pliable, never as a piece of gristle or bone. And neither is it bone, though a stiffened lid can feel strangely hard at the slit lamp. Bone cannot remodel after injury the way fibrous tissue can, and the tarsus is remodeled constantly, by wear and by the surgeon's knife. Notice also what the plate lacks: there are no hair follicles anywhere inside it. The lashes belong to the skin edge in front of the plate, which is why the tarsal surface itself stays smooth and gland-lined all the way across.

Host: Good. Now give us the numbers. How big are these plates?

Guide: Here are the standard figures, and they explain a great deal of clinical behavior. The superior tarsal plate stands about ten millimetres tall at its center, and it tapers toward each end until it is barely one millimetre high. The inferior tarsal plate is much shorter, roughly four to five millimetres at its center. Both plates are about one millimetre thick, and each runs about twenty-five to thirty millimetres across, matching the width of the lid fissure between the corners of the eye. Notice the pattern in those numbers. The upper lid has a far taller plate because it has far more work to do. It must cover and uncover the whole cornea with every blink and every gaze change, so it needs a deeper shelf, a longer row of glands, and stronger anchoring. When you evert an upper lid and see that broad, flat expanse of tarsal conjunctiva, you are looking at the payoff of that ten millimetre height.

Host: Now take us around the plate like a map. What are its surfaces and borders?

Guide: Think of it as a thin plate with two faces and four edges. The anterior face lies against the orbicularis oculi, the muscle whose full story waits in episode eight, and high on the anterior face of the superior tarsus the levator aponeurosis plants itself. The posterior face is welded to the palpebral conjunctiva, and I mean welded: that conjunctiva is bound so firmly to the tarsus that you cannot lift it with forceps, which is why the everted lid presents such a smooth, fixed surface. Of the four edges, the free edge becomes the lid margin. The opposite edge, pointing away from the margin, is called the orbital border, and the orbital septum we met in episode six attaches along that orbital border of the tarsus. That junction is also a compartment boundary, the kind of wall we studied in episode five, Extraocular Spaces, where we saw how fascial planes decide where swelling and infection may travel. The remaining two edges, medial and lateral, are where the plate anchors to the bones, and those deserve their own moment.

Host: Let us have that moment. Where do the ends of the tarsal plates attach?

Guide: Medially, the ends of the two tarsi are gathered and carried by the medial palpebral ligament, a strong fibrous band that runs from the medial ends of both plates to the frontal process of the maxilla along the anterior lacrimal crest. You may remember that bony neighborhood from episode two, Orbital Bones and Their Clinical Importance, where we traced the lacrimal groove and the thin bone that forms its floor. The crucial spatial fact is that this ligament passes in front of the lacrimal sac, with related fibers passing behind it, so the sac sits cradled between the bands. What that means for the pumping of tears is a story we reserve for episode twenty, Complete Lacrimal Drainage Pathway, so for now just hold the geometry. Laterally, a much smaller band, the lateral palpebral ligament, carries the lateral ends of the plates to a small bony tubercle on the zygomatic bone just inside the lateral orbital margin, called Whitnall's tubercle. A large anchor medially, a small one laterally, and that asymmetry matters whenever lids are pulled, lax, or repaired. The ligaments also write the geometry of the eye corners: the medial palpebral ligament is short and powerful, so the inner corner of the lid fissure keeps its sharp, angular shape, while the lateral anchor is weaker and the outer corner rounds off gently. Anchor strength draws the shape.

Host: And the top edge of the superior tarsus? Things clearly hang from it.

Guide: They do, and I will name them now but keep them brief, because the muscle stories belong to episode eight, Eyelid Muscles. On the anterior surface of the superior tarsus inserts the levator aponeurosis, the flat tendon of the main lid opener, which also sends delicate slips forward into the lid skin to create the lid crease. On the upper border of the tarsus sits a tiny strip of smooth muscle, the superior tarsal muscle of Muller, which adds a fine tonic lift to the open lid. In the lower lid, a fibrous sheet called the capsulopalpebral fascia reaches the inferior tarsus with its own small smooth muscle strip. For today, the anatomical point is simple: the tarsal plates are the landing zones for the lid retractors. Every force that opens the lids finally plants itself on these dense shelves. Remember that handoff, and episode eight will feel like meeting the engine after studying the mounting brackets.

Host: Now for the stars of the title. What exactly are the meibomian glands?

Guide: The meibomian glands are modified sebaceous glands, oil glands of the same family as the glands of the scalp, but rebuilt for the lid margin. They are also called the tarsal glands, and that older name tells you where they live, because they are embedded within the substance of the tarsal plates, arranged vertically, filling the plate like seeds packed in a pod. The German anatomist Heinrich Meibom described them in the seventeenth century, and the eponym stuck. The counts are worth memorizing: roughly thirty to forty glands in the upper tarsus and twenty to thirty in the lower. Each gland follows one beautiful plan. There is a single long central duct running vertically through the plate, and around that duct hang clusters of acini, little hollow sacs of secretory cells, like grapes bunched along a stem. In structural language we call the whole gland compound tubuloacinar: one tube, many berry clusters. Because the upper plate is taller, its glands are long and elegant; the lower lid's shorter glands are stubbier and slightly more twisted.

Host: Let us shrink down and go inside one acinus. What is happening in there?

Guide: An acinus is a small sac of secretory cells wrapped around a tiny central space. In a meibomian gland those cells, called meibocytes, do something dramatic called holocrine secretion. New cells arise at the edge of the acinus, then migrate inward while filling themselves with lipid droplets, until the entire cell becomes a droplet-packed balloon. Then the whole cell breaks down and pours its contents into the lumen. The cell literally becomes the secretion. The product is called meibum, and chemically it is a rich mix of lipids, dominated by wax esters and cholesterol esters, with phospholipids and triglycerides alongside. It leaves the gland as a clear, sterile, oily fluid. Notice what the holocrine design implies. Because the secretion is delivered by cells that must die to release it, the gland must constantly regenerate its acini from the edges. A gland that works this hard, this close to the open air, needs flawless drainage, and that is where the anatomy gets interesting. Students hear the word secretion and picture the watery product of a sweat gland; remember that here the secretion is the lipid itself, manufactured inside cells and delivered by their sacrifice. It is why a meibomian gland under the microscope looks like a bunch of grapes at different stages of ripening, pale young acini at the edges and fat, oil-laden ones crowding the duct.

Host: Then follow the route out. Take us from acinus to surface.

Guide: Each central duct runs a long, straight, vertical course through the dense tarsal tissue, widening as it gathers acinar clusters along its whole height, and then it reaches the lid margin, where it opens in a single neat row, one orifice per gland, just behind a landmark called the gray line that we will define in a moment. Look at that duct under the microscope and you find it is lined by stratified squamous epithelium, the same lining family as the skin, and near the orifice it actually keratinizes, meaning it produces small horny flakes like dry skin. That is the anatomical Achilles heel of the whole system. A long, narrow tube, lined with cells that can shed keratin, discharging a thick lipid into the open air at the lid margin, through an orifice barely wide enough to see. Give the oil the right conditions to thicken and the duct the right conditions to shed, and you have built a plug waiting to happen.

Host: So what does the oil actually do once it reaches the eye?

Guide: Meibum spreads out with each blink to form the outermost layer of the precorneal tear film, the thin coat of fluid that covers the exposed front of the eye. You met that tear film back in Series 1, in the episode called The Front of the Eye, where we pictured it as the cornea's protective jacket. The lipid layer is the topcoat of that jacket, and its chief jobs are to slow evaporation from the aqueous layer beneath and to seal the lid margin so tears do not simply spill over the edge. How the three layers of the tear film are built, how thick each one runs, and how fast they turn over, that is a full story we reserve for episode fourteen, Tear Film Anatomy, so I will not re-teach it here. For today, hold the connection: the tarsal plate, through the glands buried inside it, manufactures the topcoat of the tear film that keeps the cornea from drying between blinks.

Host: Then let us walk the lid margin itself, because that is where these ducts finally surface. What are the landmarks?

Guide: The lid margin is the strip between the front of the lid, where the lashes stand, and the back, where conjunctiva takes over, and it is only about two millimetres wide. Walk it from front to back. First comes the rounded anterior border carrying the cilia, the lashes; the upper margin carries on the order of a hundred of them and the lower about half that. About midway across the margin you meet the gray line, also called the intermarginal sulcus, a faint gray streak running the length of the lid. Just behind it sits the row of meibomian orifices, one tiny dot per gland, best seen at high magnification. And behind the orifices lies the marginal strip, a narrow band less than a millimetre wide, clothed in conjunctival epithelium and kept constantly wet, ending at the posterior border where the wet surface meets the skin side of the world. That posterior border is the mucocutaneous junction of the lid, the exact frontier where the dry territory of skin hands over to the wet territory of the conjunctival surface. And notice what is missing from that wet strip: no glands of any kind open onto the marginal strip itself. It is a clean, polished pavement, wiped by every blink and kept gleaming by the oil arriving just behind it.

Host: The gray line sounds like a small thing, but I suspect it carries great weight. Why does it matter?

Guide: Because it is the dividing line of the entire lid, clinically and surgically. In front of the gray line lies the anterior lamella, which means skin and orbicularis muscle. Behind it lies the posterior lamella, tarsus and conjunctiva. Surgeons split lids along the gray line because it is the natural plane between those two worlds, and a tiny strand of orbicularis at the very margin, sometimes called the muscle of Riolan, sits right at that line and reinforces it; its muscle story waits for episode eight. The line also sorts disease for you at a glance. The lashes exit in front of it, so an inflamed, infected lash follicle is an anterior lamella problem. The meibomian orifices sit behind it, so plugged, pouting, inflamed oil openings are a posterior lamella problem. When a colleague describes a lid lesion as anterior or posterior to the gray line, that one anatomical streak is telling you which lamella, which family of glands, and which treatment logic is in play.

Host: Now put the gland architecture to work. Why does it matter at the lid margin? Take us to the lumps and bumps this geometry explains.

Guide: This is the payoff, and it flows straight from the anatomy. Consider the chalazion first. A chalazion is a chronic lipogranuloma, which means a firm, painless nodule built of trapped lipid walled off by inflammatory cells, and it begins when a meibomian duct is obstructed. The acini keep making meibum, the trapped lipid leaks through the acinar walls into the tarsal tissue, and the body quietly walls it off. Because the gland is embedded in the dense plate, the lump sits deep within the tarsus, points toward the conjunctival side, and is far easier to feel than to see. Now compare the stye, the external hordeolum, which is an acute infection of the small Zeis or Moll glands at a lash follicle, the accessory glands of the lash line we mentioned in episode six. It sits at the lash edge, in front of the gray line, red and tender. There is also the internal hordeolum, an acute bacterial infection of the meibomian gland itself, angry and swollen within the tarsus. One margin, three lumps, and only the anatomy tells them apart.

Host: And when the whole row of orifices misbehaves together, is that the story behind posterior blepharitis?

Guide: Precisely. Posterior blepharitis is chronic inflammation centered on the meibomian orifices, and the anatomy explains every sign you will see. The duct epithelium keratinizes and sheds flakes into the lumen, the meibum thickens, and together they obstruct the duct. At the margin you find the orifices pouting and capped with little plaques of oil and keratin. Press a lid gently and the expressed meibum comes out turbid and thick, sometimes like toothpaste, instead of a clear oil. And here is the anatomical reason the optometrist can do this at all. The glands are parallel and vertical, all converging on that single accessible row of openings, so modest digital pressure empties them in a visible line, while the tarsal plate holds the lid firm and flat under your thumb, like a cutting board steadying a vegetable. Evert the lid and you can even transilluminate it, shining light through the tissue so the glands appear as faint vertical stripes; healthy plates show long, even stripes, while diseased ones show gaps and stubs where glands have shortened or vanished. Grading and management belong to episode ten, Eyelid Anatomy in Clinical Practice, so today we stay anatomists.

Host: A strange question to test how well we have learned the architecture. What would happen if a lash follicle tried to grow where a gland should be?

Guide: You get one of the most striking demonstrations that the meibomian orifices are the lid margin's only back doors. The condition is called distichiasis, an extra row of lashes emerging from or behind the meibomian orifices. In its congenital form, the cells destined to become meibomian glands develop into hair follicles instead, so a lash exits through the very opening designed for oil. The gland blueprint and the hair blueprint are swapped in the same slot. Those misplaced lashes, sprouting from openings meant for clear lipid, rub against the cornea and tear film with every blink, and they irritate the eye out of all proportion to their number. Anatomy predicts the whole story: put a cilium where a duct should be, and the duct's job is done by a hair that scratches.

Host: You mentioned the firmly adherent conjunctiva and the everted lid. Is there a groove up there I should know about?

Guide: Yes, a small but famous one. On the everted upper lid, about two millimetres above the upper border of the tarsal plate, the surface dips into a shallow horizontal groove called the subtarsal groove, or subtarsal sulcus. Its anatomy explains a classic exam finding. When dust or a foreign body lands on the tarsal conjunctiva, blink after blink it is swept upward and comes to rest in that groove, where it sits and scrapes the cornea with every movement of the lid, producing the textbook pattern of fine vertical linear abrasions on the corneal surface. So whenever a patient has those vertical scratches, the culprit is hiding two millimetres above the tarsal border, and everting the lid, sometimes double everting it, exposes the prisoner. Chronic scarring in this region, classically in trachoma, settles along the same line as a horizontal band of scar. And when you see raised follicles or papillae dotting the tarsal conjunctiva, remember that their cellular detail belongs to episode twenty-three, Palpebral Conjunctiva, so we note them today and move on.

Host: One more anatomical consequence before we close. Why does the tarsal plate's stiffness matter over a lifetime?

Guide: Because the plate is the rail that holds the lid margin against the globe, any change in the plate changes the position of the margin. With age the tarsus loses tone and the ligaments and corner attachments slacken, and a lid that once hugged the eye begins to flop. If the margin rolls inward, a condition called entropion, the lashes turn against the cornea and abrade it with every blink. If the margin rolls outward, called ectropion, the wet marginal strip falls away from the eye, tears no longer collect properly, and they spill onto the cheek instead of draining. Scar tissue is the other great distorter: burns or chronic conjunctival disease can shorten the posterior lamella and drag the margin inward, which is called cicatricial entropion. Nearly every surgical trick for these problems is ultimately an operation on tarsal position, length, and anchoring. The rail shapes the train, and when the rail bends, everything riding on it suffers. And the lower lid does not escape either; its plate is only four or five millimetres tall, so a small scar or a little slack moves the lower margin dramatically. A small plate allows only a small margin of error.

Host: Quick check before we close. What about blood and nerves near the plate?

Guide: One preview only, since episode nine owns those stories fully. Each lid carries two arterial arcades, and the marginal arcades run just in front of the tarsal plates near the lid margin, so every structure we named today, glands, ducts, marginal strip, is richly fed within a millimetre or two of where you examine. The sensory nerves reaching the lids, and the motor nerves driving the lid muscles, are told properly in episode nine, Eyelid Blood Supply and Innervation. Today's job was structure, and the structure is now on your map.

Two tarsal plates, one per lid, made of dense fibrous connective tissue with elastic fibers, never cartilage, about ten millimetres tall centrally in the upper lid and four to five in the lower, each about one millimetre thick and twenty-five to thirty millimetres long.
