# Description: Tears do not simply sit on the eye; they quietly disappear into the air, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks follows that water out. Learn how evaporation is the dominant route of tear loss between blinks, how secretion budgets to replace it, and how blink rate, humidity, airflow and temperature set the pace. Follow the salts left behind as tear osmolarity climbs between blinks and resets with every blink, the normal saw-tooth rhythm that helps drive the eye's compensating secretion. Then see why the lipid layer is the passive regulator that keeps the whole water budget survivable. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want ocular surface physiology explained for the ear.

That sheet is sitting there at this moment, a film thinner than a thread of silk, holding the optical surface perfectly smooth and wet. And above it, open air. Room air is a drying machine, and the tear film is water on display with nowhere to hide. Between blinks, that water is quietly leaving, molecule by molecule, into the air around you. Host: Before we give the air the whole hour, show us the rest of the season map, so everyone knows which door is still closed.

Guide: Two episodes remain, and they are a matched pair. Today, episode nine, Tear Evaporation: the loss of tear water into the air, and the economy the body runs to pay for it. Then episode ten, Tear Clearance, closes the season with the other exit, the drainage pathway, and asks what happens to tears that are not evaporated. So today the subject is the air; next time, the plumbing. Keep that pairing in mind whenever I say the word loss, because in physiology that word has two addresses, and this episode owns only one of them.

Host: Then let us start with bookkeeping, the way you like to. Tears are secreted more or less continuously, the eye sits open to the room most of the day, and yet the surface neither floods nor dries out through a working day. Ask the ledger question for us directly. When a tear leaves the ocular surface, where can it go?

Guide: There are exactly two exits. Exit one is into the air: water at the surface escapes as vapour, and that is evaporation. Exit two is the drainage system: the puncta at the lid margins near the inner corner, the canaliculi, the sac, and the duct down into the nose, which carry surplus fluid away. Now the timing point that shapes this entire episode. Drainage is a blink event. It works mainly when the lids close and the tear passages are squeezed, which we will prove properly next time. Between blinks, with the lids open and the pump idle, drainage is nearly silent. So in every open-eye interval, evaporation is the dominant route of loss. Most of the water that leaves the film between blinks leaves as vapour, not as drainage. Today belongs to the air.

Host: Give us the scale of this. How much water are we actually talking about, on the eye and leaving it?

Guide: The whole tear apparatus of one eye, the film on the surface, the menisci that pool along the lid margins, and the reserve held in the conjunctival sac, holds about seven microlitres. Seven millionths of a litre; a very small drop by any standard. Into that budget, the glands pour basal secretion at roughly one to two microlitres a minute, which we established in episode six, Tear Secretion. And the air takes its share: in a comfortable room, measured evaporation from the ocular surface runs at about a tenth to a fifth of a microlitre every minute. Those numbers look trivial, but they are relentless, and they have to balance every single minute the eye is open.

Host: Seven microlitres is abstract, and a tenth of a microlitre is worse. Convert it for me. Of the whole store on the eye, what fraction does the air claim?

Guide: At the rates I quoted, the air claims roughly two parts in a hundred of the entire tear store every minute. That sounds like small change until you remember the store is never topped up by anything except secretion. Now add one more piece of geography. The eye is not equally exposed. Between the lids, in the interpalpebral zone, the film stands open to the room, and that exposed patch, a few square centimetres of it, is where the loss happens. Under the lids, and pooled in the tear meniscus at the lid margins, fluid is sheltered and free to diffuse back across the surface. So the picture is a thin, exposed sheet losing water across its whole face at once, renewed from a reservoir measured in microlitres. That is why the renewal rhythm matters more than any single number.

Host: All of this is astonishingly difficult to imagine measuring. How do you weigh something as faint as water leaving an eye?

Guide: With patience, and with a small chamber. The method is usually called evaporimetry, and in essence it seals a little chamber against the skin around the eye so that the only moist surface inside is the exposed part of the film. Air is moved through the chamber at a known rate, sensors track how much humidity the air picks up on the way past, and from those two numbers you can calculate the microlitres of water leaving per minute. Vary the room, the humidity, the airflow past the eye, and you can watch the dials of the evaporation equation move one at a time. That is where the figures I quoted come from, and it is also why they are quoted as ranges: the rate is genuinely a property of the room as much as of the eye.

Host: That word balance is the hinge of the whole episode, I think. Say precisely what steady state means for a film this thin.

Guide: Steady state is income matching expenditure. Secretion is the income. Evaporation, and eventually drainage, are the expenditures. When income equals loss over each minute, the film holds a constant thickness, and the surface stays wet and smooth. You can even watch the expenditure directly: instruments that measure film thickness see it thinning slowly through every open-eye interval, a few micrometres per minute in ordinary conditions, until the next blink deposits fresh secretion and restores it. So the steady state is not stillness. It is a running balance, like a bank account with a steady wage arriving and a steady trickle leaving. The body does not try to abolish the expense of evaporation. It arranges income to cover it, and every structure we have met this season exists somewhere in that arrangement.

Host: You have implied, without quite saying it, that the film can outlast an interval. How much margin does a normal film have over a normal interblink stretch?

Guide: This is one of the most quietly beautiful numbers in the season. A healthy tear film, left completely undisturbed, does not fail in three or four seconds. Its natural endurance, the time it can hold together before it breaks up, is typically well beyond that, often ten seconds or more, and sometimes far longer. In other words, the ordinary blink schedule renews the film long before the film is in any trouble, and the system runs with deliberate slack. The margin is the design. It means a normal film treats a normal interblink interval as easy duty, and even a stretched interval, eight or ten seconds of deep concentration, still fits inside the endurance it was built with. The four dials we are about to meet decide how comfortably that endurance is spent.

Host: Good, and that leads straight into the mechanics. Evaporation is not one number fixed for every eye and every room. You promised me dials. Set up the equation in plain words.

Guide: In plain words, the rate of evaporation from the eye follows four variables, and you can hold them in a single sentence. More open-eye time means more loss. Drier air pulls harder. Moving air strips away the eye's cushion of humidity. Warmer water escapes faster. Open-eye time, humidity, airflow, temperature. Those four dials set how fast the film pays its water out into the room. None of them is exotic; you have felt every one on your own skin. But skin can retreat into clothing or indoors. The ocular surface is permanently on display, so for the eye these dials are not lifestyle choices. They are the weather it lives in, minute by minute, and the body must run its budget under whatever weather it gets.

Host: Take the dials one at a time, starting with the one the body itself controls: open-eye time. What sets it?

Guide: Open-eye time is blink rate's shadow. At rest, an adult blinks about fifteen to twenty times a minute, which leaves each open-eye interval at roughly three to four seconds. Every second of it is evaporation time. Now change the behaviour: sit down with a gripping book or a screen and concentrate. Blink rate falls, commonly to roughly half, and each interval stretches toward eight seconds and beyond. The air has not changed at all, but the exposure has: fewer blinks means every patch of film works longer before renewal arrives. Half the blinks, roughly double the interblink time, and the water budget has to stretch with it. This is the purest dial in the equation, because it is set not by the weather but by what you are doing with your attention.

Host: And to be clear, that is ordinary behaviour, not a fault. Say more about what concentration does to blinking, because every listener is doing it right now.

Guide: Entirely ordinary, and I want to keep this firmly inside normal physiology. Concentrating makes blinks less frequent, and often less complete, with the lids not always sweeping all the way down. That is what attention does to the blink circuitry, it happens to everybody, and no management belongs in this series. What matters for our budget is the arithmetic. The film is built around continuous renewal: spread, thin, renew, spread, thin, renew. When blinking quiets down, the same design simply runs with longer stretches between renewals, so evaporation gets more time on every interval and thinning goes further before the next blink restores the film. And the compensating side, extra secretion or an urgent blink when the surface has been open too long, belongs to the control story we will reach later in this episode. For now, hold the dial: reading and screens change blink statistics, and blink statistics are open-eye time.

Host: Here is a case I had not thought about. What happens to this entire economy while you sleep?

Guide: It closes for the night, almost literally. When the lids shut, the exposed zone disappears, a boundary layer of humid air is trapped against the film, and evaporation collapses to nearly nothing. The drying dials we are walking through are dials for the working day, the hours of open-eye time. Sleep is the reset shift: closed lids, no vapour loss worth naming, and the surface resting in its own humidity. Episode one of this season, Why We Need Tears, framed the eye's central problem as transparency plus exposure; nightly closure is the oldest and cheapest part of the solution. It is also why the evaporation budget only has to balance while the eye is open, which is exactly the condition we are studying today.

Host: Dial two, then: humidity. Most of us cannot feel the air taking water from our eyes, but you say it pulls. Explain the mechanism.

Guide: Sit close to the tear film in your mind, and notice that the air touching it is not ordinary room air. The first sliver of air above any water surface rapidly becomes saturated: it holds as much vapour as it can carry, forming a thin, humid boundary layer that clings to the surface. Water can only leave the film as fast as it can diffuse through that layer into the drier air beyond. So the driving force is the difference in vapour between the boundary layer and the room, and humidity is the dial that sets the far end of that difference. Dry air, heated winter rooms, aircraft cabins, wide open deserts: each widens the difference, and the pull grows. Humid air narrows it, and evaporation slows to a crawl. The eye never stops this exchange; it only feels the gradient. A wet surface in dry air is a surface being taxed.

Host: Dial three is airflow, and I suspect this one surprises people, because a fan feels refreshing rather than relevant to tears.

Guide: Airflow matters because of the boundary layer we just met. In still air, the humid blanket above the film stays put, and escaping water must diffuse through it, which is slow. When air moves, wind, a ceiling fan, a ventilation duct, even brisk walking on a cool day, that blanket is stripped away and replaced with fresh, drier air. Every replacement resets the gradient to its maximum, like peeling a wet coat off the surface and doing it again and again. This is why a still room is kinder to the ocular surface than a breezy one at the same temperature and humidity, and why the same winter day feels different on a sheltered street than on an open one. Airflow does not change how dry the room is. It changes how faithfully the room's dryness is delivered to your eyes.

Host: Dial four, temperature. Water evaporates faster when it is warm, and yet the eye sits exposed at about thirty-four degrees. Walk us through it.

Guide: Warm water molecules move faster, so a warm surface loses vapour more readily than a cool one. Now notice a quiet convenience of ocular design: the front of the eye is one of the coolest surfaces of the whole body. It sits at the air interface, it is not buried under insulating tissue, and it loses heat to the room continuously, so the exposed surface runs a few degrees below core temperature. That slight coolness lowers the vapour pressure at the surface, and evaporation runs a little slower than it would from a warmer fluid. The cornea pays for its exposed transparency with mild coolness, and quietly collects a discount on evaporation in return. Temperature still works as a dial, of course: a hot, dry wind steals more water than the same wind cooled. But the body's baseline is set a notch lower than you might have guessed.

Host: Here is my question, then. The body cannot set the room's humidity or switch off the wind. Of the four dials, which ones does physiology actually get to touch?

Guide: Only two, and they are the internal ones. Open-eye time is physiological: the blink circuitry answers to the nervous system. Secretion is physiological: the glands answer to the control circuit we built in episode six, Tear Secretion. Humidity, airflow and temperature belong to the room and the weather, and the body adapts around them rather than commanding them. But adaptation is not helplessness, because there is one more player in the budget, a passive one. The eye does not fight the weather with weather. It fights it with a lid of oil. That is the lipid layer, the topmost sheet of the film, and it is the reason this whole evaporation economy stays survivable. We gave it a full episode already, so today we only reclaim the parts that matter for the water budget.

Host: Remind us, then, without stealing episode three's thunder. What is the lipid layer, and how does a film of oil slow water loss?

Guide: The lipid layer is an exceedingly thin sheet of oily secretions, delivered mostly by the meibomian glands, whose anatomy we visited in Series 2, episode seven of season one, Tarsal Plates and Meibomian Glands. Each blink expresses fresh oil and spreads it across the open eye, where it floats on the aqueous because oil and water refuse to mix. Its molecules line up with water-loving heads pressed against the aqueous and water-avoiding tails presented to the air. In that arrangement the layer becomes a toll gate on the evaporation road. A water molecule in the aqueous must cross an oily sheet to reach the air, and crossing oil is slow. Slow, notice, not stopped: vapour still passes, which is exactly why evaporation happens at all. The layer does not abolish the loss. It taxes the loss, heavily, and that tax is what keeps the budget affordable. Episode three of this season, Lipid Layer Function, owns this story in full.

Host: Give us a sense of how heavy that tax is. What would the loss look like with no lipid lid at all?

Guide: Measurements comparing the same surface with and without effective lipid cover find evaporation rising several-fold when the cover is absent, which is a dramatic effect for a sheet measured in nanometres. And notice what that means in budget terms. The lipid layer secretes no water and answers to no control circuit. It is passive infrastructure, renewed by every blink, that sets the exchange rate between the film and the air. A thin oil sheet turns a surface that would lose water quickly into one that loses it slowly enough for secretion to keep pace. That is this season's design logic in one line: the aqueous layer is the reservoir, the lipid layer is the regulator, and the blink is the delivery system. Today we follow what escapes the tax anyway.

Host: Now the consequence thread, which I suspect is why this episode matters far beyond water accounting. When water leaves the film, everything dissolved in it cannot leave. What does that do?

Guide: This is the elegant part. The tear film is not pure water; it is a salt solution, carrying sodium, chloride, bicarbonate and a crowd of other dissolved players. Water evaporates. Salts do not. Every molecule of water that escapes leaves the remaining film slightly more concentrated, and the concentration of a solution is exactly what osmolarity measures. Normal tear osmolarity sits around three hundred milliosmoles per litre, a figure worth carrying with you, because it stands close to the body's general internal baseline. Between blinks, as the film thins, that number drifts upward. Under ordinary conditions the drift is small, a handful of milliosmoles, but it is real, it is measurable, and it happens in every open eye, every waking minute. Evaporation does not merely spend the water budget. It quietly steeps the tea.

Host: And when the next blink arrives? Describe the shape of that up and down for us.

Guide: The blink resets it. Fresh secretion is dilute: it arrives at the saltiness the glands make, lower than the drifted film, and the blink spreads it across the surface and mixes it in. Osmolarity drops back toward baseline, the film is restored, and the whole climb begins again. Plot osmolarity against time and you draw a saw-tooth: a slow climb through every open-eye interval, a sharp drop at each blink, over and over, all day long. This is not a flaw and not a warning. It is the normal operating rhythm of an evaporating surface under constant renewal, as unremarkable to the eye as breathing in and out is to the lungs. What makes the saw-tooth physiologically interesting is not just that it happens. It is that the eye is listening to it. Which brings us to the signal.

Host: Say that plainly, because it sounds as if the film's byproduct is doing a job. Is the osmolarity drift just wear, or is it information?

Guide: It is information, and it plugs straight into the control circuit. Recall the set point idea from Series 3, episode three of season one, Ocular Homeostasis: the body defends its internal variables around target values, measures deviations, and corrects. Tear osmolarity is one of those measured variables. Osmosensing machinery, from the ocular surface itself up through the neural circuitry that runs the lacrimal gland, reads the saltiness of the film. When evaporation pushes osmolarity upward, that reading is part of what drives the compensating secretion we met in episode six, Tear Secretion, asking the glands to match the loss. It also leans on the other dial the body owns, biasing the blink side of the ledger so renewal arrives when the film has been open too long. The saw-tooth is the error signal riding on the set point, and the eye spends its entire waking day cancelling it.

Host: Let me put the whole cast on stage together, because this season has introduced a lot of players and I want to watch them cooperate. Who does what in one accounting?

Guide: One system, one budget. The aqueous layer, episode four of this season, Aqueous Layer Function, is the reservoir being spent. The mucin work of episode five, Mucin and Ocular Surface Wetting, holds the water in an even sheet against a surface that is otherwise hard to wet, so the film thins uniformly instead of breaking into patches. The blink, episode eight, Tear Distribution During Blinking, repaints the film and delivers fresh secretion, resetting both the thinning and the osmolarity. The lipid layer, episode three, taxes the evaporation that makes all of this necessary. The lacrimal glands, episode six, pay the income side, paced by the very osmolarity signal that evaporation creates. And the air takes its share, at a rate set by open-eye time, humidity, airflow and temperature. Nothing in that chain is accidental. Every player exists because a wet, exposed, transparent surface must keep paying a water bill it can never stop owing.

That is the episode in five lines, and every line is normal physiology, no exceptions.
