# Description: Every blink hands you a freshly made tear film, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks explains the factory and the tap behind it. Learn how basal secretion keeps a continuous trickle flowing from the accessory and main lacrimal glands, and how the main gland scales its output up on demand. Follow the control circuit in plain words: the ocular surface reports through trigeminal sensory nerves to a tear centre in the brainstem, the answer travels the parasympathetic limb of the facial nerve through the pterygopalatine ganglion, and sympathetic fibres fine-tune the flow. Meet the gland cell at work, exporting water, electrolytes and proteins like a true exocrine factory, and hear how androgens, oestrogens and prolactin tune the system over months and years. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want tear production explained for the ear.

Host: Every blink hands you a fresh tear film, and every one of those films has to be manufactured on demand, somewhere behind the upper and outer corner of the eye. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology, the series where we switch on the machinery inside the rooms that Series 2 built for us. We are midway through Season 2, Tear Film and Ocular Surface Physiology. Episodes one through five covered the why and the what: why the eye needs tears at all, how the film stacks into its layers, and what the lipid layer, the aqueous layer and the mucin layer each contribute at the surface. Today we climb upstream, to the factory and the tap. This is episode six, Tear Secretion, and it answers two questions at once: how are tears actually made, and how does the body keep the output matched, minute by minute, to what the living surface needs? Our patient guide is back, and the tap is in good hands. Set up the big idea for us.

Guide: Thank you, good to be back. Here is the sentence to carry through the whole episode: secretion is a managed variable, not a constant. The tear film is not a puddle that filled once and sits there. It is delivered, spread, spent and drained, then delivered again, about fifteen times every waking minute, and that delivery has never once stopped in your life. So the body needs a tap with a hand on it at all times, and that is precisely what it built. Hold one picture and everything today will hang on it: two gears. Gear one is a trickle that never stops, a continuous baseline flow that keeps the surface wet between blinks. Gear two is an accelerator, an adjustable supply that can rise quickly when demand rises. Recall the hardware for a moment, because Series 2 already mapped it and we will only borrow it. The main lacrimal gland, seated in its fossa high and out to the side in the orbit, was the tour of Series 2, episode one of season two, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy. Its lobes, ducts and delicate relationships were the business of episode two of season two, Lacrimal Gland Parts and Relationships. And the small accessory glands scattered in the conjunctiva were episode three of season two, Accessory Lacrimal Glands. Today those parts start working together as a production line.

Host: Two gears, a trickle and an accelerator. Let us take them one at a time. Start with the trickle. What exactly is basal secretion, and which glands make it?

Guide: Basal secretion is the continuous, quiet production that keeps the ocular surface wet when nothing special is happening: no wind, no onions, no emotion, just eyes open and working. Two sources share the job. The first is the accessory glands, the Krause and Wolfring glands of the conjunctiva, tiny exocrine cousins of the main gland that drip their watery product into the fornices around the clock, largely without waiting for instructions. The second is the main lacrimal gland itself, which is not silent at rest. It runs a low-grade background activity, a slow idling that adds to the trickle. Put the numbers in and respect how small they are: basal flow is measured in microlitres per minute, on the order of a single microlitre or so, which is roughly a drop of moisture every hour from each eye. Across a waking day that amounts to about a millilitre, perhaps a little more, per eye. And the trickle is faithful beyond any employee you have ever met: even sleep does not stop it entirely, the flow simply eases, which is why the closed eye stays comfortable behind its lids through the night. A whisper of a flow, and yet the surface cannot stay clear for more than seconds without it.

Host: That smallness surprises me. If the gland can scale up, why does the body bother with a trickle at all? Why not fill a reservoir in the morning and spend it through the day?

Guide: Because the tear film is not stored, it is maintained, and the losses never stop. The film is only a few micrometres thick, a coat of moisture thinner than a human hair, spread over a surface that is permanently open to the air. Water leaves it continuously by evaporation, and the excess is constantly pulled away into the drainage puncta at the inner corner. The film is also, quite literally, the working optics: the smoothest refracting surface of the eye, the one the light crosses first. If it thins out and breaks between blinks, the world smears. And it is a supply line too: the living cells of the cornea take their oxygen from the air through this thin wet coat. So the trickle is sized to the leak. It is exactly what episode nine of this season, Tear Evaporation, will quantify when we reach it, and it is why the tap is never allowed to close. A reservoir strategy would fail within a blink or two. Continuous manufacture, matched to continuous loss, is the only design that works for an optical surface that also has to breathe. The lesson generalises across the body: wherever a surface must stay continuously wet, the body does not build a tank, it builds a supply line. The mouth, the airway, the gut lining, all are kept wet by continuous secretion, and the eye is the most demanding customer of the family, because its coating must also be optically perfect.

Host: Which brings us to the second gear. The main gland idles quietly at rest, you said. What changes when the demand rises, and what does the body actually get when it turns that tap up?

Guide: The main gland is the adjustable component of the whole system, the part with headroom. At rest it contributes its modest share to the trickle. When the surface calls for more, its output can multiply many fold, tenfold and beyond, and the character of the secretion changes with it: more fluid, more protein, delivered fast. That scaled-up output is what you experience as watery eyes in wind or smoke, and it is properly called reflex secretion. Ask a simple question and the difference between the gears shows itself: the eye of someone chopping vegetables in a warm kitchen runs on gear one, and the same eye, carried out into freezing wind, jumps almost instantly to gear two. Now, one boundary for today, and it matters. The full tour of the reflex, the whole loop from the onion on the cutting board to the flood, with its triggers and its remarkable emotional variant, belongs to episode seven, Reflex Tearing. I will not steal that thunder. Today we stay with the production line itself: the two gears, the wires that control them, and the factory floor where the fluid is actually made. Control first, then. Who feels the need, and where does the order to make more tears actually get written?

Host: Take me into the control circuit, then. Every managed system needs a sensor. What is reporting the state of the ocular surface, and to whom?

Guide: The sensor is the surface itself, and it is exquisitely wired. The cornea is one of the most densely innervated tissues in the entire body, hundreds of times richer in nerve endings than the skin, and we walked that wiring in Series 2, episode seven of season four, Corneal Nerves. The conjunctiva carries its own dense sensory net as well. Some of these endings are mechanical, some are thermal, and some are polymodal, answering many kinds of insult at once, and that variety lets the surface tell a cooling draught from a drifting eyelash from a whiff of chemical vapour, all within one reporting system. These are sensory fibres of the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve, riding mainly in its ophthalmic division and reaching the surface through the long ciliary nerves and their branches. Their cell bodies gather in the trigeminal ganglion, and from there the reports run into the brainstem. What do they report? Everything the surface knows: temperature, dryness signals, mechanical contact, chemical irritation, the fine continuous bookkeeping of a working front window. In the language of our two gears, this is the wire that tells the controller whether the trickle is keeping up.

Host: And the controller? Where do those reports land, and what sits there, reading them?

Guide: They land in the brainstem, in a small cluster of neurons that earns the name tear centre. Anatomists call it the lacrimal nucleus, and you may also meet it described alongside the superior salivatory nucleus, because the two sit close together in the pons and share family business: both run secretory traffic for the head. This is the controller of our loop. It listens to the sensory traffic arriving from the surface through the trigeminal wire, weighs it against everything else the body is telling it, and decides what the tap should do next. And it never goes fully silent. Even at rest there is a low continuous hum of instruction leaving this nucleus, and that hum is the neural half of your basal secretion. In control-room language, this is a modest little room with a big responsibility, and like any good controller it works on two kinds of instruction: the continuous baseline command, and the sharper commands that ride on top when the reports turn urgent. The other inputs that can excite this centre, from bright light to strong feeling, are real, and we will name them properly when we walk the reflex in episode seven. For today, hold the shape: surface reports in, tear centre weighs, decision out.

Host: So the decision is made in the brainstem. Now the delivery question. The gland sits far away, high in the orbit, tucked behind bone. How does that order physically reach it?

Guide: By wire, and the wire is the parasympathetic limb of the facial nerve, the seventh cranial nerve. Follow the order step by step. Preganglionic fibres are born in that lacrimal nucleus and leave the brainstem inside the facial nerve. Within the temporal bone they peel away as a small branch called the greater petrosal nerve, carrying nothing but the secretory order. It runs forward along the floor of the skull and meets company: sympathetic fibres heading for the same address arrive from the carotid plexus as the deep petrosal nerve. The two join, one carrying the accelerator pedal and the other the fine-tuning, and together they form the nerve of the pterygoid canal, which runs to the pterygopalatine ganglion, the relay station we are about to visit. There the parasympathetic fibres synapse, change trains, and the postganglionic fibres ride onward, hitching along branches of the trigeminal nerve, first with the zygomatic nerve and finally transferring onto the lacrimal nerve, which drops them at the gland. Notice the elegance of the routing. The facial nerve never touches the gland itself. It hands the order to a relay, and the relay hands it to a courier. The full cranial nerve cast list here is worth revisiting in Series 2, episode nine of season ten, Cranial Nerves in Ocular Function, where the facial and trigeminal nerves take their bows.

Host: A relay station is a lovely image, but it raises an engineering question. Why does the body build a ganglion into this route at all? Why not wire the brainstem straight to the gland?

Guide: Because one narrow pipeline from the brainstem serves many customers. The pterygopalatine ganglion sits in the pterygopalatine fossa, and you met that fossa back in Series 2's orbit season as a small but busy crossroads deep in the skull base. Busy is the word. Through that one relay pass the secretory orders for the lacrimal gland above, and for the mucous glands of the nose and palate around it. The ganglion is a switching yard: one trunk line arrives carrying parasympathetic traffic, and different trains are sorted onto different local tracks for the nose, the palate and the orbit. Wiring the brainstem directly to each of those destinations would need a separate canal through the skull for every one. Routing them through a single fossa and a single ganglion is the economical design, and it is why the orbit's secretory supply line and the nose's share one deep crossroads, a fact of routing that makes the pterygopalatine fossa a favourite stop on every skull-base map.

Host: Fine, the order arrives at the gland. Now take us onto the factory floor. When that signal lands on a lacrimal gland cell, what actually happens inside? What is the product?

Guide: The gland is an exocrine factory with two outputs, and stimulation switches on both. Output one is water and electrolytes. The parasympathetic messenger, acetylcholine, lands on receptors of the acinar cells, the ones packed around the beginnings of the ducts. Inside each cell, calcium rises as the internal alarm signal. That calcium opens channels for chloride in the cell membrane, salt moves out, and water obligingly follows, because wherever salt goes, water goes to even the osmotic score. The result is a steady flow of watery secretion into the duct system, and the ducts adjust its salt content on the way past, tuning the electrolyte recipe. Output two is protein, and it is a different production line in the same cell. Lacrimal proteins, the antibacterial enzyme lysozyme, the iron-gathering lactoferrin, the lipid-carrying lipocalin, plus a set of growth factors, are manufactured in advance, folded, packaged into little export vesicles and stored near the cell surface. When stimulation says go, the vesicles fuse with the membrane and empty their cargo outward. Watch the difference in speed, because it tells you the design. The fluid lever works in seconds, throwing open channels that already sit waiting in the membrane, while the protein lever spends its stores, parcels held ready on the loading dock, and restocking takes longer than dispatching. Notice the elegance: one organ, two very different products, a fluid lever and a protein lever, and they can be worked independently, because the parasympathetic hand pulls the flow lever hardest while the sympathetic hand pulls hardest on the protein lever.

Host: You said exocrine, and I want that word pinned down properly, because we have not used it in this series yet. What makes a gland exocrine, and what is the alternative?

Guide: Exocrine means secreting outward, through a duct, onto a surface. The alternative is endocrine, secreting inward, into the bloodstream, the way the thyroid or the adrenal gland works. The lacrimal gland is emphatically exocrine. Its collecting ducts gather the output of thousands of acini and deliver it through the main ducts into the upper conjunctival fornix, a secretion onto a surface, kin to sweat and saliva rather than to hormones. Around each acinus sit basket-shaped myoepithelial cells, muscle-like cells that contract on cue and squeeze the acinus to help drive the fluid along. And the factory takes in outside help for its immune product. Plasma cells sheltering in the gland's own tissue manufacture immunoglobulin A, the antibody, and the gland's duct cells stitch it into a protected form that can survive on the exposed surface. This is the machinery behind the cargo list we taught in episode four of this season, Aqueous Layer Function. That episode described what the aqueous layer carries and why; today we meet the works where the cargo is loaded. The mucin story has its own home in episode five, and the lipid is made by a different set of glands entirely.

Host: One more wire to trace before we close the loop. The sympathetic fibres joined the parasympathetic ones in that deep canal. What is their job when they arrive at the gland?

Guide: The sympathetic limb is the fine tuner rather than the main throttle. Those fibres are born in the upper chest, climb beside the spine, relay in the superior cervical ganglion at the base of the skull, and then ride the carotid artery's nerve plexus upward. From there they join the deep petrosal nerve and slip through the pterygopalatine ganglion without changing trains, continuing to the gland along its blood vessels. At the gland they do three quiet things. They adjust the calibre of the blood vessels, and that matters because a secretory factory is only as good as its plumbing: blood is the raw material of tears. They bias the gland's protein output, tugging hardest on that protein lever we met on the factory floor. And they contribute to the overall tone of the tissue. In everyday language, the parasympathetic is the accelerator pedal, the sympathetic is the mixture and the engine temperature, and both hands stay on the controls all day. There is a lesson about balance here too: the two autonomic limbs do not fight over the gland so much as share it, the way two hands share one task, and the gland's behaviour at any moment reflects the ratio between them.

Host: Which brings me back to the trickle, and a question the two-gear picture begs. If all of this is wiring and response, why does the tap never close completely? Why is there always some secretion flowing?

Guide: Because the loop is live at all times. The tear centre never stops humming its low-grade instruction, so the main gland never fully falls silent, and the accessory glands, which barely wait for orders, keep their constitutive drip going regardless. Together they hold the basal flow, gear one. Around that baseline, the loop runs its endless accounting. The surface sensors report continuously, the tear centre compares the report with the set point, a wet, smooth, nourished, optically clean surface, and the autonomic output adjusts, up a little when losses rise, down a little when they fall. That is a feedback loop in the plainest sense: sensor, controller, cable, actuator, and a variable being managed. It runs blink by blink, hour by hour, all your life. And standing behind the fast loop, like a calibration that changes over years rather than seconds, sits a second, slower layer of control, and that deserves its own answer.

Host: The slow layer. You have been keeping it back deliberately. What are hormones doing to a system whose fast decisions are already handled by nerves?

Guide: The hormones set the gain. Androgens, oestrogens and prolactin circulate for years, and they tune the secretory machinery's resting tone, its sensitivity, its protein recipe, its very structure. Androgens are the strongest supporters of secretory output, and they act on the lacrimal gland and, by the same logic, on the meibomian oil system we covered in episode three of this season. Oestrogens modulate the picture, contributing their own influence on the glands and the surface. Prolactin, better known for its milk work, turns out to be a working partner of the lacrimal gland as well, supporting its function alongside the other hormones. Notice the timescale, because it is the point. These are not second-to-second decisions, that is the nervous system's job. Hormones re-calibrate the tap over months and years, through puberty, through pregnancy, through the quiet drift of the later decades, so that the fast loop always operates on a well-set machine. And because they act slowly, their effects are easy to miss in a single visit and easy to see across a life, which is one more reason the optometrist is a student of time as much as of tissue. Think of them as the engineers who tune the instrument, while the nerves are the hands that play it.

Host: Let us put numbers on the range, the way we always do before we close. How far apart are the two gears, the trickle and the flood, in plain figures?

Guide: Basal, remember, runs about a microlitre a minute, call it a millilitre and a bit across a waking day for each eye. Reflex demand can push the main gland to ten times that flow and beyond, which is why a strong cry can outrun even the drainage system, the puncta and the ducts that Series 2 followed through its lacrimal season, and spill over as visible tears. Few organs in the body can swing their output that steeply, that fast, on demand. And yet the everyday miracle is the other end of the range: the precision of the trickle, matched so closely to evaporation and drainage that the film holds its few micrometres of thickness blink after blink, hour after hour. Both ends matter to an optometrist, because both ends are visible at the slit lamp.

Host: Let us bring it to the chair, as we always do. Our listeners are optometry students and practising optometrists. Why does the control physiology of secretion change what they see on an ordinary Tuesday?

Guide: Because everything they look at through the slit lamp is downstream of this circuit. The tear meniscus at the lid margin, the film's thickness and stability, the way it renews between blinks: those are readouts of secretion, distribution, evaporation and clearance working in balance, and this season takes them one at a time. When the story calls for it, secretion itself can be measured: a folded filter strip hooked briefly into the lower fornix wets a number of millimetres in five minutes, and that humble test is simply putting a ruler against the managed variable we studied today. You do not need any disease chapter to value the mechanism. Even the language helps: when a record says the film looked thin, or the meniscus low, you are reading, in effect, the gauge on the supply line we traced today. If you know the loop, sensor, centre, wire, gland, then any readout you see becomes a clue about where in the chain to look, and the normal numbers we recited become the yardstick the whole subject leans on.

Host: Before we wrap, do what you do so well. Walk me through the whole episode again, slowly, the way a student should say it back in an oral examination.

Guide: With pleasure. Tears are made continuously, not stored. Gear one is basal secretion, a trickle of about a microlitre a minute from the accessory glands plus the main gland's low-grade idling, sized to match constant losses by evaporation and drainage. Gear two is the adjustable output of the main lacrimal gland, which can multiply tenfold and beyond on demand. The sensor is the densely innervated ocular surface, reporting through trigeminal sensory fibres. The controller is the tear centre, the lacrimal nucleus of the pons. The wire out is the parasympathetic limb of the facial nerve, running as the greater petrosal nerve, joining sympathetic fibres in the nerve of the pterygoid canal, relaying in the pterygopalatine ganglion and hitching along trigeminal branches to the gland. The sympathetic limb, relayed in the superior cervical ganglion, fine-tunes blood flow and protein output. At the gland cell, stimulation drives two outputs: salt and water into the ducts, and stored proteins exported from vesicles, an exocrine factory with two levers. Hormones, androgens, oestrogens and prolactin, tune the whole machine's gain over months and years. And secretion is a managed variable, the tap held, never released.

Host: It is a tight list. One last question, then, the one we always save for the end. Across all of this, what do you find students underestimate most about tear secretion?

Guide: The cost, and the effort. It is easy to picture the tear film as a lucky puddle, a little water the eye happens to sit in. It is the opposite. Every microlitre is pumped out by living cells, against gradients, with salt moved on purpose so water will follow, proteins manufactured and packaged in advance, myoepithelial cells squeezing on cue, a brainstem nucleus on shift around the clock. The surface is not a customer that collected a bargain. It is an employer that never stops demanding, and the gland never stops delivering. Hold that respect for the machinery, and every later conversation about the film, its layers and its balance, will feel like reading the work rosters of a factory you have already walked through.
