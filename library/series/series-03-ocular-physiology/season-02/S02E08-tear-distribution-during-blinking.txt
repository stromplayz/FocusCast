# Description: Every blink is a pump stroke, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks walks one complete blink cycle in slow motion to show how tears are spread and drained by the same movement. Learn what the closing lid does to the tear film as it sweeps down like a squeegee, how the lid-margin reservoirs are compressed, and how the canaliculi and the lacrimal sac are squeezed to push tears toward the nose. Then follow the reopening, as fresh tears are pulled up from the reservoirs, the lipid layer spreads across the surface in a wave, and the drainage system refills by suction, priming the next stroke. Hear the numbers in words, from a resting blink rate of roughly fifteen to twenty blinks per minute to closures lasting about a tenth of a second, and learn why the tear meniscus at the lid margins is the film's supply buffer between blinks. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists, with curious listeners welcome.

Host: You have already blinked twice since this episode began, probably several more times, and you noticed none of them. That is the strange privilege of the ocular surface: its most important mechanical event happens over and over, all day, below the level of attention. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology, where we are deep in Season 2, Tear Film and Ocular Surface Physiology. We have spent seven episodes building the system: why we need tears, how the film is organised into layers, what the lipid, the aqueous and the mucin each contribute, how secretion is driven, and what happens when reflex tearing floods the surface. Today the system meets its engine. This is episode eight, Tear Distribution During Blinking, and our guide is here to walk one single blink in slow motion. Set the stage for us.

Guide: Thank you, good to be back. Here is the idea that runs the whole episode. A tear system has two jobs that must happen simultaneously: it has to deliver fresh tears onto the exposed surface, and it has to remove the used ones, so that the surface never sits in a stale puddle. Delivery without removal floods the eye; removal without delivery dries it. Notice, too, where the two jobs are located. The main tear gland sits high on the upper outer wall of the orbit, so the fresh supply arrives at one spot above the eye's outer corner, while the exit doors wait at the inner corner near the nose. Neither the arrival point nor the exit point is where the fluid most needs to be. Moving tears from one to the other, and around the whole surface in between, is a transport problem, and transport is exactly what the blink is for. The blink is the single movement that serves both at once, which is why I want you to picture it as the engine stroke of the whole apparatus. One cycle, two half-strokes: a downstroke that closes the lids, and an upstroke that opens them again. During the downstroke we will watch the film being swept and gathered, and the drainage plumbing being squeezed empty. During the upstroke we will watch fresh tears being pulled up out of storage, the lipid layer being spread in a wave, and the plumbing refilling by suction. Then we will put numbers on the whole thing and ask why the tears are stored where they are. One blink, in slow motion, from first contraction to full refresh.

Host: Before we press play on the slow motion, recall the machinery for us. Which muscles are we going to watch, and where do I find them in this series' back catalogue? Name them, and point me to the exact episodes so listeners can find their way back.

Guide: One sentence of recall each, because anatomy is not today's syllabus. The closer is the orbicularis oculi, the ring of muscle inside the eyelid that pulls the lids together when it contracts; the opener is the levator palpebrae superioris, which lifts the upper lid back to its open position. For the record on wiring, since physiology follows innervation: the facial nerve drives the closer, and the oculomotor nerve lifts the opener, which is one reason the two actions can be modulated separately. Both were mapped in full in Series 2, episode eight of season one, Eyelid Muscles, and if you want the lid's layered build, episode six of that same season, Eyelid Layers, is your reference. Notice something about the pairing, though, because it matters for physiology. Every blink you make today is a contest with a clean outcome: the closer acts while the opener releases, then the opener acts while the closer releases. Antagonists taking turns. That arrangement, one muscle per direction, is the signature of a device built to cycle, and a device built to cycle is built to pump.

Host: And the other half of the machinery, the drainage side. Where do tears leave the stage when the pump pushes them out?

Guide: Recall, again, one sentence per structure. Tears leave the surface through the puncta, the two tiny openings on the lid margins near the inner corner, one on each lid. From each punctum, a fine tube called the canaliculus runs toward the inner corner and then inward and backward, and the two canaliculi join to empty into the lacrimal sac, a small reservoir tucked into a groove of bone beside the nose. The sac drains downward through the nasolacrimal duct into the nasal cavity. Series 2 gave every piece its own episode: episode six of season two, Puncta; episode seven of season two, Canaliculi; episode eight of season two, Lacrimal Sac; and episode nine, Nasolacrimal Duct. Today those structures stop being a map and start being moving parts. Hold the shape in mind, openings at the lid margins, tubes running to a sac, a duct below the sac, because that is the pipe the blink is about to squeeze.

Host: Now the stage itself. Before the blink begins, in the instant before the lids start to move, where are the tears exactly? I used to picture a thin coat over the eye and nothing else.

Guide: That picture is half right, and the missing half is our first real lesson. The film spread across the exposed surface is astonishingly thin, a few micrometres, and astonishingly light; the volume actually coating the open eye between blinks is tiny. The bulk of the tears are not on the eye at all. They are in storage. The main stores sit along the lid margins: a strip of liquid called the tear meniscus, a low ridge of fluid running the length of each lid edge, upper and lower, deepest toward the inner corner where the puncta wait. Do not be fooled by its modest height. Seen at the slit lamp, the meniscus is a ridge of a millimetre or less, yet because the film itself is only a few micrometres deep, the great majority of the tears on the front of the eye at any moment are sitting in those marginal strips rather than spread across the cornea. Behind and below, the fornices, the shallow shelves where the conjunctiva folds back on itself, hold further reserve. Series 2, episode four of season two, Tear Film Anatomy, described those compartments, and our own episode two, Tear Film Organisation, showed how the three layers sit within them. The meniscus is the one to watch today, because the blink will draw on it the way a barman draws from a cask. Storage is not an accident of anatomy. It is the design that makes the whole cycle possible.

Host: Then let us roll the film. Slow motion, first half. The command has gone out and the orbicularis is contracting. Walk me through the downstroke from the inside, as a physical event rather than a diagram.

Guide: Start with the lid as a whole. The upper lid descends like a curtain coming down over a stage, smooth and fast, driven by the orbicularis while the levator stands down. From first movement to full closure, the shut takes about a tenth of a second, one hundred milliseconds, quicker than most single words spoken aloud. The lower lid, meanwhile, barely moves; it shifts slightly inward and upward at most. The lower lid is not the actor here. It is the shelf, the fixed blade of the squeegee, and the upper lid is the moving blade. Keep that image, because a window washer works the same way: one blade glides while the other holds the line. Everything the downstroke accomplishes flows from that simple geometry, a moving edge sweeping across a wet surface toward a collection point at the inner corner. One more detail belongs in the slow motion: as the lids close, the eyes themselves roll gently upward and outward beneath the lids. That swing is a normal reflex that accompanies closure, and it keeps the sensitive central cornea tucked up under the shelter of the upper lid while the sweep happens below. And note the energy source while you are at it. The muscle that closes the lid is also, as we will see in a moment, the muscle that wrings the plumbing. One contraction, two jobs.

Host: You called the lid a squeegee, so make it literal. What does that descending edge actually do to the tear film it passes through?

Guide: Three things, in order, as the edge sweeps down. First, it gathers the aqueous. The watery part of the film is pushed ahead of the lid like water ahead of a windscreen blade, moving downward and inward until it joins the meniscus reservoirs at the margins. Second, the edge passes over the mucin. The mucin is not really wiped away, because its deepest portion is anchored to the surface as a fine gel bound to the outer membrane of the surface cells; the blink polishes and renews that coat rather than scraping it off. Third, the edge sweeps the lipid. The oily layer, floating on top, is pushed downward and compressed into the meniscus as the lid closes, where it stacks onto the store. Notice what this means. The downstroke is not the spreading stroke. It is the collecting stroke: it harvests the used film, folds it into the reservoirs at the lid margin, and clears the stage. The spreading comes later, on the way back up, and we will be there for it.

Host: Hold on. Why is the inner corner the collection point at all? The eye is round, the lids are long, and yet everything drains toward one end of it. What decides that?

Guide: Geometry and plumbing, decided long before birth. The puncta sit near the inner corner, so the exits are there, and the lid margins taper toward that corner in a way that favours flow along them toward the puncta; the deepest pooling of the meniscus, where the upper and lower stores meet in a small lake just beside the nose, sits right beside the exits. Then add the sweep. Every downstroke pushes fluid medially as well as downward, because the closing lid edge travels slightly toward the nose as it descends, and the lower lid's tiny inward shift nudges the pooled meniscus against the puncta at just the moment the pump chambers behind them are about to be squeezed. Collection point, exits and pump chamber all meet in one small triangle of tissue at the inner corner. That concentration is also why, when tears are made faster than the pump can run, overflow gathers at the inner corner and spills over, as anyone who has wept knows well.

Host: And while the lids are shut and the film is being gathered, what is happening inside the plumbing? You have been waiting to say the word pump.

Guide: The waiting is over, because the downstroke is the pump's power stroke. Recall the geometry: orbicularis fibres are woven around the lid margins, right where the puncta sit, and fibres of the same muscle also reach the wall of the lacrimal sac. When the muscle contracts, it squeezes. The puncta are pressed shut by the closing lids, so nothing escapes outward. The canaliculi are compressed and emptied. The sac is squeezed too, and the fluid inside it, gathered since the last blink, is driven down the nasolacrimal duct and out, into the nose. Upright, the duct runs downward and gravity lends a hand; lying down, the squeeze alone does the moving, which is why the pump is built to be used constantly rather than now and then. That is why a good cry is followed by a runny nose: the sac empties a little with every squeeze. Pressure applied from outside the pipe pushes fluid along it. In one motion, the same closing lids that gathered the surface film have expelled the sac's contents. Collection on the surface, expulsion in the pipe, one stroke.

Host: Halfway. Give us the downstroke in one breath, the way you would want a student to say it back in an examination.

Guide: The lids close in about a tenth of a second. The descending edge sweeps the aqueous and the lipid ahead of it into the meniscus reservoirs and polishes the mucin coat. The closing muscle compresses the puncta, the canaliculi and the sac, and drives the sac's contents down the duct toward the nose. Downstroke: collect the film, empty the pump chamber. That is the breath. Everything in the second half of the cycle exists to undo the gathering gracefully and to prime the next squeeze.

Host: Second half, then. The downstroke is done and the lids are closed for their instant. What opens them, and is opening just the same movement played backwards? My guess is that the reopening is where the real distribution happens.

Guide: The levator takes over, the orbicularis lets go, and the upper lid rises. Opening is not the same movement backwards, and the difference is the whole point. First, the timing: reopening is a little slower than closing, roughly one and a half to two times as long, so the full cycle, close and open, still finishes in well under half a second. Second, and more important, the roles swap. The descending edge was the collector; the ascending edge is the distributor. As the lid lifts, its margin drags a fresh ribbon of tears up out of the meniscus. The store is drawn on, the edge carries the fluid upward, and behind the rising lid a new film is laid across the surface, mucin beneath, aqueous in the middle, and then the oil. The lower lid helps too, easing its meniscus upward a fraction late in the cycle, and the order of the laydown is worth memorising: the mucin coat is renewed in place, the aqueous is dragged up as a fresh sheet, and the lipid lands last, sealing the top.

Host: The oil. You said the lipid was swept down and stored during the close. Now I want to hear about the wave.

Guide: The wave is real, and it is one of the loveliest sights in the physiology of the surface. As the lid reopens, the lipid layer does not simply get dragged upward; it spreads across the surface in a wave that follows the ascending edge, from the margins toward the exposed centre, an oily sheet gliding over the fresh aqueous and settling into a continuous blanket. That blanket is what slows evaporation between blinks, and the behaviour of the layer in the open-eye interval is the material of episode three of this season, Lipid Layer Function, which owns the spreading and reorganisation of the layer in detail. For today, hold the timing, because the timing is the lesson: the lipid spreads on the upstroke. A blink is not merely a wetting event. It is a spreading device, and the reopening edge is the applicator that paints the oil across the surface, evenly, every few seconds.

Host: You also promised that the plumbing refills while the lid opens. Explain the suction to me slowly, because a pipe that was just squeezed empty seems like the last thing that would fill on its own, and this is the half of the pump nobody ever thinks about.

Guide: It fills because the squeeze lets go. Follow the recoil. The orbicularis relaxes, so the compression on the canaliculi and the sac is released. Those structures spring back toward their resting shape, and when a flexible chamber springs back after being emptied, the volume inside it increases and the pressure inside it falls below the pressure outside. Fluid flows from high pressure to low. The puncta, uncovered as the lids part, re-immerse in the meniscus, and tears are drawn through them into the canaliculi and onward into the refilling sac. Nothing mysterious here; it is the same physics as a syringe drawing back, expansion creating suction. By the time the lid reaches its open position, the sac is filling again, loaded for the next downstroke. Physiologists summarise the whole mechanism as two pressures: a positive-pressure phase on closure that drives fluid out, and a negative-pressure phase on opening that draws fluid in. The names are plain and so is the physics, but the coupling is the clever part. The system refills itself as a direct consequence of the same blink that emptied it.

Host: Then name the concept for us properly. What do we call this coupling of spreading and suction, and what does it teach us about what a blink actually is?

Guide: The concept is the lacrimal pump, and the lesson is that blinking is not just wetting, it is actively pumping drainage. Every cycle runs both strokes. The downstroke is the expulsive stroke: the muscle squeezes, the sac empties, tears move toward the nose. The upstroke is the filling stroke: the muscle relaxes, the chambers expand, tears are drawn in from the meniscus. If you like engines, it is a two-stroke serving two systems at once. The delivery side gets fresh tears spread across the surface, and the removal side gets a positive push out followed by a suction refill, every single cycle, without ever switching machines. And notice the elegance of the coupling: the very same motion that spreads tears over the eye also drives their exit. Distribution and drainage are not two processes that happen to overlap. They are two outputs of one mechanism. What the pump moves out of the eye, and how quickly the surface is cleared of the old fluid, is the story of episode ten, Tear Clearance, which owns clearance properly; today we own the pump itself.

Host: Give us the numbers. How often does this engine run, how long is one stroke, and how many strokes has it logged since we all woke up this morning?

Guide: Numbers, in words, the way you would want them recited in an oral examination. The spontaneous blink rate at rest is roughly fifteen to twenty blinks per minute, which is to say the eye completes a full close-and-open cycle every three to four seconds, all day, without anyone throwing a switch. That rate matters more than it first appears, because it sets how often the surface is refreshed, and therefore how long the film must survive on its own between refreshes. Each closure lasts about a tenth of a second, and the complete cycle, close and reopen, finishes in under half a second. Run the arithmetic and a person awake for sixteen hours blinks somewhere in the region of fifteen to twenty thousand times a day. Each of those cycles lays down a fresh film, because the full refresh, from the used film being gathered in to the new film being spread out, is accomplished in a fraction of a second. A surface that renews itself every few seconds, for a lifetime, on a budget of a few microlitres: that is the kind of engineering that only looks simple.

Host: That rate cannot be constant, though. Nobody blinks twenty times a minute while reading a good book, surely. What moves the number up and down?

Guide: Attention moves it, and that is a normal feature of the design. When the eyes lock onto a demanding visual task, close reading, a screen, threading a needle, the spontaneous rate drops, sometimes to a third or less of the resting figure, and when the task ends the rate rebounds. Conversation tends to nudge the rate up. The brain is throttling the engine to suit the load, because during hard visual tasks the system trades a thinner film for steadier viewing. Two consequences follow, and both are pure physiology. Between blinks the spread film gradually thins, and the thinner it gets, the more everything depends on what happens next, which is evaporation, the subject of the very next episode. And a slower rate means longer open-eye intervals, which the reservoirs and the lipid shield have to bridge. The engine's speed is one of the great variables of the ocular surface, and every other part of the system is built to cope with the range.

Host: One more variation before we close the engine bay. Does every blink close completely? I find that hard to picture, honestly, because complete closure looks like effort when I do it on purpose.

Guide: Many do not, and it is worth knowing as a plain fact about normal eyes. A good share of natural blinks are partial: the lid descends partway, sweeps the lower part of the surface, and rises again without ever meeting the lower lid. The system tolerates this, because distribution is graded with closure. A partial blink still stirs the film, still harvests the lower tear film, still runs the pump, just at a smaller amplitude, and complete closures are interleaved among them, especially now and then alongside genuine full closures. The point to carry is proportionality: what the blink accomplishes scales with how far the lid travels. A brief or shallow blink is a small stroke of the engine rather than a broken one. We will leave the entire subject of closure quality, how it is assessed and what its patterns mean, to the clinical series. In this series we stay with the machine at its normal settings, and the normal machine mixes full and partial strokes freely.

Host: Then let us return to the storage question, because we opened it and I want the design logic finished. Why keep most of the tears at the lid margins instead of just making more on demand?

Guide: Because the blink and the glands keep different schedules, and the reservoir is what lets them agree. Watch the two rates. Secretion, which we covered in episode six of this season, Tear Secretion, flows at a steady basal pace, a trickle sized for the whole day. The blink, meanwhile, spends its allowance in bursts: once every few seconds it draws a ribbon of fluid out of the meniscus, spreads it, and waits. Without a store, the film's supply would have to match the blink stroke for stroke, an instant of pumping the moment the lid lifts. With the store, the glands top up the reservoir continuously at their own pace, and the blink withdraws from it at its own pace. The meniscus is the buffer between two rhythms. It also works by capillarity: a narrow wedge of liquid along the lid margin holds fluid the way the edge of a paper towel holds ink, which keeps the store stable against gravity and against every direction of gaze. When you see a glistening ridge along a lower lid margin at the slit lamp, you are looking straight at the cask, not at the film. Buffer, inkwell, cask: choose whichever image you like, but keep the function. The reservoirs are the film's supply buffer between blinks, and the system's smoothness depends on them never running low.

Host: Now assemble the whole engine for us, end to end, one full cycle, the way this episode should be remembered.

Guide: Here is the cycle, one motion, start to finish. The eye is open; the film is spread, thinning slowly; the meniscus stores are full; the sac is full. The orbicularis fires. The upper lid sweeps down in about a tenth of a second, harvesting the used film into the reservoirs, polishing the mucin coat, sweeping the lipid downward; the puncta seal, the canaliculi collapse, the sac empties toward the nose. The levator fires in turn. The lid rises, dragging fresh tears up out of the meniscus, laying down a new aqueous film, spreading the lipid in a wave behind the ascending edge; the plumbing springs open, pressure drops, and the sac refills by suction. The lid settles. The film is new, and the pump is primed, the whole refresh paid for by a muscle contraction that costs the body almost nothing. In a fraction of a second the surface has been washed, fed, oiled and drained, and in three or four seconds it will happen again, and again, roughly twenty thousand times a day. The lids are the pistons, the muscles are the crankshaft, the meniscus is the fuel tank, the sac is the pump chamber, and the film lying on your cornea at this moment is that engine's current stroke, laid down moments ago and already scheduled for renewal.

Host: Which leaves one interval we have repeatedly skipped: everything that happens after the upstroke ends and before the next downstroke begins. The open-eye seconds. What is going on out there while the engine idles?

Guide: That interval is the next episode's entire territory, and it is where the system faces its hardest physics. The moment the lid finishes opening, the film is at its best: freshly spread, full thickness, new. Then it begins to lose water to the air, because the front of the eye sits directly in the atmosphere and water molecules leave the surface the whole time the eye is open. The lipid blanket is the shield that slows that loss, the rate of loss is evaporation, and the balance between that loss, the blink rate that resupplies the surface, and the reservoirs that fund the resupply, is the film's running budget for the day.
