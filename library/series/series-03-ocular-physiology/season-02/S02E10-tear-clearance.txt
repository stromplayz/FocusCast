# Description: Every tap needs a drain, and this season finale of Focus Cast by focuslinks walks the exit door of the tear system, from the pooled tears at the lid margins through the puncta, canaliculi, lacrimal sac and nasolacrimal duct to the nose. Learn how each blink drives the lacrimal pump, why the tear film is replaced many times over in a waking hour, and how turnover and clearance carry away debris, shed cells and worn proteins so the surface chemistry stays fresh and stable. The episode then assembles secretion, distribution, layer function, evaporation and clearance into one closed loop with the blink as its engine stroke, and recaps episodes one through nine by title before teasing the corneal season ahead. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want physiology explained for the ear.

Host: Here is a question we have circled all season without once asking out loud: where do old tears go? For nine episodes we have spoken of tears as if they were made once and stayed. Secreted by glands, spread by blinks, sealed by oil, anchored by mucin, left standing on the eye like a polished coat of varnish. But nothing on a living surface stands still, and the tear film least of all. It is made constantly, and what is made constantly must be removed constantly, or the eye would drown in its own protection. So today, for the season finale, we follow the exit door. We will walk the drainage route the way the water meets it: from the pooled tears at your lid margins, through two tiny openings called the puncta, into long thin tubes, down a small reservoir, and out through a duct that delivers used tears into the nose. Then we will step back and watch the whole season assemble into one closed loop. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to the last episode of our season on the tear film and the ocular surface. To send us out properly, our guide is back. Set the frame for us: why does an episode about the exit door deserve to close this season?

Guide: Because supply and removal are not two stories, they are one story, and a season that taught only supply would have taught half a system. Think of a bath. The tap fills it, the drain empties it, and the water stays fresh only because both are working. Seal the drain and the bath overflows. Seal the tap and the bath stagnates. The tear system is a bath built to a miniature tolerance, holding about seven microlitres of fluid at any moment, less than a single drop, and into that tiny volume the glands pour fresh supply minute by minute. Something must leave, at the same pace, or the chemistry of the surface would drift outside the narrow range the living cells tolerate. Here is the plan for the hour. First, the route: we follow used tears from the lid margins through the puncta, the canaliculi, the lacrimal sac and the nasolacrimal duct into the nose. Second, the concepts: turnover and clearance, the twin measurements of renewal. Third, the integration: we put secretion, distribution, layer function, evaporation and clearance together, with the blink as the stroke that drives them all. Then comes the season recap, one episode at a time, and a look through the next door.

Host: Then let us start a step before the puncta, because the drain does not begin at the openings; it begins at the waiting. Picture your own lower lid in a mirror, and look for the glistening edge where lid meets eye. You have told us all season that the film over the cornea is only a few micrometres thick, a whisper of water. So where does the real bulk of the tear fluid actually sit, and how does it get herded toward the nose-side corner between blinks?

Guide: The bulk sits at the edges. Look closely at any eye and you will see it: a glistening strip of fluid running along the margin of each lid, a little deeper than the film on the cornea, pooling where lid meets globe. Anatomists call these the marginal tear strips, and where the two lids approach each other at the inner corner of the eye, the corner beside the nose, the pooling deepens into a small standing lake. Now comes the design detail that makes the whole system work. In the margin of each lid, upper and lower, close to that inner corner, there is a tiny round opening called a punctum, plural puncta, each one a fraction of a millimetre across, ringed by a collar of dense fibrous tissue that holds it open, served by muscle fibres that can squeeze it shut. That is the mouth of the drain. Every blink presses the marginal strip toward those two little mouths, and gravity lends a hand whenever the head is upright. The full anatomy, the sizes, the positions, the neighbouring structures, was mapped in Series 2, episode six of season two, Puncta. Today we care about one thing only: tears pool at the margins, and the pool has two ready exits.

Host: Two openings a fraction of a millimetre wide, expected to carry the entire outflow of the eye, all day, every day. On its face that sounds impossible. What force could drive fluid through an opening that small, blink after blink, for a lifetime? I have heard the phrase lacrimal pump. Unpack it for us, stroke by stroke.

Guide: The phrase is exact, and we have already met the engine. In episode eight, Tear Distribution During Blinking, we watched the lids sweep the film and squeeze the marginal strip inward. The same closing stroke drives the pump. Inside each lid runs a flat muscle, the one that closes the eye, and some of its fibres wrap around the puncta, the canaliculi and the sac. Now follow the sequence of one blink. The lids begin to close, and those fibres contract. The puncta are squeezed shut, the long thin tubes beyond them are wrung like drinking straws, and the sac is compressed like a bellows. Fluid inside cannot escape backward, because folds of tissue act as one-way valves at every junction. So it moves onward, down and out. Then the lids open again. The squeezed tubes spring back to their resting shape, the sac refills, and that reopening creates a brief moment of suction which draws the pooled tears of the marginal strip through the puncta and into the canaliculi. Blink by blink, the eye takes a small swallow of its own tears. A person blinks roughly fifteen times a minute while awake, and every stroke of that rhythm is a stroke of the pump. No separate organ, no extra power supply: the machine that spreads the tears is the same machine that removes them.

Host: Good. A dose of tears enters with every blink, then. Now follow that dose. It has just been drawn through a punctum. Where does it go next?

Guide: Into the first leg of the plumbing, the canaliculi. From each punctum a narrow tube runs straight down, or straight up for the upper lid, for about two millimetres, then turns at a right angle and runs horizontally toward the nose for another eight millimetres or so. The upper lid has one, the lower lid has one, and the two meet just before their destination in a short common channel. That bend matters functionally: the first, vertical part is exactly the portion the closing lid grips and wrings most effectively, so the pump's squeeze is applied where the pipe is most exposed to it. At the entry to the sac sits another one-way fold, angled so tears can pass inward but cannot pass back out, which is why the closing stroke pushes fluid forward and the opening stroke draws fresh tears in rather than returning the old ones. The fine anatomy of these tubes, their walls, their lengths, the angle of their meeting, belongs to Series 2, episode seven of season two, Canaliculi. For today, hold them as what they are functionally: the flexible pipes the pump squeezes, the first stretch of the exit road.

Host: And the two pipes converge before the end. Where do the upper and the lower canaliculi deliver their load?

Guide: Into the lacrimal sac, the reservoir of the system. Picture a small pouch, about twelve to fifteen millimetres tall, sitting in a hollow hollowed into the bone at the inner corner of the orbit, just behind the skin you touch at the side of the nose. The common canaliculus opens into its upper part, and its lower end narrows into the duct that continues downward. The sac has no other exit, so whatever enters it must leave downward, which is what makes it a reliable relay rather than a storage depot. Functionally, the sac is the bellows of the pump: compressed at every blink, springing back between blinks, receiving a pulsing input from above and delivering a steady output below. The bony hollow that cradles it, its coverings and its neighbours were mapped in Series 2, episode eight of season two, Lacrimal Sac. Today you need only its role: a small holding tank that turns pulses into flow.

Host: The last leg is the one with a famous destination, the one everyone half knows from crying. Take the tear from the sac all the way out of the eye.

Guide: From the sac, the nasolacrimal duct runs downward, slightly backward and slightly outward, through a bony canal in the wall between the eye socket and the nose, for about fifteen to eighteen millimetres, opening at last inside the nasal cavity, beneath a scroll-shaped shelf called the inferior turbinate. That destination explains a familiar everyday scene. When you cry hard, your nose runs, and much of that fluid is tears, arriving through the drain because the outflow has overwhelmed what evaporation and the nasal lining can absorb. It also explains the faint salty taste at the back of the throat in moments of strong emotion: the used tears pass into the nose and are swallowed, and the drain quietly joins the mouth. Eye and nose share plumbing, which is why the lacrimal season of Series 2 lived beside the anatomy of the nose. The duct's own tour, its course, its lining, its folds of valve-like tissue, was episode nine of season two, Nasolacrimal Duct, and the whole route joined end to end was episode ten of the same season, Complete Lacrimal Drainage Pathway. You have now walked that route functionally, in one breath: pool, puncta, canaliculi, sac, duct, nose.

Host: Let us put numbers under the whole route now. How much fluid actually leaves the eye this way, and how fast? Give us the daily ledger of the drain, in figures worth memorising.

Guide: The ledger is small in volume and enormous in rate. Basal tear secretion runs at roughly one to two microlitres per minute, which sounds trivial, and across a waking day it adds up to somewhere near a millilitre and a half, about a quarter of a teaspoonful, so the daily outflow is genuinely modest. The striking figure is the exchange rate. The whole tear film holds only about seven microlitres, so a flow of one to two microlitres per minute replaces the entire pool roughly every five to eight minutes. Sit with that for a second: the film you are looking through as you listen to this is not the film you were looking through ten minutes ago. In a waking hour the eye turns over its tears about ten times or more, and across a waking day it completes well over a hundred full exchanges. And the ledger balances to the last drop: what the tap adds, the drain removes, at the same pace, for as long as the eyes are open. That balance is not an accident and it is not automatic. It is the steady state every other episode of this season has quietly assumed, and tonight it finally has a mechanism and an address.

Host: You used two words just now that deserve separating, because students blur them into one: turnover and clearance. Are they the same thing, and if not, what does each word measure?

Guide: They are two faces of one flow, and the difference is worth a minute. Turnover is a statement about time: how quickly the contents of the tear film are replaced with fresh fluid. Clearance is a statement about contents: how completely whatever is in the film, the salts, the proteins, the debris, is carried away rather than left behind. You cannot have one without the other, because both are produced by the same movement of fluid inward through the puncta, but they answer different questions. Turnover asks, is the water new. Clearance asks, is the old gone. And what travels with the flow in each direction is the heart of the matter. In one side: fresh water from the glands, fresh electrolytes, fresh defensive proteins, fresh nutrients for the surface cells. Out the other: the used versions of all of those, plus everything the eye does not want to keep. The tear film is not a coating that ages gracefully in place. It is a conveyor, and every minute the belt moves: fresh water, fresh proteins and fresh electrolytes continuously displacing the old, and the old leaving through the drain.

Host: So make the negative case for us. Suppose the drain simply stopped while the tap kept running. What happens to the chemistry of a surface that is supplied but never cleared?

Guide: You get a pond instead of a stream. First, salt. The surface is open to the air, and evaporation is always lifting pure water out of the film; in a flowing system the drain carries away the concentrated remainder along with everything else, but with no outflow the dissolved salts would climb steadily, and the surface cells would find themselves bathed in fluid ever further from the concentration they are built for. Second, protein. The glands load the tears with enzymes and antibodies that do their work on the surface and then age; without clearance those worn molecules linger, change shape, and stop doing the job they were sent to do. Third, debris. The surface sheds its outermost cells, the blink sweeps up fragments of lipid and mucin, the air leaves dust in the film, and all of it needs a one-way road out. Clearance is the housekeeping service of the ocular surface. It works in partnership with the secretion tap, and together the two keep the surface's chemistry fresh, stable and inside the narrow band the living cells underneath require. Neither end of the pipe can do the job alone.

Host: Then give us the inventory. What exactly rides out through that drain on an ordinary day? List the cargo, item by item.

Guide: Five kinds of cargo, roughly. First, the water itself and the salts dissolved in it, which are the bulk of the flow. Second, defensive proteins past their working life, the patrolling enzymes and antibodies that have spent their activity on the surface and are now simply spent cargo. Third, the surface's own shed cells, because the outermost layers of the cornea and the conjunctiva renew themselves constantly, and the casualties of that renewal are carried off with the flow. Fourth, swept debris, the clumps of lipid and mucin and the trapped dust and particles that each blink gathers from the film and pushes toward the corners where the drain waits. Fifth, overflow. You will remember episode seven, Reflex Tearing, when the taps open fully in response to irritants or emotion; no amount of evaporation can match that flood, so the drain becomes the safety valve, and that is why strong feeling and chopped onions both end at the nose. Notice what is absent from the list: nothing the surface needs is lost faster than it is replaced, so long as supply and clearance stay matched. The outflow takes what is old and lets the fresh remain.

Host: Now the part I have been waiting for, because a finale earns the right to say the big sentence. Every process this season has described, the filling, the spreading, the sealing, the feeding, the losing, seems to move at the same rhythm. Put the loop together for us, station by station.

Guide: The loop has five stations and one engine. Station one, secretion: episode six showed the main gland above and the accessory glands below keeping a basal flow running, ready to surge when the surface calls. Station two, distribution: episode eight showed the blink spreading that supply across the surface in a single wipe, and pressing the remainder toward the corners. Stations three, four and five are the work the film does while it sits there, one episode each: the lipid layer at the very surface slowing evaporation to a trickle, the aqueous layer delivering water, electrolytes, proteins and nutrients, and the mucin layer anchoring the whole film to a surface that would otherwise repel it. Then the two exits. Evaporation, episode nine, takes its share upward, quietly, every second, through the air. Clearance, today, takes its share inward, through the puncta, the canaliculi, the sac and the duct. And the engine that strokes all of it, the fill, the spread, the mixing at the surface, and the push toward the drain, is the blink. One closed loop, one rhythm. The eye does not manage its tears with five separate machines. It manages them with one muscle, several hundred times an hour.

Host: One muscle, one rhythm, one loop. Say the steady state sentence for the whole system, and then keep a promise we made at the very start of the series: show us which Series 1 idea this entire season has been servicing.

Guide: The steady state sentence is this: the tears are a flow, not a pool, and the surface you examine is a place where fluid passes, not a place where fluid sits. As for the idea we are servicing, you already know it. Series 1, episode four of season two, The Front of the Eye, introduced the cornea as the clear front window of the eye, exposed to the air, without blood vessels of its own, and dependent on what touches it for its livelihood. Everything this season has taught is the service schedule for that window. The cornea needs oxygen, which the film helps deliver; it needs a stable, gentle chemistry, which turnover maintains; it needs to stay smooth and wetted, which the spreading wipe and the three layers achieve; and it needs all of it continuously, which is why the loop never stops while you are awake. A window stays clear not by being covered but by being serviced. When this season is done and the next one opens, it is that window we turn to directly. The tears were its support system. Now, soon, we meet the tissue itself.

Host: Before the recap, two practical corners, because this is the finale and we can afford them. First corner: the pump is muscular. So what happens to clearance when the muscle rests, overnight, in sleep?

Guide: Then the housekeeping service takes its nightly pause. Sleep means the lids close and stay closed, the blink stops, and with it the pump; outflow slows to a trickle driven by gravity and the passive pressure of whatever the glands still deliver. Secretion slows too, so the imbalance is smaller than it sounds, but the film does sit still for hours, and standing fluid cannot carry debris the way a stroked pump can. The result is a familiar morning sight: the used fluid and its gathered debris, concentrated overnight at the inner and outer corners where the lid margins meet. Small crusts at the lid margins on waking are the ordinary footprint of a paused pump, and knowing the mechanism turns that observation from a curiosity into physiology you can explain to a patient in one sentence. When the eyes open, the pump resumes within the first few blinks, distribution is restored, and the loop returns to its waking pace. Upright posture helps the lower reaches of the duct along, but the pump itself is muscular, so it works sitting up, lying down, or head tipped any way life asks.

Host: Second corner, and it is the chair. Our listeners are optometrists and students. Where does clearance physiology show up in an ordinary examination, short of any talk of disease?

Guide: In two places you can see without instruments. First, the meniscus: the strip of tears along the lower lid margin, the very pool we started from tonight. Its height is the visible reservoir of the whole system, the balance point of tap and drain made visible at the lid edge, and trained eyes read it the way a sailor reads a waterline. Second, the drain's work rate. Place a small drop of fluorescein, the dye that glows green in blue light, and watch how long it takes to fade from the surface; what you are watching is the drain at work, the dye leaving with the flow, blink by blink. A surface that holds its dye a long time is telling you the conveyor is slow; one that clears it briskly is telling you the pump is strong. None of that is a diagnosis, and none of it needs to be. It is literacy: you cannot read the signs of a troubled clearance system later if you have never watched a healthy one work today. Physiology first, always. That has been this series' rule from episode one, and it holds at the exit door as much as anywhere.
