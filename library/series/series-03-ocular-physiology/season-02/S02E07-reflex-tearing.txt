# Description: The ocular surface runs a fire alarm, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks traces the whole circuit from spark to flood. Learn how trigeminal sensors across the cornea, conjunctiva and lid margins report a lash, cold wind, onion vapour or bright glare; how the afferent signal rides the ophthalmic branch to the brainstem tear centre; and how the parasympathetic answer fires the lacrimal gland into pouring watery tears within seconds. Discover why the response is usually bilateral, why reflex tears differ from basal tears in volume and protein mix, and how emotion, bright light and even a deep yawn feed the same shared centre. Understand the purpose of the flood: dilution, flushing and enzyme-rich protection delivered exactly where the report came from. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want tear physiology explained for the ear.

Host: Picture a cold, bright morning on an exposed road. The wind picks up, grit flicks past your face, and before you have decided anything, both eyes are flooding, vision swimming, lashes dripping. Nobody trained that response. It arrived, fully formed, in seconds, because your eyes carry a fire alarm wired straight into a sprinkler system. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks. We are deep in Series 3, Ocular Physiology, and deeper still in Season 2, Tear Film and Ocular Surface Physiology. This is episode seven, Reflex Tearing: the story of the flood. Last episode our guide showed us the lacrimal gland at its day job, secreting the quiet basal trickle. Today the same gland is driven flat out. To pick up the thread, welcome back our patient guide. Frame today for us. What kind of machine have we switched on?

Guide: Thank you, and welcome to everyone joining mid-season. Last episode, Tear Secretion, introduced the gland's control circuit: a slow parasympathetic tone, adjusted gently through the day, that keeps the main lacrimal gland and its accessory glands idling at the basal rate, the trickle that maintains the tear film between blinks. That was the housekeeping setting. Today we throw the same switch fully open, and the cleanest way to tell that story is as a reflex arc. A reflex arc has five parts: a sensor that detects the problem, an afferent wire that carries the report inward, a central station that weighs the report, an efferent wire that carries the command outward, and an effector, the muscle or gland that acts. Every classic reflex you have met, from the knee jerk to the pupil's answer to light, runs on that skeleton, and the tearing reflex is no exception. So today we follow one alarm from surface to flood: the sensors on the eye's front line, the wire in, the brainstem's tear centre, the wire out, and the gland's generous reply.

Guide: Gladly. Episode one asked why we need tears at all. Episodes two through five built the film itself: its three-layer organisation, the lipid layer and its evaporation shield, the aqueous layer, and the mucins that make the surface wettable. Episode six, Tear Secretion, opened the tap and explained the basal circuit. Today, episode seven, opens the same tap in emergency mode. Host: Then let us start where the story starts, at the sensor. Where exactly do the eye's alarm sensors live, and what kind of sensor are they?

Guide: They live on the very surface you protect every day, and they are nerve endings, plain and simple. Across the cornea, the conjunctiva and the lid margins runs a dense felt-work of free nerve endings, the bare terminal branches of sensory neurons, with no capsules and no elaborate machinery, just exposed endings that change their electrical behaviour the moment the surface is threatened. Series 2 visited their supply lines in episode seven of season four, Corneal Nerves, and the detail worth recalling is the density: the cornea is among the most richly innervated surfaces in the entire body, many times more densely supplied than the skin beside it, which is why a speck of dust on the cornea is impossible to ignore. The lid margins carry their own share, guarding the doorway where the lids meet the eye, right at the edge of the tarsal plates we dissected in episode seven of season one of Series 2, Tarsal Plates and Meibomian Glands, and inside the lid layers themselves from episode six of that season, Eyelid Layers. And notice the geography: sensitivity is not spread evenly. It is richest at the centre, over the cornea, and it thins toward the outer conjunctiva, so the tissue most exposed to the world reports the loudest.

Host: Now think of four very different insults: a stray lash, a blast of cold wind, onion vapour in the kitchen, and the glare of low sun on water. One sensor listening for four things, or four sensors reporting to one desk?

Guide: Both, in a neat way. The bare endings we just met are polymodal, which means a single ending can respond to several classes of threat. There is mechanical force, a lash or a rubbing finger. There is temperature, the bite of cold wind on a winter ride. And there is chemistry, the irritant molecules in onion vapour, smoke, or the fumes that rise from a pan, which excite the same endings by a different molecular route. Each class bends the ending's membrane in its own way, but the ending's answer is the same: a burst of electrical impulses, faster and denser as the insult worsens. Bright glare is the interesting outsider. Light does not wound the surface in that moment, and its report does not begin with a nerve ending at all; it begins in the retina and reaches the tear centre by a different road. Hold that thought, because it becomes evidence later that the tear centre is a switchboard rather than a simple pain relay. For now, the headline: the surface reports mechanical, thermal and chemical trouble through one dense sensor field, and it reports brightness separately.

Host: Now the wire in. A lash touches down, an ending fires. Where does that report travel, and along what named road?

Guide: The afferent road is the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve, the first of that nerve's three great sensory divisions, and the trigeminal nerve itself is cranial nerve five, the great sensory highway of the face. We gave the whole family their introductions in episode nine of season ten of Series 2, Cranial Nerves in Ocular Function, and this is the branch that serves the eye, the orbit and the front of the nose. The impulses gathered from the cornea and conjunctiva run into that branch, pass to the trigeminal ganglion, the relay station where the sensory cell bodies sit, and from there into the brainstem. And here the reflex-arc template from episode seven of season ten, the Pupillary Pathway, pays off. That episode showed the light reflex as sensor, centre and paired efferent arms, and the same architecture again for accommodation. Tearing is built on that identical skeleton, with one glorious difference: the pupillary pathway pairs its sensory leg with a muscular answer, while ours pairs it with a secretory one. The report has now arrived at the centre. The question is what the centre looks like.

Host: Say it plainly. Is there really a tear centre, a place in the brain we could point to, or is that a convenient fiction?

Guide: It is a real place, in the sense that physiologists use the word. Deep in the brainstem, in the pons, lies a cluster of parasympathetic neurons that drives the lacrimal gland, and it is fair to call it the tear centre, exactly the way the pupillary episode let us speak of centres without pretending they are single dots on a map. What makes this centre remarkable is what converges on it. The trigeminal report from the surface arrives here. The brightness report from the retina arrives here. Emotional signals from the limbic system, the brain's feeling machinery, arrive here too. One station, many callers, one shared output decision. That convergence is not a curiosity; it is the organising idea of this whole episode. Because the centre is shared between the two eyes, an alarm in one eye floods both. Because it accepts emotional calls, feelings can open the tap with no irritant anywhere. And because it accepts calls from the retina, mere light can do it. Keep the switchboard picture, and every quirk we meet from here on becomes predictable.

Host: Then the wire out. The centre has decided to flood the eye. What does the command look like, and what road does it take to the gland?

Guide: The efferent answer travels the parasympathetic route, the same branch of the autonomic nervous system we met last episode running the basal tone. From the tear centre, fibres leave in the facial nerve, cranial nerve seven, and head for a small relay station called the pterygopalatine ganglion, tucked deep in the face, behind the nose and below the orbit. There the command changes trains: postganglionic fibres carry the message onward and upward to the lacrimal gland itself, seated in its fossa at the outer, upper corner of the orbit, the anatomy we mapped in episode one of season two of Series 2, Lacrimal Gland Anatomy. The messenger molecule is acetylcholine, landing on receptors on the gland's secretory cells, and last episode we learned what those cells do when stimulated: they pump out watery secretion, while blood flow to the gland rises to supply the raw material. Basal tone keeps that circuit murmuring. Reflex drive shouts through it. Same wires, same chemistry, wildly different volume. That, in one sentence, is the relationship between episode six and episode seven.

Host: Give me the speed and the scale honestly. A lash lands. How many heartbeats until water arrives, and how loud is the flood compared with the trickle?

Guide: Faster than you would think. The nerve legs of the arc conduct in fractions of a second, so the command reaches the gland within about a second of the insult. The gland then needs a few seconds to ramp up, and within roughly five to ten seconds the flow is obvious, tears welling and spilling toward the lid margins. As for scale, the basal rate we discussed last episode is of the order of one to two microlitres per minute in each eye, a whisper of fluid. A strong reflex drive lifts that to many tens of times the resting rate, and here is the design problem it creates: the conjunctival sac, the eye's holding space, carries only about seven microlitres of tear fluid at the best of times. The flood therefore overruns its container almost immediately, and surplus spills onto the lashes. That spilling is not a flaw; it is the system refusing to bottleneck. A surface that holds seven microlitres can be asked to receive hundreds within a minute. The plumbing downstream has a career ahead of it, and we will follow that career in episode ten.

Host: Stay inside the gland for those first seconds with me. What is actually happening in its tissue while the flood builds, and what finally ends it?

Guide: Two lovely pieces of machinery. While the parasympathetic signal lands, the gland's secretory cells shift from idle to full production, drawing water and dissolved proteins across their membranes into the gland's ducts, and the rising blood flow we mentioned delivers both the fluid and the fuel for that work. Around the acini, the secretory clusters, run basket-like contractile cells that squeeze in step with the drive, nudging the accumulating secretion down the ducts toward the openings high in the upper conjunctival fornix. Production, delivery, and a little mechanical help. As for what ends it, the reflex carries its own off-switch, and it is beautiful: the flood itself removes the trigger. Dilution and flushing drop the irritant concentration, the endings fall quiet, the reports thin out, and the centre eases its drive until output glides back toward basal. Success silences the alarm. The exception proves the rule: while the stimulus persists, cold wind that never stops, an allergen that keeps arriving, the drive persists too, and the gland keeps pace for as long as the report keeps coming.

Host: Here is the quirk everyone notices. You rinse one eye under a tap, and both eyes flood. One eye irritated, both crying. Why would the system answer a report from one eye with output from two glands?

Guide: Because the centre is one, and it is shared. There are two lacrimal glands, but a single tear centre sits on the midline of the brainstem, and its commands are dispatched down both efferent lines at once. Physiologists call the answer on the untouched side a consensual response, and you already own a mental model for it: the light reflex of episode seven of season ten made both pupils constrict when light entered one eye, for exactly the same architectural reason. Notice the fine print, though. The response is usually stronger on the irritated side, because the report from that eye is louder and the centre amplifies its own line a little more. Why build it shared at all? The design logic is superb. Most triggers of this arc are airborne or environmental: wind, vapour, smoke, glare. A threat that reaches one eye is, by default, reaching or about to reach its neighbour. Bilateral flooding means bilateral protection, delivered without waiting for the second eye to file its own complaint. The body answers the question the environment is really asking.

Host: Next surprise, and it lives in the chemistry. Is a reflex tear just a basal tear in larger quantity, or does the gland change the recipe when the alarm sounds?

Guide: It changes the recipe. The basal tears of episode six are a measured cocktail, blended to serve as the everyday lubricant: aqueous with the right load of salts, proteins and mucin to match the three-layer film design of episode two. Reflex tears are a different product. Their volume is far greater, and their make-up shifts watery: the dissolved solids per drop are fewer, the fluid is more dilute, and the protein profile changes, with a different mix of the protective proteins compared with the everyday blend. Do not hear dilute and think useless. Reflex tears still carry the armed escort, lysozyme and lactoferrin, the antibacterial enzymes and iron-binders of the basal film, plus secretory immunoglobulin A, the surface's own antibody, all swept across the eye in bulk. The two products suit two jobs. The basal tear is the everyday lubricant: measured, layered, frugal. The reflex tear is the emergency flush: delivered fast, delivered in bulk, optimised to cover the whole surface quickly and to carry the protective proteins with it. One is a fountain pen; the other is a fire hose. Both come from the same gland, at two different volumes of the same switch.

Host: You say the flood comes from the main gland. Where do the smaller accessory glands sit in all of this? Are they part of the emergency?

Guide: Only as quiet bystanders, and that division of labour is worth pinning down. The accessory glands, the small secretory clusters embedded in the conjunctiva and the upper lid, which Series 2 met in episode three of season two, Accessory Lacrimal Glands, are steady workers built for the basal film: modest, constant, always on. They keep their housekeeping contribution running beneath the flood, but the emergency output is the main gland's act, delivered through its ducts high in the upper fornix, which is precisely why the flood needs the blink to spread it. So the picture is tiered. Basal secretion is a committee of small glands and a murmuring main gland. The reflex flood is the main gland solo, at full voice, with the committee continuing quietly underneath. When we speak of reflex tears in the clinical sense, we mean the main gland's answer, and everything we have said about volume, speed and recipe belongs to it.

Host: Which brings us to the trigger nobody files under irritation at all. Grief, laughter, a piece of music. Why does the emotional brain get to use the eye's fire alarm?

Guide: Because it is wired straight into the same switchboard. Signals from the limbic system, the deep network that generates and colours feeling, project to the same tear centre that the trigeminal report reaches. The centre does not really know who is calling; it knows that the combined drive has crossed threshold, and it opens the flood. That is why feelings and irritants share one tap. It also explains the shared output: emotional tears arrive bilaterally, they spill the same way, and they carry their own chemical signature, richer again in certain proteins and stress-related messengers than either the basal or the irritant flood. There is something elegant in the arrangement. The surface's alarm and the mind's alarm converge on one effector, so the body answers both kinds of distress with the same generous response: fluid, protection, and a visible signal to others that something is wrong. One more caller deserves mention, the one looking down from above. Cortical influence reaches the centre too, which is why a determined person can partly hold an emotional flood back, though rarely for long and never the irritant flood at all. However evolution assembled the plumbing, the lesson for the arc is firm: the tear centre is a convergence point, and convergence is why the reflex list is far longer than any single trigger deserves.

Host: Two more strange callers at this switchboard, and I want to test your picture with them. Bright light makes eyes water with no injury at all. And a huge yawn, with the stretch that comes with it, can leave you dabbing your eyes. Same circuit?

Guide: Same circuit, two different entries. Light we have already promised: the retina reports brightness upward through the visual machinery, a branch of that report reaches the tear centre, and the centre responds by raising gland output. That is why stepping from shade into low, glaring sun waters the eyes, and why glare compounds the flood when the surface is already irritated: the two calls sum at the centre. The yawn is my favourite. A deep yawn with a full stretch is one of the most powerful activators of the parasympathetic machinery we carry, the squeezing contraction of the facial and jaw muscles adds mechanical pressure around the gland's neighbourhood, and the brainstem's shared wiring between respiration, facial movement and the autonomic centres does the rest. The result is watery eyes at the tail end of every satisfying yawn. Neither trigger injures the surface. Both prove the central point: the tear centre is a converging switchboard with many callers, and the gland obeys whoever shouts loudest. The arc's sensors are the beginning of the story, not the whole of it.

Host: So the flood arrives: fast, bilateral, watery, enzyme-carrying. Before we close the loop, tell me plainly what it is for. What does a flood accomplish that the basal film cannot?

Guide: Three jobs, in ascending drama. First, dilution. Whatever landed on the surface, vapour, salt spray, the residue of a grit particle, is thinned out when a large volume of fluid spreads over it, dropping the concentration that is irritating the endings. Second, flushing. Bulk flow moves, and the flood creates real current across the ocular surface, sweeping the offending material toward the inner corner and the drainage openings, while the lids and the blink pump the mixture along; how that spreading works is episode eight's whole subject. Third, enzyme-rich protection. The flood carries lysozyme, lactoferrin and immunoglobulin A across the whole eye in bulk, reinforcing the surface's antibacterial shield precisely when the barrier has been challenged. And notice the precision of the delivery. The report came from a specific patch of surface, and the gland's output, picked up by the blink and spread by the lid margins, is delivered back to that same patch within seconds. The system does not merely shout; it answers the exact address the complaint came from. Dilution, flushing, and enzymatic reinforcement, arriving where and when they are needed, all inside the opening seconds. That is the point of the flood.

Host: Let us bring it to the chair, because our listeners will watch this reflex running every working day. Without stepping outside normal function, what does an optometrist gain from knowing this arc cold?

Guide: Three habits of mind. The first is to read watering as a report rather than a nuisance: when an eye floods at the slit lamp, the arc is telling you the surface has filed a complaint, and the reflex is working exactly as designed. The second is to think in stimulus classes: mechanical, thermal, chemical, light, emotion. Asking which class fits the moment turns a vague symptom into a specific question about the surface. The third is to respect the arc as a testable circuit. Reflex output can be measured: when a stimulating agent touches the deeper nasal mucosa, the trigeminal call goes out, and the wetting of test strips in both eyes shows the efferent machinery answering; that is the principle of stimulated tear measurement, the functional cousin of the basal secretion tests coming later in this series. And one last practical note: because the centre is shared, what you observe at one eye carries information about the other. One circuit, two outputs. Every flood is a readout of the whole arc.

Host: Then recap it the way we would want it said back to us in an oral examination. The whole arc, from lash to flood, in order, with the names attached.

Guide: With pleasure. Sensors: free trigeminal nerve endings across the cornea, conjunctiva and lid margins, densest over the cornea, polymodal, reporting mechanical, thermal and chemical threat. Afferent leg: the impulses ride the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve, cranial nerve five, through the trigeminal ganglion into the brainstem. Centre: the tear centre of the pons, a shared midline switchboard that also receives retinal brightness reports and limbic emotional input. Efferent leg: parasympathetic fibres in the facial nerve, cranial nerve seven, relaying at the pterygopalatine ganglion to reach the lacrimal gland. Effector: the gland's secretory cells pour watery secretion, reaching many tens of times basal flow within seconds, bilaterally, stronger on the irritated side. The product is an emergency flush: watery, more dilute, yet carrying lysozyme, lactoferrin and secretory immunoglobulin A, delivered by the blink to the very surface that complained. And the callers at the centre include feeling, light and the yawn. That is the reflex, end to end.

Host: One last question, the one we always save. Across all of this, which detail do students most often get wrong or underestimate?

Guide: The switchboard. Students learn the arc as a straight line, lash in, tears out, and then meet bilateral flooding, tears in bright light, tears in grief, and tears with a yawn, and they file each as a separate fact to memorise. They are one fact. Every quirk of this reflex falls out of the convergence at the shared centre: two eyes, one station; feeling, light and stretch, all callers at the same desk; one efferent chemistry played at different volumes. Hold the switchboard and the reflex loses its surprises. The other underweighted detail is the recipe: reflex tears are not merely more tears, they are a different product with a different job, the emergency flush rather than the everyday lubricant. A student who can say why the flood is dilute, bilateral, fast and enzyme-carrying, all from one picture of the centre, owns this episode outright.
