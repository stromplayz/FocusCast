# Description: Every moment your eyes are open, a spotlight inside the visual system decides what gets seen and what gets ignored, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks teaches how that spotlight works. Learn the two modes of visual attention: overt attention, where the fovea physically moves to what matters, and covert attention, where the mind's spotlight shifts while the eyes hold perfectly still. Hear what attention changes in the brain, as responses to attended locations grow while unattended competitors are quieted, and meet the two steering forces: bottom-up capture by motion, contrast and novelty, and top-down guidance by your current goals. The episode faces the limit honestly, with the famous counting demonstration in which people focused on one task miss a person in a gorilla suit strolling through the scene. Made for optometry students, practising optometrists and curious listeners who want attention physiology explained for the ear.

Host: While you have been listening to this opening sentence, your eyes have been making small precise jumps, your ears have been pulling one voice out of the room's background, and somewhere behind all of it a gatekeeper has been deciding what deserves the rest of your mind and what does not. That gatekeeper is tonight's subject. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology. We are well into Season 10, Visual Adaptation and Integration, the final season of the series. Episode one taught the pupillary light reflex, the iris answering the light in fractions of a second. Episode two walked the near response, the trio of focusing, converging and pupil constriction that serves all close work. Episode three took you into the dark with Dark Adaptation, and episode four brought you back into the glare with Light Adaptation. Both of those were stories of gain: the visual system turning its own sensitivity up and down to match the brightness of the world. Tonight we meet the same kind of lever pulled by a different hand. This is episode five, Visual Attention. To guide us, our patient teacher is back. Set the frame for us. What is attention, in the physiology of this series, and why does seeing need it at all?

Guide: Thank you, good to be back. Start with the flood, because the flood is the whole reason. The optic nerve leaves each eye carrying roughly a million fibres, and the scene they report is rich beyond any use: every colour, edge, texture, motion and flicker in view, all at once, all demanding to be finished. The cortex that must turn that report into sight works on a budget. Finishing is not free. Resolving detail, holding shapes steady, binding features into objects, all of it costs processing that cannot be spent everywhere at once. So the system runs a selection, and that selection is what we call attention: the set of processes that choose a small part of the incoming scene for full treatment and hold the rest back as unpolished background. Notice the two decisions hiding inside that sentence, because they organize the whole night. Attention decides where the fovea goes next, which patch of the world earns the high-resolution look. And attention decides what the cortex bothers to finish, which signals get built into the shapes and events you will actually experience. Everything else arrives and waits, or arrives and quietly fades. Our rule tonight is the series rule: normal function only, no pathology. And our lane is selection itself. What the selected material is finally built into belongs to episode six, Visual Perception, and we will walk right up to that door and stop.

Host: You named two decisions, and I want to pull them apart, because most people quietly assume that attention and eye movement are one single event. Sometimes they nearly are, and sometimes they are not even in the same postcode. Give us the modes. What is overt attention, and how does it relate to the moving eye?

Guide: Overt attention is attention you can see from the outside: the fovea physically travels to whatever matters. The jumping machinery that does the travelling is the saccade system, and we built it piece by piece in Season 9, episode ten, Coordinated Eye Movements: the ballistic jumps, the tiny drifts and tremors that fill the gaps between them, the three-layer hierarchy of cortex, brainstem and cerebellum that chooses targets and executes the flight. Tonight the interesting part is not the flight itself but the order of operations, and the order is genuinely surprising. In laboratory recordings, attention arrives at the destination slightly before the eyes do. The spotlight shifts first; the jump follows it. While viewing a picture or a page, a person makes about three such jumps every second, thousands upon thousands in a waking day, and every one of them is a physical vote about what mattered in the scene. So overt attention is the visible half of tonight's story: the eye acting as a courier, delivering the fovea to addresses the mind has already flagged. Between the jumps the eyes rest on each spot for a fraction of a second, and it is during those rests that the cortex gathers its harvest. The moving eye is not the cause of attention. It is closer to its hand.

Host: The hand. And the mind itself can reach without the hand, cannot it? At a busy gathering you can hold your gaze steadily on one friend's face and still catch your own name drifting across the room from a completely different conversation. Does vision have that same trick, attending without moving?

Guide: It does, and the trick has a name: covert attention. Covert means hidden, and the hiding is from the outside. The eyes hold still, locked on their fixation point, and the mental spotlight is thrown somewhere else entirely: out to the edge of view, down a hallway, toward a doorway you are not looking at. The hearing example you gave is the famous one, one voice chosen out of a din, and vision runs the very same trick on space. The classic laboratory game makes it measurable. A person stares at a small cross in the middle of a screen. A brief flash appears for a moment on one side, and then a target appears, sometimes exactly where the flash marked, sometimes elsewhere. When the flash told the truth, responses to the target are faster and surer, even though the eyes never moved a hair's width. When the flash lied, responses are slower. Nothing about the eyes changed at all. The change was pure allocation, attention alone, and it is worth tens of milliseconds, which in reaction time is a large sum. And notice what this means for the empire of the fovea. The fovea is the only region that resolves fine detail; the periphery, built from broadly pooled wiring as Series 2, season 8, episode four, Photoreceptor Distribution explained, is coarse by design. Covert attention does not sharpen the periphery into a second fovea. It changes priorities: at an attended edge you detect sooner and judge faster, on a picture that remains blurry.

Host: A spotlight that moves without a courier. That raises the question I most want answered tonight. Attention is a mental act, a choice. What is it physically, as far as the visual system is concerned? When the spotlight shifts, what actually changes in the brain?

Guide: The core finding is one of the most elegant in all of visual science, and it can be said in one sentence: attention changes the gain of the neurons that are already looking. Every location in view is reported by its own set of cortical neurons, the ones whose receptive fields cover that particular spot. Attend to a location, and those neurons respond more vigorously to whatever appears there: firing harder, with sharper contrast between what they prefer and what they do not. The circuitry did not change. The same cells, the same stimuli, a louder report. And the story has a second half, because attention does not only raise the chosen. It quiets the competitors. Where two stimuli fall inside one neuron's field, the attended one wins and the response to the ignored one shrinks, so the winner stands out against a deliberately lowered floor. Selection is loudness at one address and hush at the neighbours, together. Now think back across this season, to episodes three and four. Dark Adaptation and Light Adaptation were gain changes imposed from outside, the receptors re-scaling themselves to match the recent history of the light. Attention is the same lever pulled from the inside: gain set not by how bright the world has been, but by what you intend to do with it. Adaptation by exposure, attention by will. That parallel is the deepest thing tonight has to teach. The machinery of emphasis was already built into the system, and attention is the mind's own hand upon it.

Host: Before we go further, tell me about the size of that spotlight. Does it light a pinhead or a room? Can you carry two spotlights at once, or spread one beam thin across everything?

Guide: Its size flexes, and the flexing has a price attached. Attend narrowly, to one letter or one door handle, and the processing there is at its finest and most detailed. Widen the beam to cover a whole shape or a region of the scene, and each location inside it gets somewhat less: the zoom-lens bargain, detail traded against breadth, and both settings are useful, which is why the system offers both. The beam can even be split, imperfectly. People can monitor two separate patches of space at the same time, but both reports then run shallower than one would, and a third location slips through entirely, unmonitored. What nobody does well is flood everything. Equal-priority, full-field processing is simply not one of the options this lamp offers. There is no setting on it called everywhere, and that absence is not a flaw in you. It is the design, running exactly as built.

Host: So the spotlight has shape, size and a budget. Now, what moves it? I notice that most of the time I do not feel like I am choosing at all. A door slams, something moves at the edge of vision, and my eyes are already halfway there. Where does that yank come from?

Guide: That yank is the first of two steering forces, and it is the older of the two: bottom-up capture. Signals that mark sudden change or strong difference seize attention without your consent, and the tripwire that fires the alarm is the periphery we constructed in Season 7, episode seven, Motion Detection. Remember that division of labour. The fovea owns detail, the periphery owns surveillance, and the rod-rich, motion-tuned edge of the retina reports anything that moves, appears or flickers. Bottom-up capture is what that report does next. Sudden onset, fast motion, high contrast against the surround, novelty, anything that breaks the recent pattern of the scene, all of it enters a kind of running competition among locations, and the strongest challenger wins the eyes, typically within about a fifth of a second. The cyclist who slides into the edge of your view claims your gaze before you have formed a single thought about cyclists. The moth crossing the lamp pulls your eyes off the page mid-sentence. You did not decide anything. You were captured, and capture is exactly the right word, because the choice was made by the scene, not by you. This is the reflex that kept the fovea fed for millions of years before anyone planned anything.

Host: The scene choosing for you. And yet, just as clearly, the scene does not always win. Walk into a bookshop hunting for the psychology shelf, and you drift past hundreds of shouting spines without a single flicker. What is the second force, and how does a goal that lives inside your head steer a spotlight that operates out in the world?

Guide: The second force is top-down guidance, and it runs on expectation instead of salience. Set a goal, and the system writes a template: a brief of the features you are hunting for, held ready in the mind. From that moment, every glance and every fixation is checked against the brief, and matching features get a head start wherever they occur. Look for your friend in a crowd, and tall silhouettes, her red scarf, the particular way she leans when she laughs, all rise above brighter and more startling things around them. In laboratory searches the same law shows itself cleanly. When a target differs from the clutter by one feature, a red item among green ones, a vertical bar among horizontals, it seems to pop out, and search stays fast even in a crowded scene, because every location advertises its features all at once and the odd one simply announces itself. When the target is defined by a combination, a red vertical bar hiding among red horizontals and green verticals, no single feature gives it away, and attention must visit the candidates one by one. Search slows as the clutter grows. That slow, visiting kind of search is attention working at its most deliberate: template in hand, spotlight inspecting, a shortlist drawn and checked. And here is the picture to carry out of tonight. Bottom-up capture is the shout from the periphery. Top-down guidance is the quiet instruction from the plan. Normal seeing is the conversation between them, argued moment by moment across every scene you will ever inspect.

Host: A conversation between a shout and a plan. One more property, then, because the blue search demo is coming later and I want the groundwork laid. So far the spotlight has landed on places. But searching for something blue feels like it lights up a colour everywhere at once. Can attention select features, not just spots?

Guide: It can, and that second aiming mode explains the demo before we run it. The spotlight can be steered by location, one region of space raised above the rest, or by property, one attribute raised across the whole field. Attend to red, and red things all over the view gain a little strength, a little speed, wherever they sit. Attend to movement, and every moving thing in the scene reports more loudly. Feature-based attention is how the one-feature search feels like magic: you are not sweeping a beam from object to object, you have simply tuned the whole system to a frequency, and matching items sing above the noise of the rest. Combine the two modes and you have the full steering console of the mind: here or red, this patch or that property, or both at once. The template, remember, is written by your goal. The console has an operator.

Host: Let us bring all of this to the chair now, because our listeners live in examination rooms, and I suspect those rooms are quietly full of attention experiments that nobody calls by that name. Where does tonight's physiology show up on an ordinary working day?

Guide: In four places you already visit. First, the wiggle finger. When you bring a wiggling finger in from the side during a confrontation check, you are doing two things at once: testing the peripheral tripwire we built in Motion Detection, and testing covert attention, because you ask the patient to keep fixating straight ahead and report what arrived at the edge. Detection out there depends on both the wiring and the allocation, the hardware and where the spotlight happens to sit. Second, the scanning routine. Every disciplined reviewing pattern a student is taught, quadrant by quadrant in a fixed order, for viewing a fundus or a field, is trained overt attention. The routine exists because free viewing is capture-driven, and capture is content to visit whatever shouts loudest while the quiet but important corner goes unvisited. The grid is a top-down template imposed on a bottom-up machine, so that nothing is skipped just because nothing about it shouted. Third, the history. In Series 1, season 8, episode two, Asking the Right Questions, we learned that a good history is steered by the questions you carry into it. That is top-down attention applied to listening: the clinician's template decides which thread in a tangled story gets pulled and finished, and which waits politely in the background. And fourth, the patient who reports that the car came from nowhere. From nowhere, almost always, means outside the spotlight. The retina reported it faithfully. The gatekeeper did not finish it. Four everyday scenes, one physiology.

Host: That last patient brings us to the hard part of tonight's brief, and I want to take it slowly, because it deserves a certain respect. Before the recap, before anything else: tell them about the gorilla.

Guide: The experiment runs like this. A group of people in white shirts and black shirts pass a basketball back and forth, weaving and faking, and viewers are given one demanding job: count the passes made by one team only. It takes real concentration, because the passes come fast. Partway through the action, a person in a full gorilla suit strolls into the middle of the scene, stops, turns to face the camera, thumps its chest, and walks off again. It is plainly visible for several seconds. When viewers are asked afterwards whether they noticed anything unusual, about half of them say no. Not that they saw the gorilla and forgot it. That, as far as their experience goes, there was no gorilla. The suit entered their eyes. The light reached their retinas and travelled up the visual pathways exactly as it should. Nothing at all was wrong with any of it. The counting task had the spotlight, all of it, and the gorilla fell outside the beam. The finding has a name: inattentional blindness, the failure to notice a fully visible event when attention is committed somewhere else. And hear the moral precisely, because this is a demonstration about completely normal people, not a defect and not a diagnosis. Seeing, in the optical sense, is not the same as noticing. A busy road, a tight counting task, an absorbing phone conversation: whenever one job holds the spotlight, the rest of the scene can ship its entire report and still never be finished into awareness. That is the narrowness of the resource, demonstrated honestly, in plain sight, in broad daylight.

Host: Seeing is not the same as noticing. I want to sit with that for a moment, because it reverses something deep in how we talk about the eye. Before we gather the night, give me the misconceptions. What do students most often get wrong about attention, and what should replace each error?

Guide: The first error is the video recorder: the belief that the eye tapes the whole scene, continuously, and attention is merely where you later choose to replay the tape. There is no tape. Selection is not a preference laid over a complete record; it is a stage in the making of sight itself, and whatever falls outside it is never finished in the first place. The gorilla was never taped and missed. It was never built. The second error is the equality of looking and attending: the assumption that the spotlight lives at the fovea and only there, so that wherever the eyes point, the mind must be. Covert attention is the standing proof otherwise. Gaze and spotlight can part company for hundreds of milliseconds at a time, and they do so constantly in normal viewing, the eyes parked on one place while the mind works another.

Host: Two more, quickly.

Guide: The third error is the one-way spotlight: the idea that attention only brightens, turning a lamp up at one address and doing nothing anywhere else. Tonight's physiology says the quieting is half of the mechanism. Without the suppressed competitors, without the lowered floor, the raised winner would hardly stand out at all. Selectivity is amplification and hush operating together. The fourth error is attention as an unlimited will: the sense that a determined person can simply decide to attend to everything, for as long as they like, if they try hard enough. The gorilla settles that. The resource is narrow, it fatigues with sustained use, and it must be re-aimed and refreshed from second to second. Respecting that narrowness, in your patients and in yourself, is not a footnote to the physiology. It is the physiology.

Host: Let us make it personal before the recap. Give the listeners demonstrations they can run tonight, at home, with no equipment at all.

Guide: Three demonstrations, one for each big idea. First, covert attention. Fix your eyes on the spine of a book across the room, and without letting the eyes move one hair, throw the spotlight to the far left edge of your view and take inventory of what is there: a doorframe, a lamp, a patch of colour. Then, still fixated, throw it to the right edge and take that inventory too. You have just allocated attention twice without a single saccade, and you can feel the shift as an event, almost like a small movement that never reached the eyes. Second, bottom-up capture. Sit still with a page and simply wait. The screen flicker at the corner of the desk, a curtain stirring, a fly walking the window: each one will yank your eyes before any decision of yours, straight out of the peripheral tripwire. Third, top-down guidance. Scan the room for everything blue, and watch how blue things step forward out of clutter you had been ignoring completely, as if a lamp had come up behind them. Now search for something that is blue and round, and feel the search slow, because the template now demands a combination, and the spotlight must travel and inspect candidates one at a time. Two forces and a spotlight, demonstrated across one quiet evening.

Host: It is a strong list, and every line of it earns its place. One last question before the tease, the one we always ask. Across everything tonight, which single detail do you find students underestimate the most?

Guide: The lead time. People assume the eyes move first and attention follows, a courier setting out and the interest catching up later at leisure. The evidence says the reverse. The spotlight shifts to the destination first, by a fraction of a second, and the saccade lands into attention already waiting there, which is why the world never seems to jump at the moment the eyes arrive. On that view, covert attention is not a curiosity standing beside the main act of looking. It is the rehearsal that every glance performs. Learn to read attention and you can often predict the next saccade before it flies; learn the machinery of the saccade and you have learned the vehicle that attention most often rides. Season 9's story and tonight's are one story with a pause between the pages: the mind chooses, the spotlight moves, the eye follows, and the cortex finishes what was chosen.
