Host: Let me start with the puzzle that has bothered me since school. I close my left eye, and the world is still there. I close my right eye, and the world is still there. Open both, and I do not see two superimposed worlds, fainter or busier. I see one world. Each retina is a separate screen receiving its own image, yet nothing in my experience feels doubled. Why does the brain not charge me double?

Guide: Because the brain never asks where the light landed on the retina. It asks which direction the light came from. Think of every retinal location as carrying a label, a felt direction, a place in space it stands for. Touch one spot of the retina with light, by any means at all, and you experience brightness out there, in a specific direction, not in here on the back of the eye. Every point on every retina is a signpost pointing somewhere out in the world. And here is the hinge of the whole episode: the two signposts, one in each eye, can point the same way. A location in the left eye and a location in the right eye can carry the same directional label. When light lands on both of them at once, the brain reads two reports with the same address and files them as a single place. Two landings, one direction, one seen spot. That is why the world does not double. Most of what you see in any given moment lands on pairs of locations that were built to share a direction, and the behaviour itself, one world from two eyes, is the everyday tour we took in Series 1, in episode six of season three, Binocular Vision. Tonight we open the geometry that explains it.

Host: Pairs of locations. That is the phrase the episode title is built on, so let us have it slowly and exactly. What is a corresponding point?

Guide: A corresponding point is one member of a pair of retinal locations, one on each retina, that share the same felt visual direction. Read that as a relationship, not a property. No spot on the left retina is corresponding all by itself; it corresponds with one specific partner on the right retina, the partner that points to the same direction in space. Now add light. When an object sends light into both eyes and that light lands on both members of one pair, the brain hears the same address twice and sees one object in one place. When light lands on locations that are not partners, the two reports carry different directional labels, and the brain holds raw material for a different verdict, which we will reach in a few minutes. Notice what this pairing does to the two images. It stitches them into one coordinate system. The two retinal maps stop being two separate screens and become, in effect, one twinned map, every location partnered across the midline. All of binocular geometry, everything from tonight onward, is bookkeeping on that twinned map.

Host: The twinned map has to start somewhere. Which locations are the founding pair, the ones that pair with each other before any other pairing means anything?

Guide: The two foveas. Each retina has one fovea, the packed little centre where vision is sharpest, and the two foveas are corresponding to each other. Their shared label is the straight-ahead direction. This is not a small technical detail; it is the anchor of the whole system. Because the foveas share a direction, the eyes can aim at a single target and both centres of sharpest vision receive it at once, and the brain registers one target, dead ahead, where the two directions meet. That is what fixation is, in this language: the act of placing the image of one chosen object on both foveas at the same time. Whatever lands on both foveas together is seen singly, straight ahead. And notice the quiet consequence. The straight-ahead direction is the one direction the two eyes can share only by aiming together, so every act of fixation is also a statement of correspondence. The founding pair declares itself every time you look at anything, which means it has declared itself thousands of times before you finished this sentence.

Host: So the mug I am looking at: its image sits on both foveas, single and straight ahead. But the room is not one mug. There is a table under it, a window behind it, shelves at every distance, a chair just past the edge of the table. Where do all the other things fall?

Guide: That question has a name for its answer, and the name is the horopter. At any moment, with the eyes fixed on a chosen target, there is a particular set of places in the world whose images land on corresponding pairs, the founding pair included. Every point of that set pays the brain in the same currency: light on partners, zero mismatch, one direction, one seen place. The horopter is that set, the surface of zero difference, and at any given fixation distance its near region bends close to a circle that passes through the fixated point and through the centres of the two eyes. I will leave the curve at that one sentence, because tonight the shape matters far less than the role. The horopter is the row of seats where the twinned map and the world agree perfectly: the collection of places seen with no disparity at all, given where you are looking right now. Everything on it is single, matched, and silent in the best sense, because it gives the measuring machinery nothing to complain about.

Host: You said given where you are looking right now. I want to press on that, because it sounds as if the horopter is not a fixed feature of my room at all.

Guide: It is not, and that is the most surprising thing about it. The horopter belongs to the observer, not to the room. The correspondence is fixed in the wiring, but the eyes aim, and every change of aim carries the zero-difference surface to a new set of places. Look at the mug, and the horopter wraps the mug's neighbourhood; the surface of the mug near its centre is seen with matched images. Now shift your gaze to the window behind it, and in that instant the surface lifts off the mug and settles along the window's neighbourhood. Nothing in your wiring changed. Only the aim moved, and the surface of perfect matching swept through the room like a beam of agreement. So a horopter is always a horopter for a fixation: tied to the distance and direction of the target you have chosen this moment. The world never moves it. You do, several times a second, all day long, without ever noticing the machinery turning.

Host: And the objects that are left out. The mug is honoured while I look at it, the window is honoured while I look at it, but at any given moment almost everything I can see is off that surface. What happens to their images?

Guide: They land on non-corresponding points, and this is where the episode turns from tidy geometry into one of the great ideas in physiology. Take an object nearer than your fixated target, say a spoon lying in front of the mug while you look at the mug. Its images cannot fall on partners in the two eyes, because no single pair of locations points at both the mug and the spoon at once. The spoon's two images land on spots that are not paired, and the brain receives two directional reports that disagree slightly. That disagreement has a name: disparity, the mismatch between where a pair of images actually falls and where the corresponding pair would have received them. And here is the move that makes two eyes worth having. The brain does not discard the mismatch as noise. It measures it.

Host: Measures it. Say exactly what that means, because this is the payoff you promised at the top of the episode.

Guide: It means the disparity is read, and the reading is depth. Two facts travel together in that measurement. First, the sign of the mismatch, which way the images were pushed, tells the brain whether the object sat nearer than the fixated surface or farther. In plain words: an object nearer than the target pushes its two images toward the ear-side halves of the retinas, an object farther away pulls them toward the nose-side halves, and the brain reads those opposite pushes as in front of and behind. Second, the size of the mismatch grows with how far off the surface the object sits, so the very same measurement reports how much nearer or how much farther. That is stereoscopic disparity in action: the difference between two flat pictures, converted into a distance in the world. No ruler, no shadow, no guesswork from size. A pure geometric fact about where light landed on two curved screens, decoded into the solidity of everything you have ever seen. This is the machinery underneath the tour we took in Series 1, in episode seven of season three, Depth Perception, and it is the payoff promised back in episode one of this season, now earned in full.

Host: Let me test the limits of that reading with a demonstration anyone can do, and I will do it now while you listen. I hold up one finger, a hand's width from my nose, and behind it I pick a door handle across the room. When I look hard at the finger, the finger is single, but the door handle seems to split into two. When I switch and lock onto the handle, the handle is single and the finger splits into two. Nothing moved except my aim. Explain what just happened, and reassure everyone that they have not broken anything.

Guide: Nothing is broken, and in fact nothing unusual happened at all. That doubling is called physiological diplopia, and the word physiological is doing quiet work in the name: it belongs to normal vision, and it runs in the background of your seeing all day, every day. The explanation is everything we have built so far. When you fixate the finger, the finger's images sit on the two foveas, the founding pair, single and straight ahead. The far handle lies well off the horopter for that fixation, so its images land on retinal locations that are not partners, and the mismatch is far too large for the depth reading to fold it into one place. The two maps report separately, and the handle is seen twice, once from each. Switch your gaze to the handle and the roles swap: handle on the founding pair, single; finger far off the surface, doubled. You have just seen your own horopter, from the inside, twice in five seconds. The doubling is not a fault to fix. It is the geometry of correspondence showing itself whenever an object strays too far from the matched surface.

Host: And the too large in that answer is doing some work, I suspect. Small mismatches become depth, enormous mismatches become this doubling. Where is the line, and who owns the story of it?

Guide: The line belongs to the next episode, and I will respect the fence. Here is what can be said from tonight's side of it. The disparity reading is astonishingly fine for small mismatches: a difference between the two images far smaller than the smallest gap your one eye could resolve as visible detail is still decoded as depth, which is why two eyes judge the relief on a surface long before one eye can see the texture that would explain it. As the mismatch grows, the reading stays usable for a while, and then, as you just saw, it gives up on holding the world single, and the two maps speak separately. What the visual system does along that border, how it trades, how it keeps a whole room of objects at different distances stitched into one scene, is the story of fusion, and it begins in episode four. Tonight you only need the geometry: matched on the surface, measured off it, and reported separately when the measurement would be a shout instead of a whisper.

Host: Then let me ask the stability question, because it has been forming since the founding pair. The eyes aim all day, and the horopter sweeps accordingly. But the pairing itself, the twinned map: does it change? Does it drift when I am tired, when I grow, when I spend a year reading small print?

Guide: No, and the steadiness is the quiet miracle of the system. Correspondence is hardware. The pairing between the two retinal maps is set early in life and, once settled, it holds for decades without maintenance. The wiring does not re-pair itself when you are tired, and it does not drift across the years, and that is precisely what makes the disparity reading trustworthy. Think about what the brain is doing with every glance: comparing two images against a fixed table of partnerships. If the table changed from day to day, yesterday's depth would not agree with today's. If it drifted, the world would wobble in depth with every hour and every mood. Instead the table is carved once, early, and every measurement the brain ever makes is made against the same standard. The eyes' aim moves the horopter through the world with every fixation change; the correspondence underneath never moves at all. A moving probe, mounted on an unchanging scale: that is the design.

Host: Set early in life. Say a little more about that, because listeners will wonder whether the pairing is present at birth or whether it is built by looking.

Guide: The honest answer is that it is both, in sequence, and the sequence explains how stable the result is. The wiring plan that lets signals from the two eyes meet and be compared is present from the start, a scaffold laid down before experience begins. The fine calibration of the pairing is tuned across the early months and years of looking, as the two eyes steadily report on the same world and the connections settle into agreement with what the two maps actually receive. After that settling, the system is essentially fixed, and it is fixed on purpose, because a depth measuring instrument is only useful if its standard never changes. We will not walk the development territory further tonight; this season has plenty of geometry of its own to carry. The single sentence to keep is this: correspondence is calibrated once, early, and then trusted for life.

Host: One more piece of the machinery, and it is the piece I always trip over. The retinas report where light landed. The comparison you keep describing, the checking of one landing against the other: where does that physically happen? Not on the retina itself, surely, since each retina sees only its own image.

Guide: Correct, and it is the right trip to make. The comparison happens in the brain, at and beyond the first station where signals from the two eyes meet. We followed the retinal output on its way out of the eye in Season 7, episode ten, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal; tonight's story begins where two such outputs arrive together. Deeper in the visual pathways there are nerve cells that listen to both eyes at once, wired so that each one receives from a corresponding pair of locations. Such a cell is, in effect, a living embodiment of tonight's definition: it answers when its matched pair is driven together, and it responds differently when the two landings miss the partnership by a little or a lot. The retina hands the brain two signed maps; the brain owns the atlas of partnerships and does the measuring. So when we say the brain reads disparity, we mean it literally: specific cells are the measuring instruments, and their verdicts are the raw material for the depth you experience.

Host: Let us take this to the chair, because our listeners are optometry students and practising optometrists. Why does correspondence-level geometry change what happens in a consulting room on an ordinary Tuesday?

Guide: Because a large share of binocular testing is this episode turned into procedure. When you assess binocular vision, you are asking tonight's questions with instruments: do the two maps sit in correspondence, where in the field does single vision hold, how large a mismatch can this patient's system still read as depth, and how steady is the whole arrangement. The tests that examine seeing with the two eyes open, the ones catalogued for the patient journey in Series 1, episode six of season nine, Binocular Assessment, are all, at bottom, conversations with the twinned map. And the classic stereopsis targets, the pictures that yield depth only when the two eyes' images disagree by a controlled amount, are nothing more than disparity presented on paper: a manufactured mismatch, fed to the measuring machinery, with the patient's answer grading the reading. When a patient reports that a test target is single, or doubled, or standing out from the page, they are reporting the state of their correspondence geometry. That is the chairside value of tonight. Behind every binocular result stands the geometry we just walked, and the results stop being arbitrary once you can see the geometry underneath them.

Host: Before we close, do the misconception sweep. Three or four ideas that sound right and are not.

Guide: With pleasure, because this topic collects them. First misconception: corresponding points are mirror twins, the same spot on each retina, like two identical photographs laid side by side. They are not, and the light crossing inside each eye is the reason. A given direction in the world is served by the ear-side half of one retinal map and the nose-side half of the other, depending on where the direction sits, so the partnership runs contrary to intuition. Correspondence is defined by shared direction, never by matching position on the two maps. Second misconception: disparity is an error, a small optical failure the brain tolerates. It is the opposite. The mismatch is the signal, generated by the geometry of the world and measured on purpose; a visual system with zero disparity everywhere would be a system with no stereoscopic depth at all. Third misconception: the horopter is a fixed wall or ribbon painted into the room, a place you could walk up to and touch. It is a property of the observer, rebuilt with every fixation, and it follows your aim the way your shadow follows your body. And a fourth, quieter one: that all this geometry is delicate. It is not. It is among the most stable constructions in biology, which is exactly why it can be trusted as a measuring standard.

Host: Good. Now the oral examination. Give me the episode back in one answer, the way you would want a student to recite it at the viva.

Guide: Corresponding points are pairs of retinal locations, one on each retina, that share the same felt visual direction, so light landing on both members of a pair is seen as coming from one place. The two foveas are the founding pair, sharing the straight-ahead direction, and aiming them together at a target is fixation. The horopter is the set of world points whose images land on corresponding pairs at a given fixation distance, the surface of zero difference, whose near region bends close to a circle through the fixated point and the two eyes' centres. Objects off that surface land on non-corresponding points, and the resulting mismatch is disparity, which the brain measures and reads as depth, its sign saying nearer or farther and its size saying how much. The reading is fine enough to decode mismatches smaller than one eye can resolve, and mismatches beyond its range appear as the normal doubling you can demonstrate with a finger and a door handle. Correspondence is fixed hardware, set early in life and steady thereafter, while the eyes' aim moves the horopter through the world with every fixation change. And the measuring itself happens in the brain, in cells that receive from both eyes at once. That is the episode.

Host: Last reflective question, the one we always save for the detail people. Across correspondence, horopter and disparity, which idea do you find students and even experienced clinicians underestimate the most?

Guide: That disparity has no absolute meaning. Students file it as a property of objects, as if each thing in the world carried its own fixed disparity the way it carries its colour. It does not. Disparity is a comparison against the current fixation, and the same object changes its disparity every time you change your gaze, though nothing about the object moves. The lamp on your desk has one disparity while you look at the page you are writing and a different one, a moment later, while you look at the lamp itself, and at the instant you fixate the lamp its disparity is zero by definition. The geometry does not describe the world on its own; it describes the world relative to where you are looking. Every depth judgement the two eyes make answers two questions at once, what is out there and where am I aimed, and the second question supplies the reference for the first. Clinicians who hold that idea find binocular results far less mysterious, because they stop asking what an object's disparity is and start asking what the patient was fixating when it was measured.
