# Description: Two eyes deliver two slightly different pictures, and the brain turns them into one: this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks teaches sensory fusion, the sensory half of the binocular merge. Learn how a small tolerance window around each matched point keeps tiny eye drifts from ever doubling the world, and how the differences that survive inside that same window are read as depth rather than doubling. Hear the classic finger demonstration of physiological diplopia, why the brain normally ignores the doubling that surrounds every fixation, and what vertical lines shown to one eye against horizontal lines shown to the other reveal about the competition behind single vision. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want binocular physiology explained for the ear.

Host: Here is a small miracle you performed all day today, mostly without noticing. You own two eyes, set a few centimetres apart, so each one looks at the world from its own vantage point and delivers its own picture to the back of your head. Two pictures, taken from two positions that never quite agree, arrive every waking moment, and yet you have never once seen your desk, your street, or your own hand as two overlapping images. The pictures go in twice, and the world comes out once. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks. This is Series 3, Ocular Physiology. This season, Season 9, is Binocular and Oculomotor Physiology, and this is episode five, Sensory Fusion. Our season so far has been a careful build: episode one asked why we have two eyes at all, episode two laid down the physiological basis of binocular vision, episode three mapped retinal correspondence, and episode four, simply titled Fusion, introduced the whole act of merging two into one. Tonight we stay inside that act and meet its sensory half. Our guide is ready, so let me hand over with the framing question: what exactly did episode four hand us, and what is left over for tonight?

Guide: Thank you, and welcome to the halfway house of the season. Episode four gave us the whole achievement in one word: fusion, the taking of two images and the delivery of one. What it deliberately did not do is pull that achievement apart into its two working halves, and that split is where tonight begins. Fusion has two hands. One hand is muscular: it aims the eyes, turns them, holds them, and keeps the two lines of sight locked on the same target. That hand belongs to episode six, Motor Fusion, and I will keep pointing at it so nobody thinks we have forgotten it. The other hand is sensory, and it is ours tonight: the combining of the two retinal images into a single percept, entirely inside the nervous system. That is what the phrase sensory fusion means: image combining, performed by the brain, with no muscles involved at the moment of combination. And you already own the groundwork, because episode three, Retinal Correspondence, gave us the catalogue: points on the two retinae that are partners, matched addresses, arranged so that when the eyes aim together, images of the same object land on partner points. Correspondence supplies the addresses. Sensory fusion is the delivery service that opens both letters at every address and merges them into a single message. One warning before we start, carried over from episode two, Physiological Basis of Binocular Vision: because the two eyes sit apart, the two images are never identical. Any theory of single vision that begins by assuming two identical pictures has already failed. So tonight's real question is this: what rule lets the brain say merge for some differences, and two things for others?

Host: Let me sharpen that, because it deserves sharpening. Take two cameras, separate them by the distance between your eyes, and photograph the same room. The two photographs are similar but not the same; nearer objects sit at slightly different positions in each frame. Now imagine a printing shop told to glue them together, edge matching edge. The result would be a smudged ghost, doubled wherever the frames disagreed. And notice the awkward part: the two photographs disagree most exactly where the interesting things live, the near objects, the faces, the hands, so the brain cannot simply throw the disagreements away and keep the rest. Yet that is roughly what your head does all day, and the result is a single, crisp, steady world. Is the brain really demanding that the two pictures be identical, and simply failing gracefully whenever they are not?

Guide: No, and the refusal to demand identity is the whole secret. The demand is not sameness; it is match within tolerance. Around the point you are fixating, the brain merges the two images even when their positions disagree by a small angle. That disagreement is allowed, up to a limit, and the zone of allowance has a name: the Panum area, after a nineteenth century physiologist who first described it. Picture it as a tolerance window wrapped around each corresponding point from episode three. Inside the window, two images count as one; outside it, they count as two. Now the size of the window, and here precision matters. At the fovea, the centre of your gaze, the window is tiny. We measure it in minutes of arc, and the honest answer is a handful of them, single figures up to the low teens depending on how you measure and whom you read. To feel how small that is, hold this comparison: one minute of arc is roughly the width of a fine human hair viewed at arm's length, so the foveal window is a few hair-widths wide. Then leave the centre. Out in the periphery, the window widens several times over; minutes of arc at the fovea become tens of minutes off-centre. Hold both facts at once, because the whole episode is built on them: the window is never zero, and the window is never one size.

Host: A few hair-widths of forgiveness at the centre, several times that off to the sides. And notice you have quietly promised that this window does two jobs, not one. Take the first job first: stability. If a matcher at the heart of vision is constantly comparing two images, why does the world not shiver every time my eyes move even slightly? Do the eyes actually hold that still, or does some quiet tolerance paper over the movement before it ever reaches awareness?

Guide: They do not, and this is where the first dividend is paid. Even when you stare at a single letter and mean it, your eyes are on the move: slow wanderings, tiny flicks, a restless negotiation with themselves that never switches off. The movements are far too small for you to notice as movement, but they displace the retinal images by a few minutes of arc, exactly the scale of the foveal window. Now run the counterfactual. If the combiner demanded exact positions, every one of those drifts would throw the two images out of register, and the world would blur or double several times each second of your waking life. The tolerance window is the shock absorber that prevents exactly that. Every drift lands inside the window, the images still count as one, and the merged percept simply holds. This is the first passport the window stamps: stability. Single vision is not the reward for perfect aim; it survives imperfect aim because the combiner forgives small error, and the eyes are always committing small errors. The wandering itself is a story with its own episode later this season, Oculomotor Control, where we follow the commands that move the eyes; tonight the wandering is only the reason a window must exist. And take one more quiet credit for the design. The two eyes are never perfectly matched as optical instruments either, so the two images differ slightly in size and clarity even when the aim is perfect. The window absorbs that as well, and you have never once noticed.

Host: So the window forgives, and the world holds still. But you said two jobs, and forgiveness alone would make the window a bin for errors. Where does depth come in? If the differences inside the window are merely tolerated, in what sense are they ever used?

Guide: Here is the elegance, and it is the hinge of the whole episode. The window does not erase the differences it tolerates; it classifies them. A difference inside the window is not discarded as error. It is read as depth. Recall from episode two, Physiological Basis of Binocular Vision, that binocular disparity, the positional difference between the two images of one object, is the raw material the two eyes manufacture for free, every moment. Tonight's window is the courtroom that decides what that raw material becomes. Fixate an object in front of you. Something nearer than it sends its images to points displaced toward the nose in one eye and toward the temple in the other. Something farther displaces them the opposite way. Inside the window, the brain reads the direction of that displacement as nearer or farther, and its size as how much, and that reading is stereopsis, the finest depth sense you own. This is why we built the season in this order: Series 1, in episode seven of season three, Depth Perception, gave you the everyday tour, the cues, the demonstrations, the wonder of it. Tonight you are standing in the engine room of the finest cue, and the machinery is exactly the tolerance window we just met. Notice the price of admission, though, because it teaches you what stereopsis really is. Depth reading works only inside the window. Push the disparity beyond it, and the depth reading does not grow stronger; it collapses into doubling. The window is the stage, and stereopsis performs only on the stage. At the fovea the stage is narrow and the discrimination is exquisite; off-centre the stage is wide and the reading is coarse. Geography and performance travel together. The finest depth judgements you will ever make, threading a needle, catching a ball, judging the last step of a staircase, are foveal judgements, made inside the narrowest and most demanding window in the whole system.

Host: Then let us do what this series always does when the theory is on the table: perform it. I want every listener to prove the window exists, using nothing but a finger and the far side of the room. Walk us through it slowly, and tell us exactly what the result is called.

Guide: The demonstration needs two fixations and one finger, and it has never once failed. Hold a finger upright, about fifteen or twenty centimetres from your face. Now fixate something across the room, a door frame, a picture, a light switch, and hold that fixation honestly. Watch the finger. It has become two fingers, faint and transparent, one drifting to each side of the object you are fixating. Now reverse the arrangement: fixate the finger, and let the far wall go. The door frame splits into two frames. What you have just watched is your own tolerance window at work, and the doubling has a name: physiological diplopia. Physiological, because it is the normal output of a healthy two-eyed system, not a fault in one. The rule it demonstrates is exact. Every object you do not fixate reports double whenever its disparity exceeds the window at its place in the scene. The finger, much nearer than your fixation point, produces a disparity far too large for any window to swallow, so the honest report is two fingers. The background, farther than fixation when you reversed, exceeds the window in the other direction, so the honest report is two frames. And this is not an occasional quirk. At this moment, whatever you are looking at, most of the scene lies outside its local window, and the honest raw report of your two eyes is a single core surrounded by a doubled fringe. Try the finger test on anyone with two working eyes and it works, every time. It is physiology, not a defect, and the design intends it. Keep the finger there a while and the two ghosts hold their positions steadily, because the doubling is exactly as stable as the anatomy that produces it.

Host: And yet here is the protest every listener is already raising. If most of the visual field is beyond its window at any instant, if the raw report really is a doubled fringe around a single core, why has nobody spent a life wading through doubles? Why do we never notice the fringe? What is the brain doing with all that doubling?

Guide: It is ignoring it, and the ignoring is active, normal, and worth respecting. Three reasons carry it. First, geography. The doubled fringe is mostly peripheral, and the periphery is where vision is coarsest. Series 2, in episode four of season eight, Photoreceptor Distribution, mapped the reason: receptor density falls away from the fovea in an orderly gradient, and the tolerance windows widen in step with the falling detail. The parts of the scene that double are also the parts that are blurred and low in resolution, so the doubling arrives already de-emphasised. Second, stability. The fringe doubling never changes its meaning; it does not signal anything new from one moment to the next, and perception privileges change. A report that is permanent and uninformative is quietly turned down. Third, and most important, meaning. The brain builds its single model of the world from the region that is fixated and fused, the matched core, and treats the fringe as scaffolding around the building. Move your fixation, and the whole arrangement re-runs: the new target joins the single core, and the old one slides into the fringe. So the doubled crowd is always there and never news, which is precisely why you had to be taught about it today. And notice what this is not. It is not damage, not a failing, not a trick the tired brain plays. It is the normal economics of attention, spent on the part of the world that matters, at the expense of the part that merely persists.

Host: Before we leave the window itself, one more question about its shape. Is the Panum area one fixed measurement, the same at every place on the retina, the same for every object? Or does it flex? What sets its scale, and what makes it breathe?

Guide: It flexes, and its scale is set by geography. The master gradient is the one this series keeps meeting: resolution falls as you leave the fovea, and the window widens in step, several-fold, from minutes of arc at the centre to tens of minutes far out. Where sampling is finest, tolerance is tightest; where sampling is coarse, tolerance is loose. We walked that resolution gradient on its own in Season 7, episode four, Spatial Resolution, and tonight it returns wearing binocular clothes. The pairing is no coincidence but a bargain: a matcher that could not forgive would be useless where sampling is coarse, and a matcher that forgave too generously at the centre would throw away the very precision the centre exists to provide. But there is fine print beyond the geography, and it is worth the pause. The window is not a painted circle, fixed forever. A larger target sits inside a slightly wider window than a small one. Higher contrast buys a little tolerance; low contrast spends it. A target in smooth motion is granted more room than a flickering one. The window is a zone of allowance around each corresponding point, and its width breathes gently with the size, the contrast and the movement of whatever is being matched. None of this is exotic. It is the same matcher tuning itself to the reliability of its evidence, which is exactly what a sensible judge does with a witness. The lesson to carry out is this: when you think about the Panum area, think of a family of windows, tight at the centre and wide at the edges, not a single number.

Host: Now the stress test, and it is my favourite part of tonight. Everything so far has assumed the two eyes send versions of the same scene, disagreeing only slightly. What happens if we break that assumption on purpose? Suppose the two eyes are shown two genuinely incompatible pictures. Can the combiner still find a merge?

Guide: This experiment is old, simple, and still astonishing, and its result is called binocular rivalry. Arrange a viewer so that one eye sees only vertical stripes and the other eye sees only horizontal stripes. Nothing is wrong with either picture; each is perfectly visible to its own eye. Now ask what the person sees, and the answer is: never both. The brain cannot merge these two, because there is no single arrangement of lines that is both vertical and horizontal, no possible match anywhere. What awareness does instead is alternate. For a few seconds the vertical stripes are vivid and the horizontal ones are gone. Then, without anything changing in the pictures, the verticals fade and the horizontals take the floor, vivid and complete, while the verticals vanish. Back and forth it goes, a slow ping-pong of awareness, a few seconds per visit, for as long as the two eyes are fed their incompatible pictures. You can watch it at home with nothing more exotic than a cardboard tube held to one eye and your free hand seen by the other, because rivalry needs no laboratory and no training; it asks only two healthy eyes and two pictures that cannot agree. Two things about rivalry matter for us tonight. First, it is normal-capability physiology, the same in everyone with two working eyes; there is nothing faulty about it. Second, and this is the gift, it exposes the method. Binocular combination, it turns out, is a competition. Every moment, the two images compete for a single awareness, and in the everyday case the competition resolves almost instantly into fusion, because the two images mostly agree and matches are everywhere. The matched features lock, and one world wins. Rivalry only shows you the competition nakedly, by feeding it an input with no matches at all. The machinery does not glue photographs. It fights for a single story, and usually it wins by finding matches.

Host: Say the moral once more, because I want it in one breath. The brain is not gluing two photographs; it is running a contest, and tolerance is the referee. Put tonight's three phenomena side by side for us, the locked core, the ignored fringe, the alternating strangers, and give us the single sentence that holds them together.

Guide: Here is the sentence: the brain is a matcher, and the window is its rulebook. Start from episode three's corresponding points, the paired addresses. Around each address sits a tolerance window. Where the two images fall inside each other's windows, they lock, and that locking, binocular locking, is sensory fusion in the strict sense: the images combine, and the leftover differences inside the window are promoted to depth, which is stereopsis earning its keep. Where the differences exceed the window, the system reports two, which is physiological diplopia, and then turns the volume down on the report, which is the normal ignoring we practised a few minutes ago. Where no matches can be found at all, the competition runs in the open, which is rivalry. One window, three verdicts: merge and read depth, double and ignore, or alternate. And notice the economy, because it is the deepest thing tonight. The same tolerance that forgives the eyes' tiny wanderings also measures the world's depth. Forgiveness and measurement are one parameter, not two. A window slightly too wide would steady the world but flatten it; slightly too narrow would sharpen the depth but shatter the scene. The width we actually have is the compromise that buys both, which is why I keep calling the window a passport: to stability, to stereopsis, to single vision itself. And under all of it sits one quiet condition: the muscles must first aim the eyes well enough that the windows overlap at all. Hold that thought, because it is the door to next episode.

Host: Then let us run the misconception sweep, the way we always do before the recap. Give me the beliefs about seeing single that this episode should have dismantled, and the correction for each.

Guide: Four, and they are all common. First misconception: that single vision means the two images were identical. That has never been true at any moment of your life; the images are always different, and the requirement was always match within tolerance. Second: that seeing double means the system has failed. The finger test shows doubling is part of the normal vocabulary of a two-eyed system, the honest report for any object whose disparity exceeds the window, present in every healthy observer, available on demand, every time. Third: that the Panum area is one fixed circle with one published number. It is a family of zones, a few hair-widths across at the fovea, several times wider off-centre, breathing gently with the size, contrast and motion of the target. Fourth: that rivalry is the combiner breaking down. It is the combiner working perfectly on an impossible input, showing you the competition that fusion usually wins so quickly and so quietly that you never glimpse it. A capability, not a crack. Clear those four away and what remains is cleaner than the folklore: a matcher, a rulebook, and three lawful verdicts.

Host: One last question, and it is the one we save for the end. Across all the windows and verdicts, which detail do you find students underestimate the most?

Guide: The noise never stops. Students meet physiological diplopia as a party trick that begins when a finger goes up, and they file it away as an occasional curiosity. It is the standing condition of every moment of two-eyed life. If your eyes are open right now, the doubling is there: around whatever you are fixating, most of this room is reporting double, and the volume has simply been turned down by a system with better things to attend to. The fringe is reported and then retired, every second you are awake, by a mechanism so unfailingly consistent that its silence feels like absence. That reframes the whole achievement. The miracle of binocular vision is not that two eyes somehow deliver one picture, as if by plumbing. It is that a matcher built to tolerate, to measure and, when necessary, to compete still hands you a world so quiet you had to be taught, today, that the doubling exists at all. The single world is not what arrives at the eyes. It is what the matcher returns, verdict by verdict, fixation by fixation, all day long.

Host: And the quiet depends on more than the courtroom. We have spent tonight inside the combiner, but the combiner can only work on images that arrive roughly aimed. Next time, the other half of fusion takes the stage. Set up episode six, Motor Fusion, for us: what does the muscular partner add that tonight's physiology could never do alone?

Guide: Next time, in Motor Fusion, the muscles step forward and take their half of the bargain. The windows we met tonight forgive a few hair-widths of error, but forgiveness has limits, and something must keep the two eyes aimed so that the images land inside those limits in the first place. When a target approaches, the eyes must turn toward each other; when it recedes, they must relax apart; when it drifts, they must follow, smoothly and without being asked. That active aiming and holding, working moment by moment to keep both images inside the windows, is motor fusion, and it is the muscles answering the question tonight left open: who keeps the evidence admissible? Tonight was the courtroom that accepts, reads depth from, or doubles the two images. Next time is the crew that keeps delivering them side by side, well aimed, all day. The two halves are one system, and once they are joined the season follows the aiming machinery deeper still, into vergence physiology in episode seven.
