One machinery, three experiences, and one season closed. Our guide has carried this season with us from the first turn. Set the frame for the finale: what is the single claim that everything tonight hangs on?

Guide: The claim is short enough to say in one breath: colour and contrast are siblings, born of one comparison engine. Here is the whole pipeline, and notice how every station on it is one we have already visited. First, the catch: three cone classes, each a biased witness to the wavelength mix arriving at its patch of retinal ground. Second, the arithmetic: those catch counts are converted, almost immediately, into ratios and differences, a red-versus-green conversation, a blue-versus-yellow conversation, and a sum conversation that carries luminance. Third, the delivery: those difference conversations ride parallel routes through the visual pathway up to the cortex, each stream keeping its own wire the whole way. Fourth, the assembly: at the cortex they meet the same difference-detecting comparison machinery that serves contrast, the centre-versus-surround logic that episode after episode this season kept finding underneath the phenomena. That is the single machinery, and tonight it has to earn its keep. Here is the route: we re-walk the pipeline in four steps, we prove it against three everyday experiences, dusk, shadows and fog, we sweep away the misconceptions it quietly corrects, we salute the nine episodes that built it, and then we open the door to the season that waits. Let us begin at the front end, with the witnesses.

Host: Begin there, then. Three biased witnesses: the phrase has carried this season since its second week. Remind us what the first three episodes actually established, because everything downstream inherits their quirks, their biases and their blind spots.

Guide: Episode one, Physiology of Colour Vision, made the founding move: colour is not in the light. The physical world supplies a mixture of wavelengths at every point, an endless palette of recipes, and no patch of light carries a label saying red or blue or green. Colour begins as a question the visual system asks about those recipes. Episode two, Cone Types and Colour Detection, introduced the first layer of the answer: three cone classes, biased toward the long, the middle and the short wavelengths, biased but not exclusive, each with an overlapping tuning so that every wavelength stimulates all three to different degrees. And each cone, remember, is a mute accountant: it reports only how many catches happened, never which wavelength caused them. Episode three, Trichromatic Vision, explained why three is the number: with two classes, far too many different recipes would be indistinguishable, but with three the system can match almost any recipe by adjusting just three intensities, which is why any three suitably chosen lights can match a colour and why two physically different mixtures can look identical. So what leaves the receptor layer is not a picture, and it is certainly not a colour. It is three sets of catch counts, gathered quietly across the scene. Three biased witnesses have testified. The trial now moves to arithmetic.

Host: Arithmetic it is. Three raw counts walk into the retina and come out as colour. That transformation was episode four, Opponent-Process Mechanisms, and it is the season's hinge. Walk us through it: how do numbers become redness?

Guide: By subtraction and comparison, and almost at once. The counts do not travel raw for long. Neighbouring circuitry compares one cone class against another and builds three conversations. The first pits the long-biased signal against the middle-biased signal: a dial whose two poles we experience as red and green. The second pits the short-biased signal against the combined long and middle: the blue-versus-yellow dial. The third simply sums the long and middle contributions: the luminance conversation, the brightness ledger. Now listen to what a dial means. A cell on the red-versus-green channel can push its report toward one pole or the other, or fall silent at the balance point, but it can never report both poles at once. That single wiring fact explains an experience every listener owns: there is no colour that is reddish green, and none that is yellowish blue. The categories are built into the channels. And notice the economy of it. Three absolute counts have collapsed into two difference dials plus one sum ledger. The eye never reports the light; it reports how the light at one place compares with the light at another place, and with the light of other wavelengths at the same place. Difference is already the currency before the signal leaves the retina. Hold that fact, because it is about to become the whole story.

Host: Hold on, then, because you have just raised a puzzle worth a pause. You said a dial can fall silent at its balance point. What does a listener see when the dials go quiet? What is the colour of silence?

Guide: White, grey and black, and this is one of the quiet triumphs of the opponent design. A channel that reports no difference is not reporting nothing; it is reporting balance. Balance the long against the middle, and the blue against the yellow, and the colour conversations fall silent while the luminance conversation keeps talking: the result is white, or grey where the sum is lower, or black where it stops altogether. Now notice what that buys. A white wall under the noon sun and the same page under a warm indoor lamp deliver wildly different wavelength recipes at the eye, yet both can balance the dials, so both look white. The dials are indifferent to the recipe itself and report only the relationship between cone classes, which is a large part of why colour survives the day's changing light and why a page looks white outdoors and indoors alike. Remember episode three, where physically different mixtures could look identical: the dials are the reason. Any recipe that balances them in the same way is the same colour, because the dials cannot tell the recipes apart. White is not a wavelength. It is a verdict: the comparisons came out level.

Host: From the dials to the delivery. You said the conversations ride parallel routes. That was episode five, Colour Information Processing. Show us the journey: what travels, and how do the differences reach the cortex without being flattened back into one wire?

Guide: The dials keep separate wires. The red-versus-green conversation, the blue-versus-yellow conversation and the luminance conversation each ride their own parallel stream through the visual pathway, station by station, the route our seventh season walked from nerve to cortex. The streams are recombined much later, but through the delivery they stay apart, and the separation is a design decision with two payoffs. The first is division of labour: the streams answer different questions, so each can be tuned for its own job. The luminance stream carries fine detail and fast changes; the colour streams carry slower, coarser news about the wavelength recipe. That division is why the world's sharpest edges and its finest text ride on the brightness conversation while colour is painted on more coarsely over the top, like watercolour over ink. And when the cortex finally reads the streams against one another, it can hold a colour steady while the illumination drifts across the day, another subtraction, performed one station higher. The second payoff is robustness: separate routes can be gain-adjusted separately and read separately by the cortex, so the streams can be balanced against one another instead of tangled together. And notice what has not happened yet, anywhere along the delivery. Nowhere has anyone reported a colour. The traffic is still differences, ratios, dials, verdicts of comparison. A colour, as an experience, has not been built. It is built at the destination, where these streams arrive and are read alongside a machinery we have not yet named tonight, the one that has been waiting for us since episode six.

Host: The one that has been waiting since episode six: the contrast engine. Episodes six, seven and eight handed it to us in three passes: Contrast Sensitivity, then Spatial Frequency, then Contrast Detection. Assemble those three for us now. What does the comparison machinery actually look like from the cortex's side of the desk?

Guide: As a bank of comparators. Start with episode six, Contrast Sensitivity, the engine's performance envelope: the visual system does not ask how much light falls on a patch, it asks how different that patch is from its surroundings, and the chart of how faint a difference survives detection at each scale is the contrast sensitivity function. Episode seven, Spatial Frequency, opened the bank itself: the scene is analysed in parallel by many comparators tuned to different scales, some watching coarse swaths of the image, some watching fine grain, a whole stack of size-selective difference detectors all reading the same scene at once. Episode eight, Contrast Detection, stated the threshold law: differences smaller than a channel's limit never make it into report at all; they do not arrive faintly, they do not arrive weakly, they do not arrive. Now put the two halves of the season side by side, because this is the hinge of the finale. Colour is difference between cone classes at a place. Contrast is difference in luminance between neighbouring places. Both are reports of relationship; both are computed by subtracting one signal from another; both die when the differences die. One comparison engine, wearing two faces. The retina and the cortex have been running one trick all season, and tonight the trick has to explain your evening.

Host: Then let us collect on it. Three experiences, chosen because every listener has lived all three and none of them feels like machinery. First: dusk. The garden at sundown loses its colours one by one until the roses and the lawn have become shades of grey. Why does the light's dimming take the colour with it?

Guide: Because the witnesses change, and the new witnesses carry no colour. When daylight fails, the sensor population of your retina hands over. Recall Series 3, season six, episode three, Rod Physiology: one hundred and twenty million rods, the night shift, each carrying a single pigment that produces a single kind of answer. One pigment, one message: a catch happened. That is univariance in the rods' own key: a rod cannot see wavelength, only quantity. So as the light falls, the cones, which want generous light and report best in it, fade from the conversation, and the rods, which need almost no light at all, take the shift. The handover is not a switch but a slow changing of the guard across many minutes, the very climb that season six measured in its dark adaptation hour, which is why dusk drains colour gradually rather than snapping it off. And the blue-green tint of twilight that every listener has noticed is the same story told once more: the rod population is biased toward the middle and shorter wavelengths compared with the daylight cone mix, so the tones of the scene slide toward where the new witnesses are most sensitive. The objects have not changed. The wavelengths in the garden have not changed. The witnesses have. Dusk is the retina re-staffing its watch mid-scene, and the new staff simply have no colour to report. And notice where the change bites hardest: the very centre of gaze, which the rods never staff, as the same episode recalled. That is why a faint star at dusk vanishes when you look straight at it and returns when you glance aside; the centre is still cone country, and the cones have gone quiet.

Host: Experience two, and this one is stranger, because nothing about the light is in dispute at all. A shadow on a white wall at noon is less light by any meter's verdict, yet it reads as grey paint, never as a black hole punched in the wall. Why does the brain refuse to report what is physically there?

Guide: Because reporting raw light was never the brain's plan. The comparison machinery we assembled a few minutes ago runs a subtraction that perception scientists call lightness constancy: before reporting a patch, the machinery compares it with its neighbours and asks how much of the difference is the object and how much is the illumination. A shadow is illumination interrupted: the same white wall, the same reflectance, with the sun's contribution cut. When the machinery discounts the part of the signal that the lighting explains, what survives to awareness is the wall's own property, its lightness. Now the grey instead of black follows immediately. The shadowed patch still stands in the same ratio to its sunlit surroundings that the wall stands in everywhere else; the comparisons across the shadow's edge are reduced, but they are not abolished. The engine reports relationship, and the relationship now says slightly darker wall, so grey it is. Notice, too, that this is the same subtraction logic that built the opponent dials, now applied across space instead of across wavelength: discount a common factor that carries no news, keep the differences that do. The shadow looks grey rather than black because the brain has decided, correctly, that black is a claim about the object, and the object has not changed. The light has.

Host: Experience three, the opposite crime. Fog does not remove light, it adds it, a bright milky veil laid over everything. If the visual system reported brightness, fog should make the world brighter, and in a meter's world it does. But what we notice is that the world disappears. What exactly is vanishing in the fog?

Guide: Differences are vanishing, and brightness is merely the bystander. Fog works by scattering light in every direction, which superimposes a roughly uniform veil of brightness across the whole scene. Now recall what subtraction does to a uniform addition: nothing. The veil adds the same amount to the hill and to the sky, to the letter and to the page, so the comparisons, the differences the machinery lives on, shrink toward zero while the raw levels rise. The hill that differed from the sky by a bold step now differs by a whisper, and the whisper falls below the thresholds of episode eight, so the hill stops existing in report. The meter, meanwhile, reads more light than ever, and it is right; the two instruments simply measure different things, and only one of them is a visual system. This is why the fog experience is a contrast story and not a brightness story, and the distinction is not academic pedantry. Ask yourself what you have actually lost: not the light level, which has climbed, but the structure, which has collapsed. The comparison engine has been fed a scene of near-identical differences, and it correctly reports a near-featureless field. Fog, steam on a window, a misted windscreen: every veil in daily life runs the same single experiment, the uniform addition that starves a difference-detector. Three experiences, and every one of them fell out of the pipeline without our adding one new mechanism. That is what a synthesis buys you.

Host: Before we state what the season has actually proven, I can hear three old habits of thought protesting from the back row, and a finale should not leave them standing. Sweep the misconceptions for us. Where does the machinery contradict common sense most bluntly?

Guide: Three misconceptions, then, and each one the machinery corrects. First misconception: colour lives in objects. The tomato is red, we say, as though redness were paint baked into the skin. But the tomato's surface merely reflects the long wavelengths generously and the others poorly; whether that recipe becomes a red experience depends on witnesses, dials and a comparator, and when the illumination changes, the recipe at the eye changes with it while the tomato still looks red, which is constancy doing its subtraction on your behalf. The object offers a recipe; the visual system cooks the colour. Second misconception: brightness is the primary currency and contrast is a refinement. The season says the reverse. The machinery was subtracting before it was summing; the dials are differences; the cortical bank reads differences; a patch of light with no difference around it is nearly invisible to this system, however intense it is. Contrast is not a refinement of brightness; brightness is one report among the differences. Third misconception: the dusk handover is a design flaw, the cones failing and the rods arriving to bail the system out. It is the opposite of a flaw. Two sensor families with opposite bargains, plus circuitry that hands over smoothly between them, means the eye spans a range of light that no single sensor family could cover, from noon on white sand to a starlit lane. The design is not a compromise that fails at dusk; it is a strategy that dusk reveals. Common sense says objects, brightness and failure. The machinery says recipes, differences and strategy.

Same verdict: a washed, featureless world. There is no leftover paradox, no scene where the meter and the experience disagree and the disagreement hangs unexplained. Host: One last look back before we look ahead. Across nine episodes and tonight's synthesis, which single detail do you think students underestimate the most? The one which, if it is missed, leaves the whole season sounding like a list of separate tricks?

Guide: That nothing, at any station of this pipeline, has ever been coloured or bright in itself. The light is not coloured; it carries a wavelength recipe. The cones are not coloured; they count. The opponent channels are not coloured; they subtract. Even the cortex is never handed a colour; it is handed differences, and it commits to an experience. Colour exists only in the comparison, and so does contrast, and so does brightness, and that is the season compressed to a sentence. Students who memorise the curves and the channels as separate facts will pass the examination and still be surprised by dusk. Students who hold the single engine will find that dusk, shadows and fog explain themselves, and that every future lecture in this series is a new lease on the same building.

Host: Let us carry it to the chair for a moment, the way this series always does, and then we will open the next door. If a listener keeps one practical habit from this finale, what should it be?

Guide: Learn to hear which channel a patient is describing. When someone says the world seems dim, they are reporting the luminance conversation. When someone says the world seems washed out, faded, like looking through a veil, they are reporting collapsed differences, the fog story, whether or not there is any fog outside. The two complaints are not the same sentence in different moods; they travel on different wires, and they point to different questions at the examination. And remember what an eye chart really is: black letters on a bright page, a contrast task delivered at many sizes, a deliberate probe of the comparison engine we assembled tonight. When you understand that vision is relationships and not amounts, the everyday clinic turns into this season's laboratory, and even the advice you give at dusk, slow down, expect the greys, trust the motion sense that the rods deliver so well in the periphery, is rod physiology and opponent physiology speaking through you. The habit is one sentence long: ask whether the complaint is about amount or about difference before you decide what to measure. Nothing clinical tonight, no disease and no deviation, only the normal machine understood well enough that its everyday performances no longer surprise the person holding the ophthalmoscope.
