Host: Colour arrives at the eye as a spread of wavelengths and leaves the eye as three small numbers, and everything astonishing about colour vision lives in the crossing between those two facts. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology, where we are deep in Season 8, Colour and Contrast Vision, at episode two, Cone Types and Colour Detection. Episode one, Physiology of Colour Vision, opened the season with the wide-angle questions: what colour is for, how it earns its keep for the seeing animal, and why colour deserves physiology of its own rather than a footnote after brightness. Today we descend to the sensors themselves and read their specification sheets one class at a time. With me, as ever, is our Guide. Set up the inventory. What exactly does today own, and what does it refuse to touch?

Guide: Today owns the three sensors of colour, spec by spec, and the two design quirks that turn three ordinary light detectors into the beginning of colour vision. First, the census and the tuning: three classes of cone, called long, middle and short for the part of the spectrum each catches best, peaking near five hundred and sixty, five hundred and thirty and four hundred and twenty nanometres respectively. Second, quirk number one: their sensitivity curves overlap broadly, so any wavelength lights all three to some degree, in three different proportions. Third, quirk number two: a principle called univariance, which says that one cone alone reports only how much light it caught, never which kind. Then the resolution: with three differently tuned catchers, the ratios between their catches carry the wavelength information, and colour lives in the comparisons, not the catches. Boundaries, as always. We deal with normal function only; variation, failure and testing belong to other episodes and other series. The circuits that perform the comparing belong to later episodes of this season, and the formal theory built on these three sensors, the colour matching story, is episode three, Trichromatic Vision, so we will walk to its door and stop there. One frame to carry through everything: the cones are not colour meters. They are three differently biased witnesses, and their disagreement is the evidence.

Host: Before the spec sheets, a quick recall of the geography, because we have met these cells before in this library. Remind us where cones live, and what actually makes one class different from another.

Guide: You have met them twice, and both meetings pay off today. The anatomy we mapped in Series 2, episode four of season eight, Photoreceptor Distribution: roughly five to six million cones per retina, clustered at the macula and packed most densely at the fovea, thinning to scattered outposts in the periphery. The physiology we began in Season 6, episode two, Photoreceptors, which introduced the two sensor families, and continued in episode four of that season, Cone Physiology, where these three cone pigments made their first proper appearance. Recall the recipe from that first meeting. Every photoreceptor runs on the same two-part pigment: a light-catching molecule derived from vitamin A, identical across the retina, wrapped in a protein shell called an opsin, which is not identical. Each cone class builds a slightly different opsin, and that small edit changes how much energy a photon needs to bend the light-catching molecule, which changes the wavelength the pigment absorbs best. Same machinery, three different tunings. A point worth repeating because examinations love it: the classes are named for wavelength, not for colour. The long class does not peak in red light, and the short class does not peak anywhere you would call blue paint. Keep the names technical and the numbers honest.

Host: Sheet number one, then. The long-wavelength cones. Give me the numbers, and then tell me what the numbers actually mean.

Guide: The long class, often shortened to the L cones, peaks near five hundred and sixty nanometres. Notice where that peak sits: in yellowish-green light, well past the greens and short of anything you would call deep red, which is the first honest correction to the nickname red cone. What does the peak mean, precisely? It means that photon for photon, that pigment absorbs light of about that wavelength more often than it absorbs any other. It does not mean the cone responds only there. The absorption curve is a broad hill, not a spike. A long-wavelength cone catches light across a wide span: most readily near its peak, progressively less toward the deep reds on one side, and still meaningfully down through the greens and toward the violet end on the other. There is almost nowhere in the visible spectrum where the long cones fall completely silent. Two more notes for the sheet. First, the tuning comes from the opsin: the long class builds the long-wavelength version of the protein, and the electrical neighbourhood it creates around the light-catching molecule sets the energy price of absorption. Second, because the long cones are numerous and broadly tuned, they are workhorses not only for colour but for daylight brightness itself: most of the photons caught while you read a letter on the acuity chart were caught first by long and middle cones. That double duty, colour and brightness from the same sensor, is only possible because of the overlap quirk, and we will name it shortly.

Host: You said near five hundred and sixty. How firm is that word near? These peaks sound like the kind of numbers that move when nobody is watching.

Guide: They do move, slightly, and the honesty is built into the word. The peak of a cone pigment is not read off a label; it is measured, either directly from the photopigments in the laboratory or by inference from the spectral sensitivity of observers, that is, how strongly the whole eye responds to each wavelength under controlled conditions. Different methods shift the estimate by a few nanometres, and individuals differ a little as well, so every respectable source writes near five hundred and sixty, near five hundred and thirty, near four hundred and twenty. Treat those three numbers as anchors, not arrows. The shape, though, does not move: each class has the same broad, single-hilled tuning, and the three hills are essentially shifted copies of one another, two close together and one far away. One more caution while we are being precise. The peak is a property of absorption, of catching, not of sensation; nothing about that peak says the world looks brightest in yellowish-green. The sheet records what the pigment does, not what the person sees. With that discipline in place, the numbers are wonderfully stable: three broad hills, two near-twins in the green-yellow region, one loner down in the violet-blue. Now take sheet number two.

Host: The middle class. I am told this one is nearly a photocopy of the first, and that the nearness is the whole story.

Guide: Nearly, and the nearness is the story. The middle-wavelength cones, the M cones, peak near five hundred and thirty nanometres, right in the green. Their curve has the same broad-hill shape, spanning a wide range with the peak in the middle. Now lay the two sheets side by side. The long and middle curves are shifted by only about thirty nanometres, which, on the width of these broad hills, means they overlap almost completely. Where the long curve is high, the middle curve is nearly as high; where the middle is high, the long is not far behind. Here is the remarkable part: the two opsins differ by only a handful of amino acids out of hundreds, a tiny edit in the recipe, and that small change slides the whole absorption hill sideways by thirty nanometres. A cheap edit, a brand-new sensor. What does the near-twin design buy? A comparison between two very similar but slightly shifted meters is exquisitely sensitive to small wavelength differences wherever both respond strongly, and for these two that region is the green, yellow and orange stretch where so much of the natural world lives its daily life: leaves, ripe fruit, faces. The long and middle pair is the engine of fine red-green discrimination, and you will see exactly how the comparison works when we reach the ratios. Notice also the pairing's second gift: two densely populated, broadly tuned, heavily overlapping classes cover daylight magnificently, which is why daylight vision is bright and sharp as well as colourful.

Host: Sheet number three, the short class, and you warned me this one comes with a census oddity attached. Take the tuning and the headcount together.

Guide: The short-wavelength cones, the S cones, peak near four hundred and twenty nanometres, down at the violet-blue end of the spectrum. Their opsin belongs to a different branch of the pigment family from the long and middle twins, an older lineage, so the S cone is less a photocopy and more a separate invention. Its curve is broad like the others, but it sits mostly on the blue side, and short wavelengths are hard work for the eye's optics: the lens and the macular pigment filter a good share of the shortest light before it ever reaches the retina, a normal design feature that trims scatter and shields the finest central tissue. Now the census, and it is lopsided in every direction. Across the retina as a whole, the S cones are a small minority, roughly one in twelve of the cone population or fewer, depending on whose count you read. And there is a sharper fact: the S cones are absent from the foveal centre altogether. The very middle of your sharpest vision is a two-class zone. Elsewhere the mosaic runs at roughly two long cones for every middle cone, with S cones sprinkled sparsely among them, never in a neat repeating pattern. So the celebrated three-class system is really a two-class system at the very centre and a lopsided three everywhere else. Hold those two facts, the missing centre and the one in twelve, because they explain a surprising amount about how colour behaves across the visual field.

Host: Stop there, because that absence startled me. The fovea is the flagship of the retina, the address on every acuity chart, and you are telling me an entire sensor class did not get an invitation. Why?

Guide: Several reasons are argued, and the most compelling is pure optics. Short wavelengths do not focus as cleanly as middle and long wavelengths in the human eye, an inherent property of any curved optical system, so an image built from violet-blue light would sit slightly blurred at the very centre no matter how the sensors were arranged. Filling the foveal centre with S cones would plant a coarser, blue-biased channel in the finest-resolution patch of the whole retina, and the design declines to. The centre is reserved for the long and middle pair, the twins that share the sharpest focus. The consequence is that colour vision is not uniform across the retina. Red-green style comparisons, powered by the densely packed twins, run at full resolution in the centre. Blue-yellow style comparisons, powered by the sparse S cones, are coarser everywhere and are literally absent at the exact point you fixate. A tiny blue spot fixated dead centre can be harder to place than the same spot held slightly off-centre. None of this is a defect; it is the normal layout: three sensors with three very different headcounts, two broad, densely stacked twins and one sparse specialist with its own territory. That lopsided census is the first clue that the brain cannot simply read three equal dials. It has to compare uneven witnesses, which brings us to the two quirks that make the whole scheme work.

Host: Quirk number one, then. You have promised that broad overlap is not a sloppy accident. Make the case. Surely three narrow, cleanly separated detectors, one per band of the spectrum, would be tidier?

Guide: Consider the tidy alternative first, because it fails beautifully. Suppose each cone were a narrow-band meter, responding only within a tight stripe of the spectrum and staying silent on either side. Between the stripes would lie gaps: wavelengths that no sensor caught, and light in those bands would be invisible to the cones entirely. Brightness would fragment too, because a scene lit at a gap-wavelength would be dim for all three classes at once. Broad overlap solves both problems at once. Because each cone responds across a wide span, and the three spans together cover the visible spectrum, there is no visible wavelength that leaves all three silent. Every wavelength lights all three to some degree, and the genius is that it lights them in three different proportions. At four hundred and twenty nanometres the short class answers loudest, but the middle and long classes still answer faintly, each by its own amount. At five hundred and thirty the middle answers loudest with the long close behind and the short quieter. At five hundred and sixty the long leads, the middle follows, and the short lags. One wavelength, three responses, one distinctive pattern of proportions. The overlap does double duty, too: because the long and middle cones respond to almost everything, they double as the retina's daylight brightness collectors. Overlap, in short, is not noise in the data. It is the data.

Host: Which sets up the strangest sentence in the episode, quirk number two. Tell us plainly what univariance says, and then convince us that it is true.

Guide: Here is the plain version. One cone, taken alone, reports one number: how much light it caught. It cannot report which kind of light, because many different combinations of wavelength and brightness drive exactly the same response. Picture the cone as a small gutter. A long, gentle rain can fill it to the same mark as a short, violent downpour. Looking at the water level alone, you cannot say which rain produced it; the gutter reports a level, never a history. So it is with the cone. A faint light at the pigment's favourite wavelength and a much brighter light well off the peak can produce identical responses. Dim orange and brighter green, a weak red and a stronger yellow-green: for any single cone, countless wavelength-and-brightness pairs are indistinguishable. The principle is called univariance, one variable, and it is not a flaw of cone engineering. Every photoreceptor in every eye ever built lives under the same rule, because a receptor is a bucket with a catch, not a spectrometer with a judgement. You can demonstrate the rule tonight: the rods run under it too, which is why in near darkness, when rods do all the sensing, roses and violets fade toward the same greys. The sensors are working; the wavelength information simply is not in any single sensor's report. It was never there. One witness, one number, no colour. The information has to be smuggled out by another route, and finding that route is the next question.

Host: So comparison is the escape route. But before the full rescue, give me the stepping stone. One cone cannot do it. Could two cone classes manage, or is three a hard requirement?

Guide: One cone alone yields no colour at all: with a single report there is nothing to compare, and the whole world of wavelength-and-brightness pairs collapses into one grey scale of how much. Two classes change the game, because now a ratio exists, one comparison between two differently shifted hills, and that single ratio does carry some wavelength information along the span where both respond. Plenty of animals run retinas with fewer classes and see a coarser but real palette. So two is not useless. But one ratio is one number, and one number leaves enormous tangles: many pairs of wavelengths would drive the same single balance, and whole regions of the spectrum would remain confounded with brightness. Add the third witness, tuned far away in the violet-blue, and a second, independent ratio appears. Two ratios from three witnesses pin the wavelength far more tightly than one ratio from two, and they do it along a dimension, the blue-yellow axis, that the two near-twins could barely touch, since their hills are too similar to disagree much at the short end. Why exactly three, whether three is a limit or a choice, and how the classic experiments fixed the count once and for all: that is the material of episode three, Trichromatic Vision, and we stop at its door today. What we can already say is this: the third witness is not a luxury. It is the difference between a hint and a measurement.

Host: Then give me the rescue in full. Three univariant witnesses who each report only how much. How does their disagreement actually recover the wavelength?

Guide: Because the disagreement itself is a code, and it survives everything a single report cannot survive. Watch the logic in two moves. Move one: any wavelength lights all three cone classes in three different proportions, because the three absorption hills are shifted copies of each other. Change the wavelength and you change the proportions. At five hundred and forty nanometres the long and middle classes respond strongly but in one particular balance. Slide down to five hundred and the balance tips clearly toward the middle class. Slide up to five hundred and sixty and it tips toward the long class, with the short class trailing. Each wavelength stamps its own characteristic ratio on the three responses. Move two: raise the brightness instead, and all three responses rise together, roughly equally. Here is the elegance: a factor common to all three cancels out of a ratio. The totals grow, but the ratios stay fixed. So the comparison strips brightness away and keeps wavelength, which is exactly the separation that no single cone can perform. Colour lives in the comparisons between catches, not in the catches themselves. The long and middle pair already separates wavelengths finely along the green-yellow-orange span; the sparse short witness adds the blue-yellow dimension. Three univariant reports, two independent ratios, and the ambiguity that doomed each witness alone is broken by the other two. That is the entire trick, and everything else this season is built on top of it.

Host: I want to test that with real numbers before I believe it, so take a concrete case. Walk me through one wavelength, then a second, then a brightening, the way you would on a whiteboard.

Guide: Purely for illustration, and I stress that the values are invented for clarity rather than measured, score each class out of ten. Light at five hundred and forty nanometres: the long class answers at eight, the middle at nine, the short at one. The balance of long to middle is eight to nine, slightly favouring the middle, and that balance is the wavelength's signature. Slide the light to five hundred and sixty: now the long answers at nine, the middle at six, the short at one. Same kind of light, a little redder, and the balance has flipped to favour the long class; nothing else about the scene needed to change, because the shift in the ratios did all the work. Now the brightness test. Take the first light and double its intensity: every score doubles together. The totals are larger, and the totals are what brightness reads. But eight to nine remains eight to nine, and nine to one remains nine to one, so the colour signature is untouched. Brightness scales the catches; wavelength re-balances them. One warning: never compare ratios across very different intensities and expect them to hold perfectly in the real eye, because real responses compress and adapt. The principle is exact; the arithmetic is not. The brain, in any case, does not read ratios off a whiteboard. It is wired, downstream of the cones, to do the comparing continuously, and that wiring is a later story this season.

Host: Let us press the frame you gave us at the start, because it deserves a full airing. The cones are not colour meters. They are three differently biased witnesses whose disagreement is the evidence. Take your time with that.

Guide: Think of an event witnessed by three bystanders, each with a different bias, each able to testify only to a quantity: how strongly was I stimulated? None of them can name the cause. One witness alone is useless for identification; a thousand dim causes fit any single testimony. But three witnesses with different, known biases, all testifying about the same event, produce a pattern. The first was stirred strongly, the second moderately, the third barely at all. That pattern of disagreement, read against the known biases, narrows the possibilities to essentially one. Swap the event and a different pattern appears, and so on across the whole range of events. The biases that look like weaknesses are precisely what make the system informative: three identical witnesses would be no better than one, three times over, because identical reports disagree about nothing. So it is with the retina. The cones never see colour. Each delivers one graded report, how much, and the three reports disagree in wavelength-specific proportions. The colours you experience are computed from that disagreement, downstream, by circuits that compare the reports. This is why the honest summary of today is deliberately deflating: no single cell in your eye is a colour sensor in the everyday sense, yet the eye as a whole is a colour instrument, because the information is not stored in any one witness but in the spread between them. The cones deliver disagreement. The brain delivers colour.

Host: One question I can hear a student asking across a lecture room. If comparison is everything, do the cones compare themselves? Are the witnesses in the same room?

Guide: No, and it matters that they are not. Each cone is a solitary catcher: it integrates the light landing on it and hands on one graded number. It has no access to its neighbours' catches, and no cone anywhere in the retina performs a subtraction or a ratio. The comparing happens after the cones, in the retinal circuits that pool and oppose these signals, and then further along the visual pathway, and that machinery, which turns three private reports into colour signals, belongs to later episodes of this season. Today we stay deliberately at the sensors, because the comparison can only ever be as good as the witnesses it compares. Notice the layering: shift a hill, thin a witness, remove one from the centre, and every downstream comparison changes with it. The cones are the raw material of colour, and their quirks, the overlap and the univariance, are inherited by everything built above them. Catching first, comparing second, experiencing third. Today owned the catching.

Host: Now let us pay two debts to the first series, because tonight's machinery settles an argument that Series 1 started and a question it promised.

Guide: Series 1, episode nine of season three, Colour Vision, asked the question that opens every conversation on this subject: where is the colour, actually? The answer given there was that colour is not in the objects; it is an experience the brain builds from the light objects return. Tonight you have watched the first stage of that building, and it sharpens the old answer by one more step. The colour is not in the objects, and, strictly speaking, it is not in the cones either. The cones deliver three ambiguous catches per point of light; the brain reads the ratios between them and paints. The first series also showed how all of this is probed at the chairside, in episode nine of season six, Colour Vision Testing. Notice what those tests are really doing, now that you know the hardware: they arrange comparisons. Every colour test sets up a controlled disagreement, patches that one observer separates and another cannot, precisely because the tests interrogate the ratios between the three catches. How the system varies and fails is another door, and it stays closed today. For now, keep the connection: the tests work because colour lives in comparisons, and comparisons can be rigged.

Host: Bring this to the chair one more time, because our listeners check vision and colour every working week. What should today's specification sheets change about how a student reads those routine findings?

Guide: Three quiet consequences are worth carrying into practice. First, acuity and colour resolution are different currencies, because they rest on different populations. The letter resolved at the centre is read mainly by the dense long and middle cones, while colour comparisons ride on mosaics with very different headcounts, including a short-wave class that is one in twelve or fewer and missing at the fixated point altogether. So excellent central detail with coarse performance on some colour axes is not a contradiction; it is the expected output of this design. Second, colour is not uniform across the visual field. The centre is rich in red-green comparisons and poor in blue ones, and the periphery is poorer in colour altogether, a direct consequence of cone density rather than any failure. Third, brightness and colour travel on separate ledgers. Because the broadly tuned long and middle cones collect most of the daylight photons, the total catch reports brightness while the ratios report colour, and the two can be read independently downstream, which is why a colour can look unchanged while its brightness fades, and why brightness can survive where colour detail cannot. None of this requires memorising curves. It requires one habit: when a colour finding seems odd, ask which of the three witnesses, or which comparison, the finding actually tested. That single question turns colour data from trivia into physiology.

Host: Good. Then there is one door left deliberately shut today, and I want it named before we close the file. What did we not do?

Guide: We never proved the large claim hiding under everything we said: that three numbers are enough. Today established that three differently tuned cones deliver three numbers per point of light, and that their ratios carry the wavelength. It is the episode where the specification sheets you heard today harden into a law of colour vision, and where the phrase trichromatic finally earns its meaning. Bring your three witnesses with you.
