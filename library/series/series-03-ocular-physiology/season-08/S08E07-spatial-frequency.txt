# Description: Spatial frequency is the ruler vision measures with, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks teaches it for the ear. Learn how stripes of light and dark, counted in cycles per degree, give every scene a scale from broad and coarse to tight and fine, with your thumbnail at arm's length marking one degree of view. Hear how any picture, however complex, can be built by adding stripe patterns of different finenesses and strengths together, and how a family of neural channels in the visual system reads that stripe recipe out of every image you see. The episode also shows why the contrast sensitivity curve has its humped shape, and why the acuity chart asks about only one rung of the scale ladder. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want the physiology of detail and scale explained in plain words.

That little nail hanging in the air is the ruler your vision measures with. Everything this season has been saying about contrast, and everything we say tonight, gets measured against a patch of the world about that wide, and once you know the trick you will never look at a scene the same way again. Last episode, Contrast Sensitivity, asked how faint a difference can be and still be seen. Tonight we ask a different question about the same world: how coarse is the layout, how fine is the detail, and who in your head is keeping track of both at once? Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology, where we are midway through Season 8, Colour and Contrast Vision. This is episode seven, and its title is two plain words carrying one big idea: Spatial Frequency. Set the ruler on the table for us. What is a degree, and why is angle the only yardstick sight can honestly use?

Guide: Start with the degree, because without it there is no unit at all. You do not see the world in metres and centimetres; you see it in angle. A tree across the street and a twig at your nose can cover the same angle of your view, and to your visual system those two are the same size, because each casts an image of the same size on the retina, the light-catching screen at the back of the eye. The degree is the natural unit of that angle: a full circle contains three hundred and sixty degrees, and the whole scene in front of you, eyes still, from far left to far right, spans a little under two hundred of them. Hold your whole hand upright at arm's length and it spans roughly ten degrees, so the hand becomes a rough ten-degree ruler once the thumbnail has given you the single degree. The handy everyday anchor is the one you are holding right now: a thumbnail at arm's length covers about one degree of your view. The full moon covers about half a degree. Every size your retina ever hears about is an angular size, measured in degrees of view. So a ruler for how coarse or fine the visible world is has to be marked in those same degrees, and that ruler has a name: spatial frequency.

Host: Now the phrase itself, in plain words before any jargon settles in. Spatial frequency. Most listeners have never heard those two words joined. Start with what counts as a stripe, because I suspect the entire unit hangs on that.

Guide: A stripe, for our purposes, is a band of lighter lying beside a band of darker. Picture a zebra crossing seen from a high window, a picket fence, a striped shirt, the jersey of a football referee. The repeating unit is one light band plus one dark band together, and that repeating unit is the thing we count: one cycle. Now the unit assembles itself. Spatial frequency is how many of those light-and-dark cycles fit into one degree of your view. Broad, gentle stripes, the kind a single hill makes against the sky, may not complete even one cycle in a degree: that is low spatial frequency, the coarse end. Tight, fine stripes, like the weave of a shirt cuff held close, may pack twenty cycles or more into a single degree: that is high spatial frequency, the fine end. And the word spatial is doing real work, because it marks this as counting across space, from place to place in the image. You have already met its twin. Last season, in Season 7, episode five, Temporal Resolution, we counted flickers per second, brightening and dimming repeated in time. Tonight we count stripes per degree: the same idea, rotated from time into space.

Host: Give me anchors, though, because numbers only stick for me when they carry textures. What does the middle of this scale feel like, and what everyday things live at the different rungs of the ladder?

Guide: The ladder is everywhere once you know to look for it. The bottom rungs carry the broad statements a scene makes: the sweep of a wall from shadow into light, the dark mass of a tree against the sky, the overall shape of a face. These are layouts. The middle rungs hold the features that give objects their identity: the courses of a brick wall, the pickets of a fence, the buttons and seams of a coat. The top rungs hold fineness itself: the weave of fabric, the stubble on a jaw, the threads of a spider's web seen close, the strokes and gaps inside small print. Or take a roof: across the street the tiles read as one even texture, from an upstairs window each course of tiles becomes a stripe pattern, and from the ladder every tile edge stands as an individual line. Same roof, three rungs. And here is the twist worth keeping: the ladder is not fixed to the object alone. It belongs to the object and your viewing distance together. Walk towards that brick wall and its courses spread across more degrees of view, sliding down the ladder towards coarse. Walk away and the same wall packs its stripes into fewer degrees, sliding up towards fine. Distance is a converter of spatial frequency. It is why a face across the street reads as smooth, coarse content, while the eyelashes that are fine content at speaking distance simply drop out of the recipe.

Host: Take something our listeners stare at every working day: print. Walk me up the ladder through a single page of text, from the shape of the paragraph down to the strokes of a letter, and tell me what happens when the page moves further away.

Guide: A page of print is a lovely ladder all by itself. Squint until the words blur and you see the coarsest content first: the pale rectangle of the page, the grey blocks where the paragraphs sit. Those blocks are broad light-and-dark patterns, low on the scale. Step up a rung and the blocks resolve into lines of text, a stripe pattern with a fineness of its own in the middle of the range. Step up again and the lines resolve into letters, and the letters are built from fine stripes: a vertical stroke is a light-to-dark change, the gap beside it is its partner, and the smallest print asks the sharpest questions about fine stripes. Now push the page away. Every pattern on it covers fewer degrees of view than before, so every rung of its recipe slides upwards: the paragraph blocks stay comfortable, the lines tighten, and the letter strokes race up past the fine end. Push far enough and the fine content climbs beyond what the eye can carry at all, and the page melts into grey texture, which is exactly what a distant poster does. Printed photographs play the same trick in reverse: come close and they resolve into a fine grid of tiny ink points, but step back and that fine stripe pattern vanishes into smooth tone, its fineness pushed beyond the eye's reach while the coarser picture it carries remains. Fineness was never a property of the ink alone. It was always ink and distance written together.

Host: That raises the question underneath the whole episode for me. Why should a brain bother sorting a scene by scale at all? What job does the coarse content of a scene do that the fine cannot, and the other way round?

Guide: Because the two scales answer different questions you need answered at the same time. The coarse content tells you where you are: floor, walls, furniture, the big shapes and their arrangement, the gist of the scene in a single glance. The fine content tells you what you are looking at: which object is which, whether the letter is a b or a d, whether that face is the friend you arranged to meet. You can hear the division in an old phrase: not seeing the wood for the trees. The wood is the coarse statement, the trees are the fine ones, and a working visual system delivers both at once. It is also why a scene glimpsed for a blink is still meaningful: the coarse content arrives with the layout intact even when the fine detail never gets a look in, and you can name a room, a street or a landscape from that broad stroke alone. Cook in an unfamiliar kitchen and you steer by the coarse content, counter, stove and doorway, while the fine content waits for the moment your hand reaches for the right knob. Scale is not decoration in vision. It is the first sorting: layout here, detail there, and every judgement you make about a scene draws on its own band of that recipe.

Host: Now the deep idea, the one this title is really standing on. You have said that any scene, however complicated, can be built out of stripes of different finenesses added together. The first time I heard that, it sounded like a party trick. Make me believe it, in words, with no mathematics.

Guide: Let me build it from the easy cases upwards. A soft shadow lying across a plain wall is about the simplest scene there is, and it is already a stripe: one long, gentle change from light to dark, a single very coarse cycle. Add an object with an edge: a dark book on a pale shelf. That edge is a stripe too, finer and sharper than the shadow, living higher up the scale. Keep going. The wood grain of the shelf adds a family of middle rungs. The lettering on the book's spine adds fine ones. A portrait does the same: the soft gradient of a cheek is one broad coarse sweep, the line of the jaw is a mid-scale change, and the individual strands of the hair are among the finest stripes in the picture. A real scene is this, repeated in every direction: broad gentle changes for the layout, and sharper changes at every scale for edges, textures and detail. So the claim is only this: any scene can be built by adding stripe patterns of different finenesses and strengths together. Picture a piano. One key pressed is one note. A chord is several notes sounded together, each at its own loudness. A picture is a chord in which every note is a stripe pattern of a particular fineness, played at a loudness set by how strong its dark-against-light difference is. The full list of finenesses and their loudnesses is the scene's recipe of stripes. There is real mathematics underneath this idea and it works beautifully, but you never need a single symbol to hold the thought: every image is a chord of stripe recipes.

Host: A composer builds the chord from notes; that is the direction you just described, stripes into scene. But eyes are handed finished scenes and have to do the reverse: hear the chord and name the notes. Is pulling a scene apart into its stripe recipe what the visual system actually does?

Guide: That reversal is exactly what happens, and it happens in hardware, not metaphor. Inside the visual system there is a family of neural channels, and each channel is tuned to a narrow band of fineness. This is measured physiology, not a picture I am painting for convenience. Cells at the early cortical stages of vision respond most vigorously when the image falling on their small patch of the view contains stripes near a particular fineness, and they respond less and less as the stripes move away from that favourite. One channel is at its best for broad stripes, its neighbour for slightly finer ones, and so on up the scale. Fineness is only half of each channel's taste, because every one also prefers stripes of a particular orientation, vertical or horizontal or one tilt between, so the full bank is a lattice of fineness and tilt; tonight we stay on the fineness axis, which is the axis the contrast curve is drawn along. Each behaves like a sieve with its own mesh, passing stripes near its tuning and ignoring everything else. Show a scene to the whole family and every sieve reports how much of its own fineness the scene contains: the recipe, recovered from the picture. Analysis is the honest word: the brain analyses each scene into stripe content. And you can catch the family at work on yourself tonight. Stare at broad stripes for half a minute, then look at medium stripes: they briefly seem finer than they really are, because the broad-preferring channel is tired and the balance of evidence has shifted. The aftereffect is the tuning itself, demonstrated on your own head.

Host: Why bands at all, though? Why not one detector per fineness, or one grand detector covering everything? What does a handful of overlapping sieves buy that those two extremes do not?

Guide: Economy and coverage, in a single design. One grand detector for all finenesses could not work: a receiver tuned to everything distinguishes nothing, and the recipe would be lost. One detector per fineness, on the other hand, would be ruinously expensive, millions of separate lines for every patch of view. A handful of overlapping bands is the workable middle: few enough to be affordable, and overlapping enough that no fineness falls between two neighbours. The bands are even neatly spaced in size, each one tuned about a doubling of fineness away from its neighbour, so a short ladder of doublings covers the whole span without a gap. Each band is a little tolerant, responding best at its favourite but still speaking for the finenesses beside it, and the brain reads fineness from which bands are talking and how loudly. The overlap has a second gift: in the middle of the scale, where several bands respond at once, the family is at its most sensitive, and that fact will matter enormously when we put the contrast curve on the table in a few minutes. A chord needs only a few notes to be rich, and the family works the same way: a modest set of tuned bands, played together, carries the whole scale.

Host: How big is the family, then? Am I carrying two channels and a spare, or dozens? And what is the full span they cover, from the broadest stripe they bother with to the finest they can register?

Guide: At any one patch of your view the family is a handful: at the fovea, the central pit of sharpest vision, the literature commonly describes somewhere between four and eight distinct tuned bands. Between them, those few cover an enormous span. At the coarse end, channels answer to stripes so broad that a single light-and-dark cycle can stretch across many degrees of view, a wash of light covering half a wall. At the fine end, a young healthy eye carries stripes so tight that fifty or sixty full cycles are packed into a single degree: the fineness of small print at reading distance, and finer. For a feel of the top rung, the finest strokes and gaps on an acuity chart, the ones that mark ordinary sharpness when the chart is read at its proper distance, sit near thirty cycles per degree, and the best young eyes carry a little beyond that. Coarsest to finest, that is roughly a hundred-fold range. And the top of the span is not a free choice; it is capped by sampling. You already know the cap from Season 7, episode four, Spatial Resolution: the cones of the foveal mosaic sit at a fixed pitch, and a sampling grid cannot honestly carry stripes finer than its own spacing. Series 2, season 8, episode four, Photoreceptor Distribution, showed where those cones crowd together and where they thin out. The sampling limit measured in those episodes is simply the top rung of tonight's ladder: the finest stripe the family owns.

Host: Then let me put last episode on the table next to this, because I think the two have just shaken hands. The contrast sensitivity curve we walked in episode six had that memorable hump: sensitivity best in the middle finenesses, falling away towards both ends, coarse on one side and fine on the other. Last time we described the curve. With the family in hand, can we finally say why it has that shape at all?

Guide: We can, and it is the most satisfying sentence in the episode: the hump is the family portrait. That curve has history, too. Series 1, episode eight of season three, Contrast Sensitivity, introduced it as a pure measurement of faintness against fineness, and last episode gave it its modern shape. Tonight the physiology finally explains it. Every point on that curve asks one question, how faint can a stripe of this fineness be and still be seen, and every question is answered by whichever channel is tuned to that fineness. In the middle of the scale several channels overlap and each is working near its best, so sensitivity climbs to its peak, exactly where the hump sits. Towards the coarse end the nervous system carries less of that content: few channels prefer broad washes, so a faint coarse stripe goes unheard, and sensitivity sags. Towards the fine end two brakes press at once: the eye's own optics blur fine stripes before they ever reach the receptors, and the sampling cap we just named leaves no channels for the very finest of all. Sensitivity sags again. Add the tunings of the whole family together, rung by rung, and the humped curve of episode six is what falls out. That is why the curve has a shape at all: it is many channels stacked, not one all-purpose machine, and a single detector tuned to everything would have traced a flat, dull line. The hump is evidence that you see with a bank of sieves.

Host: One more structural question before we take this to the chair. Does every patch of your view carry the same family? When something sits off to the side, out in the periphery, is that patch running the same roster of channels as the centre?

Guide: The roster is local, and it changes across the retina. Every patch of the visual field has its own set of channels, and the sets are not identical. At the fovea you get the full range, from the coarse rungs to the very finest. Move out towards the periphery and sampling coarsens, with receptors spaced wider apart and gathering from larger areas, so the fine rungs are simply not staffed: the peripheral family runs coarse. This is the channel-level reason that fine detail lives at the centre of gaze. You cannot read with your periphery, thread a needle with your periphery, or judge the weave of fabric off to the side, not because those regions are lazy, but because the channels for that fineness do not exist out there to be used. Point the fovea at the task and the fine channels come on line. Watch a driver at dusk: the roadside sign is a coarse smudge while it approaches in the periphery, and one glance brings the fovea round so that the lettering snaps into its fine content. So the honest picture of a single visual moment is not one scene seen at one scale. It is a wide field played coarse at the edges and fine at the centre, all at once: periphery carrying layout, centre carrying detail, and the family everywhere in between playing its own band of the chord.

Host: Let us cash this in at the chair, because our listeners are optometrists and students, and vocabulary only earns its keep on a Monday morning. The acuity chart: every consulting room has one. In stripe language, what is that chart actually asking, and what does it leave unasked?

Guide: The acuity chart is a question about a single rung of the ladder. The strokes of a letter and the gaps between them are fine stripes near the very top of the range, and reading the smallest line asks whether the finest channels still carry their content. Series 1, season three, episode two, Visual Acuity, laid out that logic, and it is a fine question to ask of the system. But it is one rung. The contrast sensitivity story of episode six samples the whole ladder, fineness by fineness, which is why the two tests are companions and not duplicates: acuity probes the finest stripe the family owns, while contrast sensitivity asks how well every scale is carried. It is also why the letters on a chart are chosen with different stroke weights, since a thick-stroke letter is a gentler question on the ladder than a thin-stroke one of the same height. Daily life runs on the whole ladder too. Walking through an unfamiliar room leans on coarse content, walls and floor and furniture edges. Reading leans on the fine rungs. Recognising a face across a room mixes both, the broad shape first and the features after. And when a patient says textures seem to be fading, or that the world looks somehow flatter and less crisp, the physiology underneath those words lives in some band of the stripe recipe, and tonight's scale language is what turns that vague complaint into something describable.

Host: One detail from the consulting room before the summary, because it used to puzzle me as a student. The chart hangs on one wall, and the chair sits at one marked distance. What does that marking on the floor have to do with tonight's ruler?

Guide: Everything, and it is the neatest practical echo of the episode. The fineness of the chart's stripes is fixed by the letters and the distance together, exactly as the brick wall's stripes were fixed by the wall and where you stand. Move the chair nearer and the same smallest line covers more degrees of view, its strokes slide down the ladder, and the test quietly becomes easier. Move further away and the same letters climb towards the fine end, and the test becomes harder. That is why the distance is measured and why the chair position is marked, and it is why the same chart can be reprinted at a different scale for a shorter room: a scaled chart at a scaled distance asks the same stripe question. When you next walk into a consulting room, look at that fixed chair position with tonight's vocabulary in mind. The clinic has pinned down, literally to a mark on the floor, the fineness of the question it intends to ask.

Spatial frequency is the scale language of vision: coarse for layout, fine for detail, and every scale in between accounted for by its own channel.
