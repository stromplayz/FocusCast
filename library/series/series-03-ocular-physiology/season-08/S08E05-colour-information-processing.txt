# Description: How does a colour signal travel from the eye to the brain, and how does the brain keep colours steady when the light itself keeps changing. This episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks follows the opponent channels out of the retina, through the sorting gateway and into the cortical areas where colour comparisons are computed over wide neighbourhoods. Host: Good. Stage one, then, the departure. Last time the opponent comparisons were assembled: red weighed against green, blue against yellow, and the brightness channel running beside them. Now I am looking at a red flower against green leaves. What physically leaves my eye in that moment? What is actually in the cable?

Guide: Not a palette. That is the first and biggest thing to unlearn. The retina never ships the colour red, or green, or any named hue, because no neuron anywhere carries such a thing. What leaves the eye is a bundle of differences, and it leaves in parallel families, exactly the shipping pattern we learned in Season 6, episode nine, Retinal Ganglion Cells. The red-versus-green comparison rides mainly in the midget family, that great population of small-field, sustained, detail-loving cells that fills most of the optic nerve. The blue-versus-yellow comparison rides in its own smaller set of cells with a distinctive branching shape, a separate lane for a separate question. The brightness channel, black weighed against white, rides in the biggest and fastest families, because luminance differences carry the detail and the timing. So picture the flower's patch of retina reporting: redness exceeding greenness by such a margin. The leaf beside it reporting: greenness exceeding redness. Above both, a third report: how much light each patch caught compared with its neighbours. Notice what all three families share: every one of them answers a question of the form, how much more of this than that. No family ever reports an absolute. The retina, from its very first output onward, speaks only in comparisons. Three kinds of comparison, already computed, already discrete, already in the ganglion cells' vocabulary, and all of them heading for the optic nerve together. Colour, at the moment of departure, is not a set of paints. It is a set of argued differences.

Host: And why differences? I asked a version of this question in earlier seasons about edges and contrast, and the answer there was efficiency. Is it the same answer here, or is there something special about colour that makes the difference code the right one?

Guide: The same answer, and one more. Efficiency first, because the logic never changed, and Season 6's finale, episode ten, Retinal Information Coding, made the full case. Three raw cone signals carry a great deal of redundancy: all three shift together whenever the overall light brightens or dims, so shipping them raw would spend cable on information the brain does not want. Convert them to differences and the redundant part, the overall level, is set aside, and what remains is precisely the part that identifies surfaces. That is the same move that made centre-versus-surround the retina's native language. Now the extra reason, and it belongs to today's destination. A difference code is a code built for further comparison. The cortex will take these red-versus-green and blue-versus-yellow reports and compare them again, across wider and wider neighbourhoods, and those comparisons only work because the currency arriving is already comparative. You cannot easily compare measurements anchored to a light source you cannot see. You can compare differences. So the opponent code is not just tidier packaging; it is the correct raw material for the brain's final corrections. The signal that leaves the eye is ready for exactly the work it will meet, and today we follow it to that work.

Host: Ready, then, and on the road. Stage two is the gateway. Every other visual stream we have followed through this series got sorted somewhere on the way up. Does colour get the same treatment, or does it travel by some private route of its own?

Guide: The same treatment, and that sameness is the point. The colour channels join every other stream at the lateral geniculate body, the small egg-shaped sorting station halfway up the road to the cortex, and Series 2 walked the whole road in episode ten of season ten, Complete Visual Pathway. Six floors it has in the classic account, and each floor answers to one eye, so the left-eye and right-eye versions of the world stay separated while they pass through. The maps on those floors are stacked in register, point matching point, so the sorting rearranges nothing about where things are. What does the gateway add? Regulation, not interpretation. Fibres descending from the cortex vastly outnumber the ones arriving, and brainstem state sets the gain, so the gateway amplifies what attention and arousal demand and lets the rest pass quietly. The colour lanes ride all of this like any other cargo: the red-versus-green family keeps its sustained, fine-grained character, the blue-versus-yellow family keeps its own smaller, slower lane, and nothing about the code is translated at the gateway. It is sorted, boosted where needed, and forwarded, and from the gateway every lane, colour included, fans out through the radiations toward the back of the head, each lane still carrying its own patch of the map. For colour, the journey so far is deliberately boring, and the design intent is easy to admire: change nothing yet, and save the real work for the cortex.

Host: Which brings us to stage three, the arrival. Remind us, in one clause, of the ground we are walking into, and then take me through the door.

Guide: In one clause: you met this ground in Series 2, episode six of season ten, Visual Cortex, which mapped the point-by-point sheet of tissue at the back of the head where the visual world is laid out again, this time on cortex. Now watch what happens to colour on that sheet. Within the first map, threaded among the cells that answer edges and motion, sit specialised clusters whose cells answer hue questions for the patch they serve: a spot of cortex asking red or green, another asking blue or yellow. The map's foveal privilege is colour's privilege too, and the richest hue comparisons live where the map is finest. But the decisive step, the one today is named for, is what happens next. As the colour reports climb onward from the first map, they enter the route we walked in Season 7, episode nine, Visual Information Processing, the what pathway running downward toward the temporal lobe, and along that route the neighbourhoods widen. Early cells compare a patch against its immediate ring. Higher cells pool reports across larger territories, then larger ones again, until a cell's answer depends on a whole region of the scene and how its many patches weigh against one another. A wide neighbourhood cannot be computed in the cable, because a one-to-one relay has no room for a cell to ask about its neighbours, so the widening waits for the place where the brain can wire sideways, and that place is the cortex. Colour is computed over contexts. That sentence is the hinge of the whole episode, because a comparison over a wide neighbourhood is exactly the instrument you need for the correction we are building toward.

Host: Let me make sure the routing is clear, because last season's two roads still matter here. Which stream carries colour, and what does colour do for each of the two?

Guide: Colour is, above all, a discrimination cargo, so it rides the lanes that feed the what pathway, the ventral stream, the road toward the temporal lobe that assembles identity. That fits its job. Recognising the banana, the face, the ripe versus the unripe, is identity work, and stable colour is one of identity's strongest clues. The where and how pathway, running upward toward the parietal lobe for location, motion and action, draws mainly on the brightness channels, which carry the timing and the geometry. But do not box colour in too tightly, because the cortex is a market, not a set of silos. Colour information serves everywhere it is useful, and one of its oldest useful jobs is drawing boundaries. We saw a hint of this in Season 7's edge story: a red flower against green leaves can put almost equal light in the eye on both sides of its outline, so the brightness comparison finds no edge there, and a hue comparison must draw the line instead. Camouflage works by matching both comparisons at once. So the honest answer is that colour rides the discrimination stream as its main road and walks everywhere else as a working passenger. What matters today is where the main computation happens, and it happens in the widening neighbourhoods of the recognition road.

Host: Stage four, then, and the star of the show. Let me set the scene again, stated properly as a problem this time. Seven in the morning, the kitchen window faces east, and the light in the room is cool and blue-leaning. At eight, white daylight floods in. At nine you are in the office under a lamp with a greenish tint. Tonight, candles. The light bathing the fruit bowl has swung through four different colour balances in a single day, and the banana has stayed yellow through all of them. Before you give me the mechanism, tell me why this is genuinely hard. Why should we have expected the banana to change?

Guide: Because of what the eye actually receives. The retina never sees surfaces and light separately; it sees only their product. Every patch of the scene sends the eye a single combined report: surface times illuminant. A banana surface reflecting golden morning light and a paler banana surface reflecting white noon light can produce the very same arriving signal. The information that would let the eye separate the two factors was discarded the moment the light bounced, and no measurement made at the retina can get it back. Follow the arithmetic through the day and the naive prediction is stark. If the banana's report is surface times illuminant, then when the illuminant swings from blue-leaning morning to golden candlelight, the arriving signal for the banana should swing with it, hour by hour, from cool to warm to something odd under the greenish lamp. The brain should experience a fruit that cycles through a day's worth of tints. It does not, and the failure of that prediction is the phenomenon. And notice why the design is worth having at all: the useful constants in this world are surfaces, not lights. Fruit ripens under a moving sun, a friend's face is the same face at dawn and at dusk, and the light is just weather passing over the things we need to recognise and act on. Colour constancy is the name of the correction that defeats the arithmetic: the banana stays recognisably yellow, the white page stays white under a gold bulb and under a blue sky, and your own hand looks like your hand indoors and out. Something in the system is undoing the illuminant.

Host: Undoing it, yes. And since nothing in this system is magic, the correction must be computed from the only material available: the differences we shipped at stage one. Sketch the mechanism in plain words. How does the brain subtract a light source it has never seen?

Guide: With comparison, over wide neighbourhoods, which is why the cortex builds them. Here is the sketch, in honest steps. Step one: the brain reads each patch against its surroundings. The banana patch is read together with the table around it, the wall behind it, the shadows across it. Step two: the brain estimates the scene's average light, the overall tint the illuminant has laid across everything, by weighing all the patches against one another. Many different surfaces lit by one lamp share that lamp's bias, so the bias is visible in the ensemble even though it is invisible in any single patch. Step three: the brain discounts that estimate. It subtracts the inferred tint from every patch, the way you would subtract a known filter from a photograph, and what survives the subtraction is reported as the surface. Be gentle with the word subtract, though, because the brain is not doing schoolroom arithmetic on light. It weighs the comparisons until the best explanation, one set of surfaces under one light, is settled on; the subtraction is a story that stands in for that inference. That is the whole trick, and it is why the same word, comparison, has followed us from the retina to the cortex: the retina compared cones, and the cortex compares the comparisons, over neighbourhoods wide enough to contain the light's fingerprint. The correction is even local enough to handle shadows, because a patch lying in shadow is compared within its own darker, bluer corner of the scene, so shadows read as shadows on a surface, not as sudden repaintings of it. Cameras borrowed the idea honestly; their white balance setting does by arithmetic what your cortex does continuously and without being asked. The snow by the door is white in the bluish morning and white at golden dusk because the brain has subtracted the morning and the dusk.

Host: I want to catch this machinery at work, because a description this elegant deserves evidence. Are there demonstrations where the correction is visibly running, ideally ones a student could set up on a desk tonight?

Guide: The best ones cost almost nothing. First, simultaneous colour contrast. Cut a grey patch from neutral paper and place it in the middle of a bright red field, and the grey leans subtly toward green, the red's opponent. Move the identical grey onto a green field and it leans toward red. Nothing about the paper changed; the comparison neighbourhoods changed. Second, the same demonstration played out across whole scenes is the reason the same grey patch can look two different colours against two different backgrounds, a result we will name honestly when we count the cost. Third, afterimages: stare at a red square for twenty seconds, look away to a white page, and a greenish ghost appears, the tired comparison rebalancing itself the moment the evidence stops. Fourth, a scene lit by two different lamps at once, say a warm bulb and a cool window falling on the same table: the shadow cast by one lamp is lit only by the other, and it does not look black. The correction runs there too, on the one light that still reaches it, and the result is the famous faintly tinted shadow. Fifth, the modern classic: a photograph of a garment that splits a room in two, because the picture strips away almost every cue about the light it was taken in, and half the brain's population infers one illuminant while the other half infers another, each camp seeing a different pair of colours with total confidence. Notice what every one of these has in common: they are not malfunctions. They are the discounting machinery running exactly as built, on contexts that make its inference visible. The machinery is normally invisible precisely because it works.

Host: You used the word cost, and I promised the audience we would pay it honestly. Name the cost of colour constancy, plainly.

Guide: Here it is. The system does not report reflections; it reports surface guesses. Constancy is an inference, and an inference is only as good as the context it reads. When the context genuinely tells the brain what the light is, the guess is superb, and the banana stays yellow across a day of light you would swear should repaint it. But the very same comparison that rescues the banana also means the brain's answer depends on the surroundings, which is why one grey patch can wear two different colours in a single display, and why two surfaces carrying different light can be reported as the same colour under different lamps. That is the trade the visual system made, and it was the right trade. A brain that reported raw reflections would give you a world whose objects change colour with every cloud and every lamp, identity dissolved hour by hour; a brain that reports surfaces gives you a stable world and pays for it with occasional visible guesses. There is a second, quieter cost worth naming: constancy takes a moment. Walk indoors from bright daylight and the world looks briefly tinted while the estimate re-runs; the correction is fast, but it is not instant, and that lag is the inference doing its work in real time. Illusions, in this frame, are design features caught in the act: the seams of a system optimised for meaning rather than measurement. And one quiet bonus for the philosopher in you: the colour you call a surface's true colour is itself a convention, anchored to the wide, white light our species lives most of its days under.

Host: Now bring it to the chair, because our listeners are optometrists and students, and Tuesday is coming. What does colour information processing change about ordinary practice?

Guide: It changes one habit, and the habit is worth the whole episode. Every colour judgement you or a patient makes is an inference about a surface, made from the light you are both standing in, so control the light before you trust the colour. The consulting room lamp, the window, the corridor outside: each sets a different inferred illuminant, and the same wall, the same garment, the same iris will be described differently across them, by normal, healthy visual systems working exactly as designed. Patients will tell you, truthfully, that a shirt matched under shop lighting looks different by the window at home, and now you can explain that neither report is wrong; each is a correct correction for a different light. Expect your own inferences to re-zero within moments whenever you move between rooms with different lamps, because adaptation never sleeps. The same principle explains why colour-matching tasks, from advising on a frame colour to sorting test materials, go better beside a window in even daylight than under a single warm lamp, and why two rooms can quietly disagree about the very same coat. The practical moral is the one examiners love and clinics forget: when colour matters to a judgement, use a steady, even, consistent light source, and use the same one every time, so that the inference your system performs sits on a constant footing. One fence sentence and we move on: what happens when the machinery is built differently in a given person belongs to other conversations; today was the machine at its standard settings.
