Guide: Thank you, good to be back. The frame comes in two definitions, and both are easier than they sound. The first is the threshold. Show a pattern and fade it, step by step, until the observer says it has just vanished. The faintest version of that pattern still visible at the moment before vanishing is the threshold, the boundary between seen and unseen. The second definition is its reciprocal, sensitivity, which is simply one divided by the threshold. If a pattern must be strong to be seen at all, the threshold is high and sensitivity is low. If a pattern can be nearly invisible and still be seen, the threshold is low and sensitivity is high. So sensitivity is a score for faintness: high scores mean the eye hears whispers, low scores mean it needs shouts. You will remember from Series 3, season seven, episode six, Contrast Processing, that differences are the currency the visual system trades in, not light levels on their own. That episode taught the principle. Tonight we do the bookkeeping: for every fineness of pattern the eye can face, we record the faintest difference it can catch, and the ledger we draw from those records is the subject of this hour.

Host: Before the ledger, a word about the tools, because you have chosen a very particular test pattern: stripes. Not a single dim spot, not a glowing disc, but alternating light and dark bars, filling the screen from edge to edge. Why stripes? What does a stripe pattern buy the measurement that a simple spot does not? And when we say a stripe pattern, tell me exactly what is held constant and what is varied, because I suspect the whole method hides in that answer.

Guide: Stripes buy the measurement two things, and the first is control of one variable at a time. A single spot mixes its questions together: is it faint, or is it small, or is it in the wrong place? A grating, which is the formal name for these bar patterns, lets us hold everything steady except one dial. Every bar is the same width across the whole field, so the pattern has one fineness, one coarseness, one repeat distance from bar to bar. And the brightness of the whole pattern averages out to the same grey whether the bars are bold or whispering. What changes is only how far the light bars rise above that average and how far the dark bars fall below it. That rise and fall, taken as a fraction of the average, is the contrast, and it can be turned down smoothly all the way to zero, where the screen becomes even grey. So a grating lets us ask the eye one clean question at a time: at this stripe width, how faint can the difference get before you lose it? Change the width, ask again. That is the whole experimental idea, and it is why stripes, of all the patterns we could have chosen, carry the measurement.

Host: Then let me live through one measurement, because I want to feel it as an experience and not a definition. The threshold lives exactly inside that wobble, and careful measurements handle it with repeated trials and honest guessing rules, but the heart of the matter is simple. We record the contrast at which the pattern hovers at the edge of visibility, the faintest version that can still be reported more often than chance. Host: And here is where I want numbers, because the phrase faintest visible difference can float away on us. Guide: At the sweet spot, the numbers are startling. In good light a healthy young adult can see a stripe pattern whose bright and dark bars differ by only about one part in a hundred of their average brightness, and at the very best widths the margin shrinks further still, toward one part in two hundred. Put plainly, the bars can differ by less than one percent and still be seen. Now walk to the edges of the page. At the coarsest widths and the finest widths the threshold climbs steeply, ten times higher and more, until a pattern must be nearly half white and half black before the eye concedes that it is there at all. That is the span the ledger covers: from differences so faint they flirt with invisibility on an ordinary display, to differences as blunt as a chessboard. And between those limits the eye covers nearly everything daily life presents to it. Hold both ends in mind at once, because the shape traced between them is the curve we are chasing tonight.

Host: Now the shape. We have a page of pairs: stripe width on one side, faintest visible contrast on the other. Turn the numbers into a picture for me, and start at the fine end. As the stripes grow thinner and thinner, what does the threshold do, and where does the story end? I ask partly because I suspect the fine end connects to an old friend of this library, the acuity measurement, and I want to see the handshake.

Guide: Start at the fine end and the threshold is high: very thin stripes need strong contrast to be seen, and as they grow thinner the needed contrast climbs faster and faster. Sensitivity, the reciprocal, falls the same way, which is why the curve's right-hand side slides downhill toward the floor. And then it ends, absolutely. There is a stripe width so fine that no contrast whatsoever brings it back, no matter how black the dark bars and how white the light bars. The pattern simply cannot be seen, because the bars have grown finer than the eye's sampling can honour, the point where the optics have blurred them and the cone mosaic has run out of resolution. That terminal width is the acuity limit, the finest detail the eye can resolve at all. There, sensitivity reaches zero, and the curve stops.

Host: A hard edge at one end, then. Now take me to the other end, the broad end, where the bars are wide and lazy. Intuition says broad stripes should be the easiest of all, since they are big and obvious. Does the curve oblige? And describe what a near-threshold coarse pattern actually looks like to the observer, because I suspect it fails in a strange way rather than a simple one.

Guide: Intuition fails, and the failure is instructive. At the broad end the threshold climbs again: the bars must differ strongly before the pattern shows itself. And the way a coarse pattern fails is strange, exactly as you suspected. Fade the contrast of very wide bars and you do not watch the stripes dissolve bar by bar into a grey blur. Instead the whole field simply looks evenly lit: a grey screen that brightens and dims a little as the display is adjusted, but with no structure inside it at all. The eye reports light, not pattern. It makes sense the moment you say it aloud. When a bar is broader than the comparisons the visual system makes, the bright bar and its neighbouring dark bar are too far apart to be weighed against each other, and without a comparison there is no pattern, only a level of light. Somewhere down at that end, the stripe pattern and the blank grey screen become the same report. So the curve, which fell off a cliff at the fine end, now slides gently toward the floor at the broad end. Both extremes cost the eye dearly, and for opposite reasons.

Host: Between a cliff on the right and a slide on the left, something must sit on top. Guide: The peak sits in the middle, and the everyday anchor is this: hold your thumb out at arm's length against a stripe pattern, and the widths your eye is most sensitive to are the ones where a few stripes, three or four or five, fit across the width of your thumbnail. That is the sweet spot. On either side of it the curve falls away, steeply toward the fine end where we watched it drop to zero, gently toward the broad end where the field goes uniform. And the height difference is dramatic. At the peak the eye catches differences near one part in a hundred or better, while at the ends it may need twenty, fifty, even a hundred times more contrast before it reports anything. This hump is the signature shape of the measured eye, and it deserves a pause, because a curve that rises, peaks and falls on both sides is telling you something about design. A system built for a single task would show a single slope. A hump with a specific middle says the machinery underneath comes in sizes, and one size fits best. Host: Then walk through that doorway with me, because the shape demands an explanation. Why would middling stripes be the cheap ones, the ones seen at whispers, while both extremes, the very fine and the very broad, cost shouting? Guide: The callback is real, and it is the heart of the hour. Series 3, season seven, episode three, Centre-Surround Processing, taught the retina's favourite comparison: a ganglion cell whose small central receptive field is weighed against an antagonistic ring around it, centre against surround, every cell asking how its own patch differs from its neighbourhood. That comparison has a size. A unit with a small centre is a fine-grained instrument, brilliant at differences that change quickly across space and blind to slow ones. A unit with a large centre embraces more territory, perfect for broad, gradual differences and hopeless for hairline ones. Now match the machinery to the patterns. When a stripe's width matches the size of the comparison units doing the weighing, excitation and inhibition line up cleanly and the difference is reported at full strength, so a whisper of contrast is enough. When the stripes are finer than a unit's centre, the bars wash together inside one field and cancel. Guide: Many, and that is the deepest point of the night. The retina and cortex do not run a single comparison size. Each family answers only for the widths it was built for, catching its own band of fineness and ignoring the rest. Now the curve rereads itself. The measured sensitivity at any stripe width is not one instrument's answer but the best answer among all the families listening at that width. The smooth hump we drew is the outline traced across the tops of these overlapping windows of sensitivity, the envelope of everything the comparison machinery can do. And notice what that predicts. Widths near the peak have several families answering at once, which is why visibility there is robust and forgiving. The far ends have only one reluctant responder, which is why they fail fast. Host: A shape and a why, then. But the curve is not carved in stone, and our brief for tonight lists its modifiers, the things that move it. Take them one at a time, starting with light. Guide: Light lifts the whole curve. Raise the illumination and sensitivity climbs at nearly every stripe width: the faintest visible difference grows fainter, the threshold falls, and the hump rises. Dim the light and the opposite happens, with a twist worth savouring. The whole curve sinks and, importantly, its peak slides toward broader stripes. In dim light the rods and the peripheral machinery carry more of the load, and they are built for coarser comparisons, so the best-answered width moves away from fine stripes. In bright light the cone-rich centre dominates, and the peak sits where we found it, over the middling widths. Tonight's curve is that same re-scaling seen from the outside, as a measurement. One light level, one curve. Another light level, another curve, slid in height and, at the dim end, in position too. The eye does not have a sensitivity; it has a family of sensitivities, and the prevailing light chooses among them.

Host: Light is the modifier that arrives from outside. The second modifier comes from inside, and it is the stranger of the two. I am told that simply staring at bold stripes for a minute or so can redraw the curve, with no change in the light at all. What happens, why does it happen, and how long does it last?

Guide: It is true, and we met its principle in Contrast Processing, in the gain control that re-zeros the system against its recent diet. Stare at bold, high-contrast stripes for a minute or two, then look at fainter ones, and the faint ones have become harder to see: sensitivity at those stripe widths has temporarily dropped, sometimes by a surprising amount. The machinery that has been shouting all minute now demands more difference before it bothers to report, as if it has raised its criterion for news. The effect is selective, falling hardest on the widths you stared at, and it fades over minutes as the system relaxes back toward its resting curve. This is adaptation, the second modifier: the curve is not only moved by light but re-weighted by experience, briefly, reversibly, and entirely normally. Nothing has been damaged. The eye is a listening instrument that tunes itself to whatever it has been hearing, and a loud diet leaves it briefly deaf to whispers at the same pitch. Even the classic after-images of everyday life carry a trace of this same re-zeroing, and the measured curve is where that daily experience becomes a number.

Host: And the third modifier is time itself, in the longest sense, the lifespan. Our boundary tonight is normal function, so let me ask it carefully and keep it there. Guide: Asked that way, the answer is a slow, ordinary drift. Across adult life the measured curve gradually loses a little of its height, and the loss is gentle rather than dramatic, more noticeable toward the fine end of the page than the broad end. Guide: Here is the sentence: visual acuity is one point on the contrast sensitivity curve, not the curve itself. Guide: The quiet one is that no measurement tonight ever touched a brightness on its own. Listen back and check me: every threshold we defined was a difference, a rise and fall around an average, a weighing of bar against neighbour. The curve never plots how bright something is. It plots how different something can be and still be seen. That is why a candle can vanish at noon and blaze in a cave, why print that is perfectly legible under a lamp disappears in shadow, and why the visual system seems to care so little about the absolute level of the world. It does not report light levels; it reports disagreements. The sensitivity curve is simply the audit of how small a disagreement each width of pattern can carry and still be noticed, written in the eye's own hand. Miss that, and the curve looks like a strange optical trick. Grasp it, and it becomes the most honest self-portrait the eye ever sits for.
