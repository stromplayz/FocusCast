Today is the foundation stone. Set the frame for us. Why does this season begin by insisting that wavelengths are physics but colour is physiology?

Guide: Thank you, good to be back, and welcome to everyone joining the series at this season's front porch. Here is the line the whole season hangs on. Light arriving at the eye is a physical event, and physics can describe it completely without ever mentioning colour: a mixture of electromagnetic waves, each with a wavelength, each with an amplitude, all travelling at one fixed speed. Wavelength is a length, literally, the distance between successive peaks, and a physicist could measure every wavelength in the room with instruments and no eye in sight. When we say red light, physics only hears a sentence about waves near the long end of the visible range. Colour is a different kind of thing altogether. The redness of red, that immediate, unmistakable, private quality you experience, is nowhere on the wave. It is manufactured, assembled by your nervous system out of comparisons between the responses of differently tuned sensors in your retina. The light carries the question; the brain constructs the answer. And the physics would look identical to any other observer or any other instrument: a creature built with different sensor tunings would live in a different colour world, even though not one wavelength around it had changed. That is the reframe of the season, and every episode that follows is a payment on it: ten episodes showing exactly how the eye and brain build colour and contrast from the raw retinal report.

Host: It is a strong claim, so let me push on it the way a sceptic would. Surely red is just red. A fire engine looks red, a stop sign looks red, and they looked red long before anyone studied a photoreceptor. What precisely do you mean when you say the redness is not in the light?

Guide: I mean it with complete literalness. Consider what actually arrives at your cornea when you look at that stop sign: a train of electromagnetic waves, and the entire physical inventory of that train can be written in two columns, how long each wave is and how much of it there is. Wavelength and intensity. Those are the only facts the light itself supplies. Nowhere in that inventory does the word red appear, because red is not a fact about the wave; it is a fact about what happens to the wave after a nervous system gets hold of it. Cut the optic nerve and the waves still arrive at the retina; the redness stops. Two observers with different retinal wiring could, in principle, carve the very same physical scene at different boundaries, and no measurement on the light alone could say who was right. The light is indifferent. What we call colour is the nervous system's verdict on the wavelength mixture, a sensation it assigns, and the way it assigns that verdict is the business of this season. Notice the word verdict, because a verdict implies weighing evidence, and that is exactly where we go next: why a verdict, why weighing, and why comparison is not a luxury but the entire mechanism.

Host: Then give us the problem in its sharpest form. Suppose the retina had only one kind of light-catching cell, one tuning. What breaks? You promised me a puzzle about a dim red and a bright green.

Guide: This is the single most important idea in the episode, so let me build it slowly. Any photoreceptor catches different wavelengths with different efficiencies: it may be excellent at one wavelength, mediocre at its neighbours, and hopeless at the far ends of the range. Its response to light, whatever the wavelength, is one graded quantity, a single number: total catch. Now here is the trap. A dim red and a bright green can arrive at that same cell and be caught in exactly equal amounts, because what the red lacks in efficiency it can repay in intensity. The cell's output is identical in the two cases. To the cell, the dim red and the bright green are the same event. One number cannot carry two facts. The wavelength, which decides how efficiently the catch is made, and the intensity, which decides how much light there was to catch, are fused into a single output, and no cleverness downstream can unmix them, because the information was never there to unmix. Physiologists call the principle univariance: one receptor type yields one variable, and one variable cannot span a two-dimensional world of wavelength and intensity. One number, one opinion. Colour, therefore, cannot begin in any single sensor. It begins where sensors of different tunings are compared, because comparison is the only operation that pulls wavelength apart from intensity.

Guide: A good back-reference, because that episode is this one's ancestor. Back in Series 1, we established the practical map: that colour has three everyday dimensions, hue, the redness or blueness itself, brightness, how luminous it seems, and saturation, how pure or washed out it seems; that the visible range runs from violet at the short-wavelength end to red at the long end; and that the eye needs only three lights, mixed in the right proportions, to match almost any colour it is shown. That episode told you what happens. Series 1 later showed how colour vision is measured at the chair, in episode nine of season six, Colour Vision Testing, the clinical how. What neither could do, inside a foundations series, is answer why. Why three lights and not four, and never only two? Why does the visual system divide colour along particular axes at all? Today we rebuild the whole map as physiology: the wavelength problem, the comparison solution, and the ten-episode ladder that turns colour from a description into a mechanism. Nothing we say today will contradict that foundations episode; everything we say will explain it.

Host: Then let us recall the machinery. Season six took us inside the retina and left us with two families of photoreceptors. Refresh the essentials only, because I know you are guarding episode two's material.

Guide: Guarding it jealously, yes, because the three cone types get their full christening in episode two. Recall only the essentials from season six. The retina keeps two families of light sensors, rods and cones, introduced early that season and built into a full physiology across its episodes. Rods are the dim-light specialists, and we will borrow them for a demonstration in a moment. Cones are the bright-light workers, concentrated at the fovea, the pit at the centre of your gaze where acuity lives, and they were given their detailed physiology in episode four of season six, Cone Physiology. The one fact I need today, and I ask you to hold it lightly because episode two will make it heavy: cones come in more than one kind, and the kinds differ in which wavelengths they catch best. Each cone contains one photopigment, and each pigment has its own tuning curve, its own efficiency profile across the spectrum, overlapping its neighbours but not identical to them. That overlap with difference is the raw material of everything ahead. How many kinds there are, what their peak sensitivities are, how they are distributed across the retina: that is episode two's inventory, and we will not pre-teach it. Today we need only the principle: several sensors, differently tuned, reporting the same scene.

Host: And the bridge from season seven. The finale, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal, was the handover. State the handover explicitly, because I think today's whole argument is hiding inside it.

Guide: It is hiding inside it, beautifully. Episode ten of season seven, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal, closed the season with this summary: the retina does not send a photograph. It sends an edited, parallel, difference-based report. Around a million ganglion cells, each carrying an account of a small territory in which a centre reports and a ring argues, and the argument, the difference between centre and surround, is the message. The machinery that creates that argument was Centre-Surround Processing, episode three of season seven, where the receptive field's architecture showed the retina's favourite trick: never report an absolute, always report a difference. And notice why that design is so economical. An absolute level would have to be re-signalled everywhere, all the time, including all the unchanging background; a difference throws the constant away and keeps only the news. Now watch what that means for colour. If the retina's native language is difference, then colour, which we have just argued must begin in comparison, is not a strange new subject bolted onto this season. It is the same language, spoken about wavelength. The retina was built to compare; colour is what its comparisons about wavelength feel like from the inside. That is the handover received: season seven gave us the comparison machine, and season eight watches it build two great products, colour and contrast.

Host: Say more about comparison as wiring, because that word is doing heavy lifting. When you say two sensors are compared, what is physically happening in the circuit? And how much of that story belongs to episode four?

Guide: The word is doing exactly the lifting it should, and the full circuit diagram is episode four's property, Opponent-Process Mechanisms, so I will stay at the level of principle. Comparison, in neural hardware, means this: a cell's output is driven up by what one sensor reports and driven down by what another sensor reports, so the cell's firing is proportional not to either input alone but to the difference, or the ratio, between them. Two sensors catching equally may produce silence; two sensors caught unequally produce a signal whose sign and size encode which way and how far apart. Notice what that buys. The intensity problem, which corrupted the single number, largely cancels: a change in overall light level pushes both inputs up or down together, and the difference stands still, while a change in wavelength pushes them apart. The retina, in short, converts how much each sensor caught into how the catches differ, and that conversion is where the construction of colour actually happens. Episode four will name the channels and their rules; today, hold only this: the sensors catch, and the wiring compares, and the comparison is the first moment at which colour exists in the nervous system.

Host: You said you would borrow the rods for a demonstration. Do it, because I want to see univariance in the wild. Why does the world drain toward grey when the light fades?

Guide: Because at dusk your cones fall silent, too insensitive for the faint light, and you are seeing with rods alone, the dim-light specialists whose physiology we walked in episode three of season six, Rod Physiology. And the rods are a single sensor type: one tuning, one output number per cell. Univariance, live. Under rod vision every wavelength must speak through the same efficiency profile, so a dim long-wavelength light and a brighter middle-wavelength light are genuinely indistinguishable to the rod: whatever wavelength arrives, the rod reports only total catch, and the brain, receiving one number, can paint in only one dimension. So the garden at dusk still shows you shapes, textures and movement, all the gifts of spatial comparison, but the colours are gone: flowers that blazed at noon become gradations of silver and grey. Nobody has switched colour off. The comparisons that make colour have simply lost their inputs, because only one tuning remains on duty. The grey world is the cleanest demonstration physiology ever ran: take away the differently tuned sensors, keep the light, and colour disappears with them. That is as close to proof as biology gives that colour lives in the comparison, not in the light.

Host: Let us give physics its full due before we leave it, because the wave deserves its day in court. What exactly arrives at the eye in a typical scene, and why does a tomato, physically speaking, throw long-wavelength light at us?

Guide: Physics first, then. Sunlight is a broad mixture, and the slice of it your eye responds to runs from roughly four hundred to seven hundred nanometres of wavelength, the visible range: violet and blue at the short end, green in the middle, orange and red at the long end. Below four hundred lies ultraviolet, above seven hundred lies infrared, both physically present and both invisible to you, which is itself a quiet reminder that the retina samples only a sliver of the electromagnetic world. Surfaces edit that mixture. A tomato's skin carries pigments that absorb much of the middle and short wavelengths and reflect the long ones, so the light leaving the tomato toward your eye is physically enriched in long wavelengths. That enrichment is a fact, measurable with a meter, and it belongs entirely to physics. But notice the final step. The description long-wavelength-enriched is what the meter says. Red is what the brain says, after comparing the reports of its differently tuned cones, discounting for the illumination, and assigning the verdict. And here is a seductive fact we will hold for episode three, Trichromatic Vision: quite different physical mixtures can drive the cone catches to the same values, and when they do, the two lights look identical, no matter how different they are on the meter. The eye cannot see the difference; the meter can.

Host: But that raises the objection everyone actually feels. If colour is a construction, why does it feel so solid? The banana in my kitchen looks yellow under the morning sun, under a cloudy sky, under the kitchen bulb. A construction that flimsy would be useless.

Guide: You have reversed the charge, and correctly. The construction feels solid because it is engineered to be solid. The nervous system is not trying to report the lamp; it is trying to report the surface. The banana's reflectance, which wavelengths its skin throws back, is a stable property of the object, while the light falling on it changes all day long. So the comparison machinery is arranged to keep the answer anchored to the object: outputs are taken relative to neighbours, relative to the scene's overall illumination, and relative to the sensors' own adapted states, so the verdict tracks the surface rather than the source. The banana stays yellow because the system is built to discount the changing light and hold the object constant. That achievement, often called constancy, is one of the quiet wonders of the visual system, and we will feel its mechanisms as the season unfolds; the full treatment belongs to later episodes. The point for today is that stability is not an argument against construction. It is the specification the construction was designed to meet. A meter reads the light; the brain reads the world.

Host: Stop at that hinge for a moment, because the second half changes scenery from colour to contrast, and I do not want the join to feel arbitrary. What is contrast, exactly, in this physiological language, and why do you call the second half the measured world?

Guide: Contrast is comparison again, now wearing its simplest clothes. Where colour asks which wavelengths were caught, contrast asks the blunter, older question: how much brighter here than there? A scene of perfectly even light would be invisible; vision exists because the world is patterned, because surfaces differ, and contrast is the size of that difference taken relative to the average. What makes the second half the measured world is that contrast, unlike colour sensation, lends itself to clean numbers: you can build a patch that differs from its grey surround by a known fraction, march that fraction downward until the observer just loses sight of it, and read off a threshold. Contrast sensitivity is the measure of how small a difference still counts, and the low-contrast letter charts you will meet at the chair are direct descendants of that measurement. Spatial frequency, episode seven, refines the question to fineness: how many stripes of light and dark pack into a given width of your vision. Contrast detection, episode eight, is the threshold game played in full. And luminance and brightness, episode nine, closes the loop with the construction theme, because brightness shifts with surroundings and adaptation in ways no light meter predicts. The grammar you learned today for colour is exactly the grammar the second half uses.

Host: Let us hold on brightness for one more beat, because it sounds like the one thing that should be honest. Surely brightness is just how much light there is?

Guide: The most natural mistake in the whole season, and episode nine exists to dismantle it. Brightness depends on surroundings, on adaptation, and on context in ways a light meter never sees. The same physical intensity looks dazzling against a dark room and modest on a sunny street; a grey patch looks lighter on a dark background than on a light one; and your own retina recalibrates itself by the hour, shifting the percept while the physics stands still. If even brightness, the most physical-seeming of all sensations, is a construction from relative reports, then the season's framing is not a philosophical pose. It is the honest description of the entire visual system: the retina reports differences, and the brain builds quantities from them.

Host: Before we close, let us clear the deck of myths. What is the commonest misconception about colour that a listener should consciously retire after this episode?

Guide: Two, and they are related. The first is the camera myth: that the eye passively receives the scene and colour simply comes in with the light, a property riding on objects like paint. After today, retire it: the light carries only wavelengths and intensities, and colour appears when the nervous system compares differently tuned reports. The second is the sensor myth, the half-right idea that runs, cones see colour and rods do not, as though a cone on its own could see colour at all. It cannot; no single sensor can, and univariance forbids it. What sees colour is the comparison across cone types, and the wiring that performs the comparison. By the season's end even the sensors themselves are best understood not as little cameras but as tuned instruments whose individual outputs are nearly meaningless until they are played against each other. Wavelengths are physics; colour is physiology. Hold those two sentences and the whole season opens.
