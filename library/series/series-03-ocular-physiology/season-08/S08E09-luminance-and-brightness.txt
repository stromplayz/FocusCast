Host: Here is a small experiment you can run without leaving your chair. Picture a white shirt hanging on a washing line in the shade on a bright summer day. Now picture a lump of coal lying on sunlit snow. In the raw physics of it, the coal in full sunlight sends more light toward your eye than the shirt in the shade, many times more. And yet the shirt reads as white and the coal reads as black, instantly and stubbornly, and no amount of persuasion changes that. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks. This is Series 3, Ocular Physiology, Season 8, Colour and Contrast Vision, and this is episode nine, Luminance and Brightness. The season has walked colour from cone types through opponent mechanisms, and contrast from gratings through spatial frequency to detection. Today we take the two words in our title, which sound like synonyms, and pull them carefully apart.

Guide: Thank you, and this is a step I have looked forward to, because it is the hinge of the whole season. Everything we have said so far about cones, colour and contrast was secretly a story about two different worlds: the world of physics, where light is a measurable quantity, and the world of perception, where light becomes an experience. Luminance belongs to the first world. It is what an instrument measures, how much light a surface sends toward the eye. Brightness belongs to the second. It is what a person feels, the constructed sense of how intense that light is. Way back in Series 1, season two, episode two, From Light to Vision, we walked the whole road from photons arriving to experience arising, and today we stop in the widest gap along that road, the gap between the measurement and the feeling. The plan: define luminance, define brightness, tour the three lighting regimes, watch dusk reverse red and blue, and finish with the brain's boldest trick, lightness constancy.

Host: Let us start with the measured one, then, and be precise about it. When you say luminance is how much light a surface sends toward the eye, that already sounds like a simple statement of brightness. So what exactly does the word luminance mean, and what makes it a measured quantity rather than a felt one?

Guide: Luminance is defined from the eye's point of view, which is the first surprise. It is not simply the amount of energy a surface reflects. It is the amount of light a surface sends to the eye after that light has been weighted by the eye's own daylight sensitivity. Here is the weighting in words, no curves needed. Of all the visible wavelengths, the eye in daylight is most sensitive to the middle of the spectrum, the greenish region, and wavelengths there count heavily. Slide toward the deep reds at one end or the violets at the other, and the weighting falls away steeply, so those wavelengths count far less. Two surfaces reflecting equal physical energy do not have equal luminance if one reflects green and one reflects deep red. The green one wins, and wins comfortably. That weighted amount of arriving light is what a photometer reports, expressed in units called candelas per square metre, and the weighting itself carries the name photopic luminous efficiency, which we will keep entirely in words.

Host: Stay in words, gladly, but push the weighting a little further. Why should the eye be built so that the middle of the spectrum dominates the measurement? And what does that weighting do to a real scene, a garden, a bookshelf, a street?

Guide: The weighting exists because luminance is not a property of light alone but of light meeting an eye. The daylight visual system is a cone system, and the cone population responds most strongly through the greenish middle wavelengths, peaking near five hundred and fifty-five nanometres. Luminance simply respects that. The consequence in a real scene is quiet and constant. Light a green leaf and a red flower identically, let them reflect the same physical energy, and the leaf still registers far more luminance, because the eye's own daylight ruler weighs green heavily and deep red lightly. Deep violet and deep red are, in luminance terms, almost whispers. Notice what has happened philosophically. We tried to define how much light arrives, and we could not do it honestly without talking about the receiver. The physics sets the stage, but the eye writes the numbers, and that is exactly why luminance deserves its own name rather than being called energy or intensity.

Host: Now the other word, then. If luminance is the measured quantity, the instrument reading, what is brightness? Give me the definition, and tell me what kind of thing brightness actually is.

Guide: Brightness is the percept. It is the felt intensity of light, the answer your visual system produces when asked how bright something looks, from dazzling down to dim down to invisible. And the crucial word is produces. Brightness is not read off the retina the way a number is read off a gauge. It is constructed, computed, inferred. It depends on luminance, yes, but it also depends on context, on contrast with neighbouring surfaces, and on the observer's own state of adaptation, which sets the operating scale of the whole system. Same luminance, different context, different brightness. Same luminance, different adaptation, different brightness. So hold the pairing this way. Luminance is the brain's raw material. Brightness is the brain's best guess about that material, a hypothesis about how much light is out there, shaped by everything else in the scene and by everything the observer has lived through in the past hour.

Host: Hold on. You have called brightness a feeling, and I want to press on that, because feelings sound unmeasurable. Two people can stare at the same lamp and one calls it glaring while the other calls it cosy. If brightness lives in the mind, how did science ever attach honest numbers to it? What does it even mean to measure a percept?

Guide: With matching, and it is one of the prettiest methods in all of vision science. You cannot read brightness off a person directly, but you can ask them to compare. Split a circular field down the middle, illuminate one half, and ask the observer to adjust the light falling on the other half until the two sides look equally bright. At the point of match, whatever the observer calls equal in felt intensity can be treated as equal, and by repeating the judgement across levels you can trace how brightness actually grows as light is added. When those matches are set beside what a photometer reports, the two disagree in instructive ways: the percept grows more slowly than the measurement, and it bends whenever the surround changes. That disagreement, between what the match says and what the meter says, is precisely where the luminance-brightness distinction was born. The meter measures the stimulus. The match measures the percept. And they are not the same curve.

Host: A best guess is a strong claim, and I want to test it. Give me a concrete case where brightness visibly departs from luminance, where the same measured amount of light produces two different experiences, something a listener could stage in their own kitchen tonight.

Guide: The kitchen test is the oldest demonstration in the book. Cut two identical grey squares from one sheet of paper. Place one on a sheet of black paper and one on a sheet of white paper, side by side under the same lamp. Their luminance is identical, bit for bit, because they are the same grey. Yet the square on the black background looks noticeably brighter, and the square on the white background looks darker. Nothing about the arriving light changed; everything about the context did. The visual system does not judge a surface in isolation. It judges differences, especially at edges, and it normalises each surface against its surround. This is simultaneous contrast, and we have already met its machinery earlier this season in episode six, Contrast Sensitivity, and in the foundations laid in Series 1, season three, episode eight, also called Contrast Sensitivity. The measurement of contrast is, in a deep sense, the study of how the brain builds brightness out of differences rather than absolutes.

Host: Differences rather than absolutes. Say that again slowly, because I think it is the key to everything today, and then take it up to whole scenes. When the entire room is dim, or the whole scene is bathed in sun, what does the brain do with that global level of illumination?

Guide: It discounts it, and this is the boldest trick I promised. Return to the shirt and the coal from the opening. Measured luminance gives the verdict to the coal: on a sunny day, that dark lump sends more light toward the eye than the white shirt hanging in the shade. Perceived lightness overrules the meter: the shirt is white and the coal is black, and everyone who has ever hung out washing knows it. The brain has not ignored the physics; it has subtracted it. What it reports is reflectance, the permanent property of the surface, the fraction of whatever light is available that the surface sends back. White surfaces return most of what they receive, black surfaces return very little, and that relationship holds in shade and in sun alike. The brain treats the current illumination as a common factor scaling every surface in view, factors it out, and hands you the surfaces themselves. Psychologists call this lightness constancy, and it is why the world keeps its colours and its categories when clouds drift across the sun.

Host: How, mechanically? The eye receives a pattern of luminance, one flat image with illumination and reflectance tangled together like ink stirred into water. What does the visual system actually do to that tangled image to pull the surfaces back out of it?

Guide: Several operations run together, and we can describe each in words. First, spatial comparison. Neurons throughout the early visual system respond to differences between neighbouring regions far more than to absolute levels, so the retinal image is immediately re-described as edges, ratios and gradients, and edges are where illumination boundaries and reflectance boundaries leave different signatures. Second, anchoring. Within a scene, the brightest surfaces tend to be assigned toward white and the darkest toward black, and everything else is scaled in between, which gives the brain a provisional scale for the current illumination. Third, expectation. Surfaces in the world are usually evenly lit, and illumination changes gradually, while reflectance changes abruptly at object boundaries, so the brain assigns slow changes to the light and sharp changes to the surfaces. None of this is a chalkboard calculation. It is physiology, networks of excitatory and inhibitory connections doing the arithmetic in parallel. The output is lightness, the perceived reflectance of each surface, and that is why the shirt stays white.

Host: You keep adding ingredients. Luminance, then context, then scene-wide inference. There is one more you named earlier, the observer's own adaptation state. Bridge that in for us, and connect it back to the sensor episodes we built in Season 6.

Guide: Adaptation is the third leg of the stool, and it belongs to Season 6. In episode six, Dark Adaptation, and episode seven, Light Adaptation, we watched the visual system continuously retune its own sensitivity, gaining amplification in the dark and shedding it in the light, a slow rod-and-cone handover on the way into darkness and a fast cone recalibration on the way out of it. Adaptation sets the scale against which any luminance is judged. A candle in a pitch-black room is blinding; the same candle on a sunlit table is nearly invisible. The luminance of the flame has not changed at all. What changed is the state of the observer, and brightness follows the observer, not the meter. And remember the range it must cover. From full sunlight to a moonless night, the light available to the eye spans a range measured in billions. No single setting could hold all of it, so the system never uses one. It renormalises constantly, and that renormalisation is precisely why brightness is a guess about the world rather than a copy of it.

Host: Which brings us to the three lighting regimes, and this is the part of the episode I most want listeners to be able to recite. Name them, and for each one tell me who is doing the seeing, what the world is like, and roughly where the boundaries fall.

Guide: Three regimes, three names. First, photopic, the cone world. Daylight and bright indoor light, luminance above roughly three candelas per square metre. Cones do the seeing, colour is rich, fine detail is resolved, and the luminance weighting we defined earlier, the one that favours the greenish middle, is the photopic weighting. Second, scotopic, the rod world. Near-darkness, luminance below about one thousandth of a candela per square metre, a moonless night indoors or out. Rods do the seeing, colour is gone, the world arrives in shades of grey, sensitivity is enormous, and resolution is poor. Third, mesopic, the twilight blend. Luminance between those bounds, dusk, dim corridors, deep shade at noon. Here rods and cones run together, and the visual system is genuinely two machines at once, sharing the image and trading strengths. Those boundary numbers are convenient conventions rather than walls, and real life slides through them continuously, but the three-way picture, cone world, rod world, blend, is worth carrying permanently. It is the bridge between Season 6's sensor episodes and everything this season says about colour, because colour only exists where cones do. Notice too how wide the rod world is. From that one thousandth of a candela per square metre downward, scotopic vision keeps working through a further thousand-fold fall in light, all the way to the verge of single photons, long after the cone world has fallen silent.

Host: The blend is the one I find most haunting, the dusk regime. Walk me through the handover. When the light is fading and both systems are running, which one owns which part of the experience, and what does the listener actually feel as the transition proceeds?

Guide: In mesopic light the two systems divide the retina's labour in a new way. Cones remain responsible for the centre, for the fovea, which is rod-free, and for colour and detail wherever light still permits. Rods, far more sensitive, take the periphery and take over wherever the light has grown too sparse for cones to answer. So at dusk you get the classic mixture: colour thins out first, resolution softens, and the far periphery stays oddly lively while the dead centre of gaze grows quiet, because the fovea has no rods to fall back on. Both channels are feeding the same downstream network, and the balance slides steadily toward the rods as luminance falls. Nothing switches off cleanly. The systems cross-fade, and the experience cross-fades with them, the vivid photopic world dissolving into a grey, grainy, peripheral scotopic one. We gave the two sensor systems their full biographies in Season 6, episode three, Rod Physiology, and episode four, Cone Physiology, and dusk is exactly where those two biographies overlap.

Host: Overlap is the right word, because dusk has a signature, and it is the single most memorable fact I know from this whole season. The flowers. Tell the story of the garden at dusk, why the red flowers sink into darkness before the blue ones do, and give the phenomenon its proper name.

Guide: The story deserves to be told slowly. Stand in a garden at sunset, with red roses and blue-violet lobelias side by side. In full daylight the reds blaze and the blues are comparatively modest. Now let the light fade. The order reverses. The red flowers dim first, sliding to a deep, almost black red, while the blue flowers seem to hold their light longer and eventually look brighter than the reds beside them. This reversal of dusk is the Purkinje shift, named for the nineteenth-century physiologist who first described it, and it is not a trick in the dismissive sense. It is the two systems' spectral sensitivities taking turns. The rod system is barely sensitive to deep red, but it answers beautifully to blue and blue-green light, peaking near five hundred and seven nanometres. The cone system peaks in the greenish middle, near five hundred and fifty-five. As the rods take over from the cones, the whole brightness balance of the scene slides toward the short wavelengths. The blues keep their brightness, the reds surrender theirs, and you can watch the crossover happen flower by flower. And the shift is beautifully reversible. Brighten the garden again, let the cones resume command, and the advantage slides back to the middle wavelengths: the reds recover their blaze and the blues settle into their daytime modesty.

Host: Let us go one level down, into the receptors themselves. Why is the rod system so nearly blind to red? What is it about the rod's own pigment that leaves the long wavelengths out in the cold?

Guide: It comes down to one pigment and its appetite. Rods contain a single photopigment, rhodopsin, and its absorption is shaped like a broad hill centred in the blue-green, near that five hundred and seven nanometre peak. Light at the deep red end, well past six hundred nanometres, is absorbed so poorly by rhodopsin that a rod receives almost nothing from it, no matter how many red photons arrive. Cones, by contrast, come in three types with overlapping sensitivities that together cover the spectrum, including a long-wavelength type that owns the reds. That is the whole explanation of the shift: dusk hands the retina to a single pigment that cannot see red. The fact even has a quiet laboratory echo we noted in Season 6. A dim deep red light stimulates cones while leaving rods almost untouched, which is how dark-adaptation studies probe the cone system without disturbing rod sensitivity, and it is why the two pathways can be separated cleanly at the light source rather than at the surgery.

Host: One vocabulary check before we assemble everything, because you have used two words tonight that listeners may be quietly conflating. Brightness and lightness. Are they the same experience wearing different names, or are they genuinely different judgements?

Guide: Genuinely different, and separating them is worth a minute. Brightness is the perceived intensity of light itself, the glow question. A flame, a lamp, a patch of sky, a firefly: these are lights, and we judge how much light they send us. Lightness is the perceived reflectance of a surface, the paint question. A shirt, a wall, a page: these are surfaces, and we judge how much of the available light they return, from white at the top of the scale to black at the bottom. The same region can be described both ways, and the two judgements can come apart. A grey page in sunlight sends more light to the eye than a white page in a dim room, so it has more luminance and more brightness, yet it still reads as a grey surface, lower in lightness. Brightness answers the question, how much light is arriving. Lightness answers the question, what kind of surface am I looking at. Constancy, as we have seen, is the visual system siding firmly with lightness.

Host: Now assemble the whole machine for me, from the front of the eye to the percept. Light arrives, luminance is the measured input, and brightness comes out the other side. Walk me through the stages in order, and show me where context, contrast, adaptation and constancy each inject their contribution.

Guide: Think of it as four stages, each feeding the next. Stage one, the physical input. Surfaces send weighted light to the eye, and that weighted arrival, luminance, is the only raw measurement the system ever gets. Stage two, contrast extraction. The network immediately converts absolute levels into differences and ratios at edges, so every surface is encoded relative to its neighbours. This is where context injects itself, and it is why the same grey looks different on black and on white. Stage three, gain control. Adaptation resets the operating scale continuously, so the encoded differences are interpreted against the observer's current state, the candle in the dark versus the candle in the sun. Stage four, inference. Anchoring, the discounting of the illuminant, and the assumptions about surfaces deliver lightness, the reflectance the brain finally reports. And running through all four stages, the mapping is compressive: double the luminance and brightness rises, but by less than double, and each further doubling buys less. Brightness, in the end, is the brain's best guess, luminance corrected for context, renormalised by adaptation, and reinterpreted as surfaces.

Host: That compressive mapping deserves another minute, because students meet it in psychophysics as a law and often miss what it means. Tell me plainly. If brightness is not proportional to luminance, what is the relationship, and why did the visual system build the squashing in?

Guide: The relationship is a systematic compression. Across a wide range of everyday luminances, perceived brightness grows roughly like a root of the physical intensity, the kind of rule psychophysicists capture in power laws, so the luminance must multiply several times over for brightness merely to double. Why build it that way? Because the important information in the visual world lives in ratios, not absolutes, and a compressive map lets a finite nervous system cover an enormous range without saturating. In bright light the system does not waste its whole output announcing that everything is very bright; it preserves the differences between surfaces, and differences are what objects are made of. The squash also explains a familiar moment: stepping from a sunlit street into a dim shop feels like blindness at first, and then the world quietly reappears. That compression, together with adaptation, is the reason one visual system can be sensitive enough for starlight and stable enough for sunlight without ever changing its basic wiring.

Host: Let us land this on solid ground for our students and clinicians. No disease today and no advice, just function. When someone is learning to measure and test vision, what changes in their practice once they truly hold the distinction between the measured and the perceived?

Guide: Three habits of mind. First, respect the instrument and the person as different reporters. A photometer states luminance, full stop, while a patient states brightness, and both are honest about different things, so a mismatch between them is information rather than error. Second, control the conditions that brightness depends on. Every serious measure of contrast, from this season's episode six, Contrast Sensitivity, through episode seven, Spatial Frequency, to episode eight, Contrast Detection, holds the observer's adaptation steady and specifies the surround luminance, precisely because the percept depends on those and the meter's reading does not. Third, interpret constancy rather than being fooled by it. When two surfaces lit differently still look the same shade, the lightness system is doing exactly what it evolved to do, reporting the surfaces. The same distinction explains why display and imaging specifications quote luminance in candelas per square metre while the experience those numbers produce is negotiated by the viewer. The measurement tells you the input. The physiology tells you the rest.
