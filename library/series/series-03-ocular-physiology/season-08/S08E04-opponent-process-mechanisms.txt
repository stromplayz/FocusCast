# Description: Three cones hand the eye three numbers, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks teaches the rewiring that turns those numbers into the colours you actually see. Learn why nobody has ever seen reddish green or bluish yellow, and how the cone catches are immediately recombined into a red-green channel, a blue-yellow channel and a light-dark channel of signed differences. Meet the retinal circuits and ganglion families that carry each comparison, from the fine-detail midget lines to the small-fibre blue-yellow channel. Watch a staring afterimage bloom in the opposite colour and learn why it is opponent fatigue, normal physiology and nothing more. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want the second edit of colour explained for the ear.

Host: Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology. This season is Season 8, Colour and Contrast Vision, and we are four episodes deep. Episode one, Physiology of Colour Vision, opened the great question of what colour is and what it is for. Episode two, Cone Types and Colour Detection, introduced the three cone families and their overlapping sensitivities. And episode three, Trichromatic Vision, gave us the theory that made colour measurable: reduce every light to three catch numbers, and any colour can be matched exactly by mixing three lights in the right proportions. It is one of the most successful theories in all of vision, and tonight we push it until it creaks. Because that same theory quietly predicts something that has never happened to anyone who has ever looked at anything: it predicts that red should sometimes look greenish, and that yellow should sometimes look bluish. Neither has ever happened, not once, in the entire history of looking. Tonight's title, Opponent-Process Mechanisms, names the rewiring that explains what matching cannot. Walk us to the crack. What did matching actually achieve, and where does experience refuse to follow?

Guide: Let me give the theory its full credit first, because tonight is not a correction, it is a completion. Three cone types catch light in three overlapping bands: long wavelengths, middle wavelengths and short wavelengths. Any surface you look at boils down, at the retina, to three numbers: how strongly the long cones caught it, how strongly the middle cones caught it, how strongly the short cones caught it. Episode three showed that with three suitably chosen lights you can match any colour in the world, not by reproducing its light but by reproducing its three numbers. That is metamerism, and it is no trick; every screen you have ever watched runs on it. But notice what the theory is: a complete account of matching, and a completely silent account of seeing. If experience were assembled directly from three independent numbers, then mixtures should be free. Somewhere in colour space there should be a light that looks simultaneously red and green, a true reddish green, and another that looks bluish yellow. No observer has ever reported either. Red is always simply red, yellow is always simply yellow, and the hues seem to arrive pre-arranged as two natural opposites: red against green, blue against yellow. Nineteenth-century critics of the three-receptor view pressed exactly this point, and they were right about the conclusion before anyone knew the wiring: matching and seeing are different acts, and seeing must be built by a further step. Listeners who want the experience side of that argument can revisit Series 1, episode nine of season three, Colour Vision, where colour was taught as a property of seeing rather than of light. Today we teach the step the experience was hinting at. The retina takes the three cone numbers and immediately recomputes them as differences, and those differences, not the numbers themselves, are what leave the eye.

Host: Give me the sharpest possible form of the mismatch, because I want to feel exactly where the prediction fails, not just be told that it does. If three independent numbers built colour experience directly, what exactly should I be able to see that no one has ever seen? And when you answer, tell me what the pattern of the failures hints about the machinery underneath, because I suspect the failures are not random.

Guide: Three predictions fail together, and their failure has a pattern. First, free mixture. Three independent dials should allow any combination of dial settings, so red light mixed with green light ought to be able to look red and green at once. It never does; it looks yellow, a single new hue, pure and without parts. Second, purity of the extremes. At full strength the colours that feel elemental, red, yellow, green and blue, never carry their opposite inside them. You can find oranges that lean red and oranges that lean yellow, but you cannot find a red that leans green, however long you search. Third, cancellation. If a colour carries even a trace of redness, adding the right amount of green light cancels that trace, driving the colour toward neutral, and the reverse works identically. That is the signature of a single axis with two directions: red and green are not two independent flavours, they are one dial read in two directions, and pushing toward red cancels green exactly as thoroughly as pushing toward green cancels red. The same holds for blue and yellow. Experience is telling us, in the only language it has, that the underlying quantities are differences: one dial for red against green, one for blue against yellow, and one more for light against dark. And the pattern is the clue. Failures of prediction that line up on neat opposing pairs are not noise; they are the fingerprint of subtraction happening somewhere between the cones and the mind.

Host: Then let us name them properly, one at a time, because these three channels are tonight's entire cast. Give me the wiring map: the three channels, and for each one exactly which cone catches are compared, which way the answer swings, and what its quietness means. Plain words, and no mathematics beyond adding and subtracting.

Guide: Three channels, each built from the same three cone numbers. The first is the red-green channel, and it compares the long cone catch against the middle cone catch. If the long catch exceeds the middle catch, the channel's answer leans to the red side. If the middle catch exceeds the long catch, it leans to the green side. If the two catches are equal, the channel goes quiet, and its quietness is information too. The second is the blue-yellow channel, and it compares the short cone catch against the sum of the other two. When the short cones catch strongly relative to long and middle together, the answer leans blue. When the short catch is weak relative to that sum, the answer leans yellow. The third is the light-dark channel, and here the bright-sensitive pair is summed rather than opposed: long catch plus middle catch, with the rods quietly adding their voice in dimmer light. This channel does not ask which cone caught more; it asks how much light arrived overall, and its answer is what reads to you as brightness. Notice what the names are not. The red-green channel does not compare red light with green light; it compares two cone catches, wherever they came from. The comparison is the message. Those three quantities, one difference, a second difference and a sum, are the retina's real colour report, and everything else tonight is where they are computed, how they are carried, and what they explain.

Host: Stop on that word difference, because it carries the whole design. Why subtract? The cones have already done the hard work of catching light. What does the retina gain by immediately setting the raw numbers aside and keeping only their differences?

Guide: It gains three things, and one of them will sound familiar. First, stability. Any change that strikes all the cones together, the sun sliding behind a cloud, a lamp dimming, the overall cast of morning light versus evening light, pushes both sides of the comparison equally, and in a difference those common changes cancel. The colour channels therefore report the make-up of the light from a surface, not the strength of the illumination falling on it, which is the first taste of why a red apple stays recognisably red indoors and out. Second, sensitivity. The long and middle cone catches are usually similar in size, and the interesting news is their small difference; computing that difference directly makes small relative changes easy to read, instead of burying them inside two large absolute numbers. Third, economy. A difference can swing either way around zero, so one line carries both the reddish direction and the greenish direction, one line both the blue and the yellow, and the cable budget this series keeps admiring is spent twice over. And here is the familiar part. Series 3, episode three of season seven, Centre-Surround Processing, taught the ganglion cell's signature computation: an excitatory centre arguing with an inhibitory ring, subtraction between neighbouring patches of the mosaic. Opponency is that identical arithmetic aimed at cone types instead of places. The surround has simply changed its membership: instead of a ring of neighbours of the same kind, it is the other cone class. Same trick, new cargo.

Host: Now place the wiring on the map we already own. Where in the retina are these differences actually computed, cell by cell, and which cells carry the finished comparisons out of the eye? I ask because the retina season promised us that nothing leaves without a ganglion cell's signature, and tonight should honour that promise.

Guide: It honours it beautifully, because the carriers are the same three families we counted in Series 3, episode nine of season six, Retinal Ganglion Cells. The computing happens through the retina's inner editing floors. Horizontal cells pool sideways across neighbouring photoreceptors and feed comparisons back; bipolar cells inherit the signed differences at the very first synapse; amacrine cells refine the conversations further before the ganglion cells convert everything into pulses. Then the finished comparisons ride out on the families. The red-green comparison travels largely on the midget cells, about four fifths of the entire ganglion population, the small, sustained, fine-detail reporters with their foveal private lines. In the central retina the private wiring is the point: one cone feeding one bipolar feeding one midget ganglion cell is precisely the arrangement that keeps cone types apart, so the channel that reports fine address is, on its own cable, also reporting long against middle. The blue-yellow comparison travels on its own, much smaller channel: the small bistratified cells, named for dendrites that branch in two tiers and gather different bipolar signs at different depths, comparing the short cone story against the combined long and middle story. That stream is thinner and rarer, a genuine small-fibre line of its own. The parasol cells, the big-field motion reporters we met as the second family, are not colour channels at all; they pool across cone types, which is exactly why motion and flicker reach you colourless.

Host: That division of labour is lopsided, and I want to know why. Why does red-green share the fine-detail cables while blue-yellow gets its own thin line, and what does that split cost and buy the observer in everyday seeing?

Guide: History explains the lopsidedness. The blue-yellow comparison is the ancient channel; some version of short against the rest runs through the eyes of most backboned animals, which is why it has its own dedicated, small-fibre line. The red-green comparison is the recent arrival. Splitting the long and middle pigments into two cones is primate work, comparatively late, and evolution did not lay new cables for the innovation. It moved into wiring that already existed: the midget lines, which were already keeping cones apart for the sake of fine detail. Red-green colour, in that honest sense, rides the detail network as a passenger that pays its way. The consequences follow the same split. Short cones are a small minority of the mosaic, and Series 2, episode four of season eight, Photoreceptor Distribution, mapped that unevenness: short cones are sparse everywhere, nearly absent from the very centre of the fovea, and the small bistratified fibres that serve them are few. So the blue-yellow comparison is coarser in address, thinner in cable and quieter in numbers, while red-green rides the densest, finest network in the retina. Hear the design rather than any shortfall: every normally wired eye on earth is built this way, and the division of labour suits the jobs. Fine colour judgements happen at the centre of gaze, where the private lines live, and the blue-yellow channel does its steadier work across the wider field.

Host: Let me test the machine on three ordinary sights before we go any further, because diagrams are cheap and the high street is honest. A red apple on a market stall, a lemon beside it, and the grey pavement under both. Take each one through the three channels in turn and show me the report the retina actually files.

Guide: The apple first. Its skin reflects long wavelengths generously and middle wavelengths weakly, so the long catch towers over the middle catch: the red-green channel is driven hard toward the red side. The short catch stays modest against the sum of the other two, so the blue-yellow channel leans gently yellow, which is part of why a ripe apple reads as a warm red rather than a cold one. The light-dark channel reports a moderate brightness. Total report: strongly red, slightly yellow, medium bright, and that is the experience. The lemon next. Its skin returns long and middle together in abundance, so on the red-green axis the two catches nearly balance and the channel sits near zero, neither reddish nor greenish. But the short catch is low relative to that combined long and middle sum, so the blue-yellow channel swings firmly to the yellow side, and the light-dark channel reads bright. Near zero, firmly yellow, bright: the experience is yellow, full stop. The pavement last. Grey reflects all wavelengths roughly equally and moderately, so long catch, middle catch and short catch are all middling. Both chromatic channels sit at their neutral points and go quiet, while the light-dark channel alone keeps talking at a mid rate. Grey it is. And notice the lemon's quiet gift to episode three. A spectral yellow light and a mixture of red and green light land the channels at the same address: long and middle together, short quiet. Matching works because matching happens here, at the channel arithmetic, downstream of the cones.

Host: One mechanical worry before we climb to the afterimages. A nerve fibre cannot fire a negative number of pulses, and yet you are asking one red-green line to report two opposite hues. What does that fibre actually do when the comparison is exactly balanced, and how does the brain tell the two directions apart on a single cable?

Guide: It hums in the middle, and the answer around that hum is the message. Every ganglion fibre runs a tonic resting rate, its own personal baseline ticking away even in steady light, a fact the ganglion episode made much of in season six. The comparison is coded as a deviation from that baseline: firing above it means one polarity, firing below it means the other. A red-green fibre with the long catch leading speeds up past its baseline, and the brain reads acceleration as red; the same fibre with the middle catch leading slows beneath its baseline, and the brain reads the quieting as green. Exact balance returns the fibre to its hum, which the brain reads as neutral. This is why the mirror families matter, the ON and OFF populations from season six covering both directions crisply, and why the brain never receives absolutes, only adjustments, a million rhythms rising and falling around personal middles. And it plants the seed of tonight's second half. If the message is a deviation from a baseline, then the baseline itself is a control the retina can turn. Hold that thought, because in a few minutes it will explain every afterimage you have ever seen.

Host: Then let us collect that debt, because everyone listening has already run this experiment without knowing it. You stare at something strongly coloured, look away to a plain wall, and a ghost of the opposite colour hangs in the wall, green where the red was, yellow where the blue was. Most people file that under eyes playing tricks, or worse, under something going wrong. Which is it: fault, or physiology?

Guide: It is physiology, ordinary and dignified, and it is one of the cleanest demonstrations of the wiring you can perform with no equipment at all. Follow the mechanics. Stare at a solid red patch for half a minute or so. All that time the red-green channel is being driven hard in one direction, the long catch leading, the fibres on the red side of their baselines running hot. Channels under steady drive adapt: the retina is a gain-control machine, always re-tuning its sensitivity to whatever it has been receiving, and sustained pressure in the red direction dials the channel's responsiveness down on that side. Now look at a plain white wall. White drives the long and middle cones equally, so on paper the channel should read zero and see nothing. But the red side of the machinery is now the tired side, under-reporting relative to its rested partner, so the freshly computed difference tips to the green side of the re-centred zero. A green patch blooms, exactly the shape of what you stared at. Reverse the colours and the ghost reverses: stare at blue, see yellow; stare at yellow, see blue. Even the light-dark channels play: after a camera flash, the world's brightness briefly inverts, and a mid-grey wall can carry a faint tint of the opposite of whatever saturated the flash. Call it opponent fatigue if you like, but hear the word fatigue in its narrow technical sense: a temporarily shifted baseline, a dialled-down gain, fully reversible within seconds, present in every normal eye, part of the price and the poetry of a system that never stops adjusting. Nothing is damaged. The ghost is the channel's fingerprint.

Host: Which means the laboratory trick has been happening to all of us all along. Where do listeners meet opponent adaptation in an ordinary day, without ever arranging a staring experiment? Give me the everyday sightings, because I want people to recognise their own week in this.

Guide: Everywhere a glance lingers. The camera flash is the classic: the burst drives every channel hard, and for the next few seconds the world wears the complement, darker where the flash was bright and tinted toward the opposites of whatever was illuminated. The bright window is another: stare at it, then the wall, and the wall carries a dark, faintly tinted rectangle for a moment. Notice, too, the quieter effect on the stared-at thing itself: keep your eyes on any saturated colour and it visibly drains, losing saturation minute by minute, sliding toward grey. That is the same adaptation working in real time, pulling the channel back toward its baseline while you watch. It is why colours look richest on the first glance and plainer under a long stare, why a fresh look at a familiar room seems to restore its colours, and why anyone who works with colour knows the eye tires and the instrument does not. And observe the rule the ghosts obey: they always respect the two axes. Red leaves a green ghost, never a blue one; blue leaves yellow, never green. Across every afterimage ever reported, the pairs hold. The ghosts never break the pairs, because the pairs are the wiring.

Host: Step back with me to the design question, the one we ask at the end of every physiology season. The eye could have shipped the three cone numbers raw and left the subtracting to the brain, a hundred million small delays downstream. What does doing the arithmetic immediately, in the retina, actually buy the whole animal?

Guide: Four payoffs, and the last of them is the frame of the whole episode. First, economy, the theme this series keeps returning to: the optic nerve is a million cables carrying an edited summary, and differences are the most compressed useful form of the colour news. Shipping raw catches would spend the cable on information the brain would only subtract away. Second, stability: because common illumination cancels in the differences, the colour report of a surface survives the day's changing light far better than any raw number could, which is the retina's opening move in the long story of colour constancy. Third, discrimination: subtraction amplifies the small differences between similar catches, and small differences between surfaces are exactly what an animal foraging among leaves needs, or a driver scanning a grey road for a red brake light. Fourth, and this is the frame, the recombination shapes experience itself: two axes, four elemental hues, brightness laid across the top. That four-hue shape of colour, the shape every listener carries in their own seeing, is not a philosophical accident; it is the geometry of the subtraction, printed onto experience. One honest boundary before we close the science. Opponency explains how the channels are built and loaded inside the eye. What the brain then does with those loaded channels, how they climb toward the cortex and become surfaces that hold their colours, is the next chapter, and we will not steal it.

Host: Then do what you do best, and give me the whole episode back as a list, slowly, the way a student should say it in an oral examination. Every channel, every carrier, every mechanism, in order.

Guide: Slowly, then. The inputs: three cone catches, long, middle and short, delivered by episode three of this season. The recombinations: a red-green channel comparing long against middle; a blue-yellow channel comparing short against long plus middle; a light-dark channel summing long plus middle, with rods assisting in the dim. The coding: signed deviations around tonic baselines, faster for one hue, slower for its partner, neutral at the hum. The carriers, from Series 3, episode nine of season six, Retinal Ganglion Cells: the red-green comparison largely riding the midget fine-detail lines, four fifths of the population; the blue-yellow comparison on its own small bistratified small-fibre channel, thinner and coarser by design; the parasol cells staying colourless for the sake of motion. The arithmetic's cousin: centre-surround subtraction, met in season seven, here aimed at cone types instead of places. The afterimage: sustained drive adapts a channel in the driven direction; a neutral look then under-reports that direction, and the opposite colour blooms; gain control, reversible in seconds, normal in every eye. And the frame, one last time: the retina's first edit reduced every spectrum to three numbers; the second edit recomputed those numbers as differences. Matching is a three-number story. Seeing is a difference story. That is the whole episode in one breath.

Host: Before the tease, the misconceptions, because this topic collects them like lint. Give me the ones you most want scrubbed from a student's notebook, and scrub them now.

Guide: Four, and they are all quiet corruptions of correct ideas. First, that opponent means colours fight it out somewhere in the mind. Nothing fights; subtraction happens. The channel is a circuit computing a difference, and the only contest is arithmetic. Second, that three cones should mean three kinds of colour experience. The experience offers four elemental hues, red, yellow, green and blue, precisely because the three numbers are recombined before they are reported; the cones supply the ingredients, and the opponency supplies the shape. Third, that afterimages are strain, or damage, or a sign the eyes need rescuing from screens. They are gain shifts, the same adjustment the retina performs continuously in ordinary looking, complete within seconds, and they occur in every normally wired eye on the planet. Fourth, and this one deserves the most care, that the red-green channel compares red light with green light. It compares cone catches. A light can be intensely coloured and still leave the red-green channel nearly silent: take a pure yellow light from the middle of the spectrum, which drives the long and middle cones almost equally, and the red-green axis sits near its neutral point even as the light blazes with colour, because that yellowness lives on the other axis entirely. The channels read the cone catches, never the colour names. Students who fix that one sentence have understood half of tonight.
