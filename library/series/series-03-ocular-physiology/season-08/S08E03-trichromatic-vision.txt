Host: Every colour you have ever seen arrived at your eye as light, and left your eye as three numbers. That one sentence is today's entire story, and we are going to earn it slowly, the way it was earned two centuries ago. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks. We are deep in Series 3, Ocular Physiology, inside Season 8, Colour and Contrast Vision, and this is episode three, Trichromatic Vision. Two threads from this season are already in your hands. Episode one, Physiology of Colour Vision, established the strange opening fact: colour is not a property of the light. The light carries wavelengths, plain physics, and nothing else. Episode two, Cone Types and Colour Detection, introduced the retinal answer: three kinds of cone, each catching light across its own overlapping band, each reporting a single caught quantity. Today those threads tie into one of the most elegant arguments in vision science, a piece of reasoning that read the retina's design out of human behaviour before any microscope could have confirmed it, and then left a puzzle on the table that its own success created. Our guide is here to run the experiment with us. Set the scene. What kind of story is a theory of colour?

Guide: A discovery story, and I will tell it as one, because the theory was not handed down from anatomy. It was reverse-engineered from something wonderfully ordinary: people judging when two patches of light look the same. Long before anyone could record the electrical whisper of a single cone, experimenters noticed that colour judgments obey a strict arithmetic, and the arithmetic has exactly three moving parts. That is the claim packed into the word trichromatic. Today we watch the claim being built in four steps: a bench with three lamps and three knobs, a rule about matching, the deduction that the eye must contain three kinds of catcher, and the strange consequence that physically different mixtures of light can be indistinguishable twins. At the end I will show you the puzzle, why three numbers cannot explain everything you feel about colour. We begin at the bench. Picture it with me.

Host: Picture it for us, then. A darkened room, a century and a half ago or more. What is physically on the bench, and what is the observer actually asked to do?

Guide: At the centre of the bench sits a small circular field, split down the middle into two half discs that touch along a vertical line. The left half is lit by the test light: some colour the experimenter has chosen, perhaps an amber, perhaps a delicate green, perhaps a colour with no friendly name at all. The right half is fed by three separate lamps. One tends toward red, one toward green, one toward blue. Each lamp has its own brightness knob, and each knob turns from off to full. The observer's whole job is to adjust the three knobs until the two halves of the circle look identical. Not nearly the same. Identical, so completely alike that the dividing line seems to dissolve. And here is the classical result, the fact everything else today stands on. Whatever colour the experimenter placed on the left, the observer could almost always find settings of the three knobs that made the right half match it. A difficult amber: matched. A pale violet: matched. A washed out bottle green: matched. Two knobs were never enough, and a fourth knob was never needed. If Series 1 gave you the everyday tour in episode nine of season three, Colour Vision, today is the engine room of that tour.

Host: Stop there, because that result hides a mountain. Three lamps sounds like a magic number pulled from a hat. Why should anyone have expected three to be enough? Walk me through the reasoning.

Guide: The reasoning is a deduction, and it is the most beautiful part of the story. Begin with the biology as episode two left it. Every small patch of light entering the eye lands on three kinds of cone, the cone families we met properly back in Season 6, episode four, Cone Physiology, and each kind answers with a single number, its catch. So suppose the eye really does sum the world into three cone catches, nothing more. For the two halves of the circle to look identical, their three catches must be equal, cone for cone. Now watch what each knob can do. Each lamp raises each of the three totals by its own fixed amount for every notch of its knob, and those amounts are properties of the lamps and the cones, measured once and remembered. So the observer is doing bookkeeping: three totals to equalise, and three knobs to move them with. When the totals agree, the halves are twins, whatever the lights themselves are doing. And the argument cuts both ways. An eye with four cone types would demand four knobs, and a fourth knob would have been indispensable. Two cone types would have made two knobs sufficient and the third a decoration. Matching behaviour is exactly what you would expect if the eye summed the world into three cone catches. The behaviour predicted the biology, backwards, from the outside.

Host: Run the counterfactual for me, because that is where the logic earns its keep. Suppose the bench had carried only two lamps. What exactly would have gone wrong?

Guide: The failure would have been instructive. With two knobs the observer could equalise two of the three totals, but the third would be left to luck, and luck fails quickly, because the three cone families respond differently to every light. There would always be test colours whose third catch could not be brought into line no matter how the two knobs were arranged: the halves would balance in overall strength and still refuse to look alike, one half leaning one way, the other half leaning another. And notice what that failure looks like from the outside. It is not a vague impression of wrongness. It is a map: for each test colour, either the two knobs reach it or they do not, and the edge of the reachable set can be measured. Three lamps erase that edge for every colour a person can see. That erasure, checked across hundreds of test colours, is the evidence. The count of knobs is not a convenience. It is the shadow the retina casts on behaviour.

Host: Which means the nineteenth century observers, without knowing it, were reading the retina's inventory from the outside in, one knob setting at a time. Who first saw this, and how did the idea take hold?

Guide: Two nineteenth century scientists are credited with the theory, and the field still carries both surnames: Young and Helmholtz. Their insight was not that the lamps were special. It was the reverse: the three lamp colours are arbitrary handles, convenient levers on the three cone families, and almost any three well separated lights could serve. The three lamp colours soon earned the name primaries, and the name misleads if it is taken literally, because the primaries are not ingredients of colour. They are adjustable keys fitted to the three locks in the retina. What matters is only that there are three independent handles, because there are three independent totals to steer. That reframing is what made the theory live, and it carried a quiet audacity with it. The locks had been counted without ever being seen.

Host: You said almost always. I noticed. What is the fine print on that word?

Guide: The fine print is honest, and it matters. A few stubborn colours, certain deep greens and certain purples among them, refused any direct match: no settings of the three knobs on one side would ever equalise the halves. The solution was elegant. The experimenter moved one lamp across the dividing line and added it to the test side, which is mathematically the same as turning that knob in reverse, subtracting instead of adding. With that one liberty, the match returned. Notice what the exception does and does not change. It adjusts the bookkeeping. It does not raise the count. Three knobs, one of them occasionally allowed to run backwards, still covered every colour a person with normal colour vision could see. The number three survived its own fine print intact.

Host: Let me say the strange thing plainly, and you correct me if I overshoot. The eye never records light. Per place, it records three numbers. The light itself is gone at the entrance. Is that fair?

Guide: It is fair, with one clarification that will earn its keep later. What the eye records, place by place, are the three catch totals: how much the long wavelength family caught, how much the middle family caught, how much the short family caught. The recipe behind those totals, which wavelengths were present and in what proportions, is destroyed at the very first stage of vision. Nothing downstream can unmix the cocktail. Everything the rest of the visual system will ever know about that patch of light is the triple. And Season 7 ended by promising us exactly this handoff: episode ten, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal, closed by saying three kinds of cone catch the world in overlapping bands, and asking how colour comes out the far end. Today supplies the arithmetic of that handoff. The eye is a three-number instrument. It compresses an endless physical variety of lights into triples, and colour vision, from here onward, is the story of what gets done with those triples. Hold the phrase: three numbers per place, recipe lost.

Host: One more question before the consequence, because listeners will ask it immediately. Where is brightness in this scheme? Three numbers sounds like colour only.

Guide: Brightness lives in the same triple, and it is worth two minutes. Scale all three catches up together, long, middle and short, and you have the same colour at a higher level: the balance among the numbers is unchanged while their overall size has grown. Change the balance, the ratios among the three numbers, and you have changed the colour itself. So every card in the catalogue really describes a family: one shape of balance, available at many levels of strength, from glimmer to glare. This is why a scene dimmed by evening stays recognisably itself. The balances survive while the levels fall. It is also why the triple, not the colour word, is the honest unit of what the eye records. Brightness and colour are not two separate recordings made by two separate machines. They are two things we say about one three-number report.

Host: Now the second word on today's marquee, the consequence everyone remembers: metamerism. Give it to us in plain words, no geometry, no jargon.

Guide: Metamerism, in plain words: different physical mixtures of light can land the same three cone catches, and when they do, they look identical. The eye cannot tell them apart. Not with attention, not with practice, not with effort. There is no report the retina could make, because the two mixtures write the same three numbers, and the retina only ever reads the numbers. I want to insist that this is not a failure of the eye, a fault to be corrected, an illusion to be debunked. It is the direct, lawful consequence of being a three-number instrument. A machine that compresses light into three totals must, by arithmetic, lump endlessly many different lights into the same answer. Metamerism is that lumping given a name. Every match produced at that bench was a metamerism: the mixture on the right was physically a different light from the test colour on the left, and it looked the same because it landed the same three catches. The twins are not rare. In a strict sense, they are everywhere. Nearly every coloured thing you look at could be replaced, light for light, by some simpler mixture that would look the same, and the world hides this from you effortlessly, because hiding it is what three-number seeing means.

Host: Give me the famous pair, then, the twins every textbook reaches for. One is a single wavelength of light. The other is a mixture. Walk us through them slowly.

Guide: The single wavelength is a pure spectral yellow, light of about five hundred and eighty nanometres, one crisp line in the spectrum. The mixture is two lamps at once, one red and one green, and no yellow anywhere in the room. Tune the red and green knobs carefully and the mixture becomes the yellow's twin. The long cone family reads the same total from both halves. The middle family reads the same total from both halves. The short family is barely involved on either side, its catch a whisper in both cases, and the tiny leftover difference is far too small for the eye to notice. To your visual system the two halves are the same colour, full stop, though a prism would instantly reveal that one half is one wavelength and the other is a partnership of two. That is the demonstration to carry out of today: spectral yellow and a red plus green mixture are twins to the retina. Neither the word pure nor the word mixture appears anywhere in the triple. The retina has no column for ingredients.

Host: And that is precisely why the glowing rectangles around modern life can get away with something that sounds like fraud. A screen that contains no yellow lamp at all can show you yellow. Explain the trick.

Guide: The trick is the matching principle, engineered. A display builds every colour out of three lamp types, one pressing hardest on the long cone family, one on the middle, one on the short. Those are the same three independent handles the bench lamps provided. By setting three brightnesses, the machine lands on whatever triple it wants, and the triple is all the eye will ever read. So the screen never makes yellow light. It makes the three-number address of yellow, and your three-number instrument, reading that address, reports yellow, because reporting yellow is all it can do. The screen fools no one by cunning. It simply speaks the eye's own compression language fluently. And notice the condition hiding in the engineering: the three lamp types must pull on the cone families independently enough to reach different triples, exactly the requirement the bench lamps had to meet. When they do, three lamp types can imitate the look of any colour within their reach. The physics is different from the bench. The bookkeeping is identical.

Host: Beautiful, and slightly unsettling. Before we leave the twins, can anything at all tell them apart, some test the eye itself could run? Or is the secret perfectly kept?

Guide: The eye can never break the secret, because the eye has no instrument inside it besides the three catches. Physics can break it in a moment. Send the two halves through a prism and they part ways at once: the spectral yellow stays a single bar, while the mixture fans out into its two contributions. A spectroscope, an instrument that sorts light by wavelength, reads recipes. The retina reads only totals. And that asymmetry is worth stating exactly, because students misplace it constantly. Metamerism is not a fact about light, since the lights are genuinely different. It is a fact about observers, about any eye built on three catches. The twins are identical to the retina and strangers to the spectroscopist, and both descriptions are true at once. Even the prism only helps because it intervenes before the eye ever sees the light. Once the three numbers are written, no effort of looking recovers the recipe.

Host: Step back with me from the mechanism for one question. Why three? Is there a reason the instrument compresses to exactly three numbers rather than two or four?

Guide: The honest answer has two layers. The shallow layer is chemistry. The retina is built from light-catching pigments, and the three families episode two introduced have broad, overlapping bands of sensitivity rather than narrow needles. Broad bands catch more of whatever light arrives, a bargain the eye cannot refuse, but broad bands also blur fine spectral detail, and three of them, placed where they are, proved enough to separate the colours that matter to a daylight animal. The deeper layer sits behind a fence I will respect: why the eye settled on that particular bargain is a story bigger than today's bench, and episode two already carried the tuning curves. What the bench adds is the behavioural consequence. Because the bands are broad and few, the compression is drastic, and because the compression is drastic, the twins are everywhere. The three-number instrument is not an arbitrary choice someone made. It is what that chemistry looks like from the outside.

Host: Bookkeeping time, then. You keep saying three numbers per place. Where do all those triples live, and what do we call the collection? Give us colour space without the geometry.

Guide: Without geometry, colour space is a catalogue. Imagine a filing system in which the card for every colour carries three numbers: the catch of the long family, the catch of the middle family, the catch of the short family. Every colour a person can see is one card in that catalogue, one address written as a triple. Change the light entering the eye and you change the three numbers, which is the same thing as moving to another card. Mixing lights, at the bench or on a screen, is walking from card to card by turning knobs. The whole collection of cards the eye can actually reach, every combination of three catches the cone families can genuinely produce together, is what scientists call colour space. The word space here means the collection itself, not a room with a floor plan, and we will keep it that plain. One more honest note: not every triple you could write down is reachable. Catches have floors and ceilings, the cone families overlap so they cannot be driven fully independently, and some combinations are simply not on offer. The catalogue holds only the addresses the eye can truly generate, and that restraint is part of the design. Familiar colours earn familiar addresses: the triple behind a ripe lemon sits far from the triple behind a midday sky, and the whole difference is three numbers read together.

Host: Our listeners hold retinoscopes and trial lenses, not benches. Where does the three-number principle touch an ordinary Tuesday at the chair?

Guide: In two places, one formal and one everyday. Formally: colour vision is probed clinically through comparison and matching behaviour, because colour matching is the readout of the three-number instrument. When an observer's judgments need different proportions from the textbook pattern, the pattern of that difference is itself the clinical information. Series 1 built the testing ground in episode nine of season six, Colour Vision Testing, and the logic underneath those tests is today's bench. Everyday: you already own the classic demonstration of metamerism, the two garments that matched perfectly under the shop's lamps and parted company the moment they stepped into daylight. The lights differed, the surfaces reflected genuinely different mixtures, and under one lamp the triples collided while under the other they separated. Nothing was wrong with the customer's eyes, and nothing was wrong with either garment. The triples simply moved. Being able to say that sentence, calmly and correctly, is what it looks like when physiology quietly rescues a Tuesday conversation.

Host: Ritual time, as every season of ours has made it. What myths does tonight's bench kill? Line them up.

Guide: Four. First, the myth that the primaries are properties of light, three sacred wavelengths hiding in the spectrum. No: the primaries are properties of the observer, handles chosen to fit three cone families, and many triplets of lights would serve. Second, the myth that the eye sees the ingredients of a mixture, that looking at the matched yellow somehow detects red and green in partnership. No: the recipe is destroyed at the entrance, and the retina has no column for ingredients, only totals. Third, the myth that metamerism is a fault, a failure of colour vision to do its job properly. No: it is the lawful consequence of compression, and compression is the design, not a defect in it. Fourth, and subtlest, the myth that three numbers means three colours, that the eye sees only red, green and blue and assembles everything else from conscious mixture. No: the three catches index an enormous catalogue of colour experiences. The triples are inputs to a construction, not the palette you experience. The yellow you see is not three numbers pasted together. It is what the system builds from three numbers. That distinction is the doorway to our final turn today.

Host: Before that doorway, the examination. Give us the numbers the way you would want a student to recite them back, slowly, in order.

Guide: In order. Three kinds of cone, met properly in episode two of this season, Cone Types and Colour Detection, each with its overlapping band of sensitivity. Three lamps on the bench, one handle per cone family, their colours convenient rather than sacred. Three totals per place, the catches, the only quantities the retina reports. Three knobs, therefore, suffice: any colour a person can see can be matched by mixing three adjustable lights in the right proportions, and the stubborn cases surrender to one knob running in reverse without ever raising the count to four. Metamerism: different lights, same three catches, identical appearance, the reason screens can imitate any colour from three lamp types and the reason two mixtures can be twins to the retina and strangers to a prism. Colour space: the catalogue of reachable triples, every visible colour one address, no geometry required. And the handoff: Season 7, episode ten, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal, promised the three-number stage, and tonight we have watched it behave exactly as promised. That is the shape of the classical theory, recited in one breath.

Host: One last ritual question before the limit. Across all of that, which detail do you find students underestimate the longest?

Guide: That the observer is the instrument. In every experiment we described today, the measuring device was a human being making a judgment of identity, and the knob settings were the data. The whole inventory of the retina was read out of people saying same and not same, patiently, thousands of times. And behind that sits the deeper underestimate: the recipe is destroyed, permanently, at the entrance. You have never seen a wavelength. You have only ever seen three-number summaries, and no act of attention can reach back past the summary to the light that made it. Vision is a one-way door between physics and experience. Students nod at that sentence, and then spend years quietly assuming they can see mixtures as mixtures. The bench says otherwise, and the bench was right.

Host: Now the limit you promised at the start. The matching story feels complete, even triumphant. Three knobs, three numbers, the whole catalogue. So here is my question, and I will aim it straight at the colour red. What can this theory not explain?

Guide: Here is the crack in the triumph, and it is a strange one. Matching experiments prove the three-number stage, but numbers are not impressions, and impressions are where the trouble starts. Ask yourself what colours actually feel like from the inside. Yellow can look reddish, leaning toward orange, or greenish, leaning toward lime, and orange honestly seems to hold both. Blue can seem crisp and cold or softened toward white. But red never, under any circumstance, looks greenish. Green never looks reddish. No blue ever looks yellowish. Those pairs behave like poles, like opposite directions that refuse to blend, and nothing in a three-number catalogue records that texture. The triples that predict matching behaviour are silent about the impressions that ride along with it. A theory that only counts catches can say which lights look identical; it cannot say why the space of colour feelings has poles and opposites at all, why some impressions feel like blends while others refuse to blend. The three-number stage is real, proved by the bench, and incomplete. To explain the impressions, the three catches must be reread against each other, compared rather than merely collected, and that rereading is a second stage of wiring with its own rules.
