# Description: This episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks follows the visual signal out of the eye and into the brain's visual workroom at the very back of the head. Learn how cells in the first cortical map grow fussy about the orientation of edges, and how that fussiness becomes the raw material of shape. Follow the two great streams out of that map: a what pathway running downward toward the temporal lobe that assembles identity, and a where and how pathway running upward toward the parietal lobe that assembles location, motion and guided action. Then watch the hierarchy climb, as receptive fields grow larger and fussier from edges to corners to whole objects, inheriting every difference map the retina already drew. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want the physiology of seeing explained for the ear.

Episode one, From Photoreceptor to Brain, promised that the retina's finished report would leave the eye for higher ground, and every episode since has watched that report being prepared: the neural circuits of episode two, the centre and surround conversations of episode three, the resolving power of episodes four and five, the contrast machinery of episode six, the motion specialists of episode seven, and last time, in episode eight, Edge Detection, the retina's finest draft of where every boundary in the world begins. Tonight the report finally arrives at its destination: the folded sheet of cortex at the very back of the head, where the brain's visual workroom begins. This is Visual Information Processing. Guide, set the frame for us. What changes now that the signal has left the eye, and what is tonight's plan?

Guide: The frame is a change of character, in three ways. First, the cast changes. We leave the retina's named cells behind and speak of cortical cells, grouped not by their wiring diagrams but by what they answer to, because up here the question a cell asks matters more than the cell's family name. Second, the job changes. Up to now the questions have been about reporting: differences, contrasts, edges, motion, all of it a curated summary of the light. From tonight the questions are about building: what the brain starts to make from that summary. Third, the ambition changes, so let me draw tonight's boundary before we begin. This is the cortical overview, the general story of how features climb the ladder, and it walks four steps. Step one, the arrival: how the signal physically reaches the back of the head. Step two, the first cortical stop, where we discover cells that are astonishingly fussy about the orientation of edges. Step three, the two great streams that leave that first map, one asking what a thing is, the other asking where it is and how to act on it. Step four, the hierarchy: receptive fields that grow and grow fussier as you climb, from edges to corners to whole objects. And one promise to keep us honest all evening: nothing the cortex does replaces the retina's work. It inherits it.

Host: Then start at the arrival. The signal leaves the eye as the ganglion cells' traffic. Walk me down the road from the back of the eye to the back of the head, briefly, because Series 2 owns the full map and I only want what tonight's story needs.

Guide: Briefly, then, with the anatomy credited where it lives. The ganglion cells' axons, the million-odd output fibres we counted in episode nine of Season 6, Retinal Ganglion Cells, gather at the disc and leave the eye as the optic nerve. Half the fibres cross on their journey and half stay on their own side, which is why each hemisphere ends up reading the opposite half of the visual world. The traffic then fans out across the floor of the skull into the optic radiations, a broad fan of fibres sweeping back through each hemisphere, and lands in the first cortical map at the back of the head. Series 2, episode five of season ten, Optic Radiations, owns that fan fibre by fibre, and Series 2, episode ten of season ten, Complete Visual Pathway - Retina to Cortex, walks the whole road end to end, so tonight we simply stand at the destination and ask what happens next. But keep one anatomical detail in your pocket, because it shapes everything that follows. The radiations are not a delivery van dumping a single parcel at a single door. They are sorted lanes. The retina's parallel channels leave the eye separated, travel separated, and arrive separated, each lane handing its traffic to its own receiving layer in the map. The sorting that began in the retina survives the journey intact.

Host: And the destination itself, the first map: where does Series 2's anatomy lesson place it, and what kind of thing is it?

Guide: The anatomy belongs to Series 2, episode six of season ten, Visual Cortex, which drew the first cortical map as a sheet of grey matter only a couple of millimetres thick, folded at the very back of the brain, with the visual world laid out across it point for point: a map of the retina printed on the surface of the cortex, and magnified absurdly at the centre, where the fovea's tiny estate claims an outsized share of the cortical territory. That lesson says where the map is and how it is drawn. Tonight we animate it. The question that unlocks the whole episode is disarmingly simple. A ganglion cell reported spots: light here, dark there, a centre against its surround. What does the first cortical cell do with spots?

Host: And the answer is the famous fussiness. Tell us what a cell in this first map actually answers to. Still spots?

Guide: No, and this is the discovery that organises everything tonight. The cortex reads edges. Record from a single cell in the first map while patterns are painted on a screen in front of the eyes, and a personality appears. Some cells ignore a vertical bar entirely and fire furiously for a horizontal one. Others prefer vertical. Others answer best for an angle in between, and they are picky about it: swing the bar by a few degrees either way and the response fades away. Each cell carries a preferred orientation, and across any small patch of the map the whole dial of angles is covered, cell by cell, vertical through horizontal and every tilt between, so wherever a contour crosses the map, a full set of orientation specialists stands ready for its angle. This is what we mean when we say the cortex reads edges as shapes-in-progress. A spot says here. An edge says here and this way: a line, with a direction to it. Every contour in the room around you right now, the top of a door, the rim of a cup, the stroke of a letter, is being reported as a chorus of orientation specialists, each calling out its own angle wherever that contour crosses its patch of the map.

Host: Before we climb, honour the method for a moment. How does anyone know what a single cell in a living brain prefers? This sounds like listening to one wire in a switchboard.

Guide: That is exactly the spirit of the method, and it is one of the loveliest experiments in all of physiology. Researchers recorded the electrical activity of single cortical cells while sweeping controlled patterns past the eyes: bars, edges, gratings, each rotated through angle after angle. Watch the meter as a bar rotates and the story tells itself: quiet, quiet, quiet, a burst of firing as the bar reaches the cell's preferred tilt, quiet again as it moves on. The cell is not photographing the pattern. It is answering a question, and it answers only when its own question is asked. One more finding completes the rung, and it is the hinge of tonight's story. The cortical cell's fussiness is assembled out of the retina's difference conversations. Its inputs come from cells that compared centres with surrounds, and by combining several of those spot-difference reports in a row, the cortex builds a detector that answers to an edge rather than a spot. So the cortex did not invent edge reading. It recombined what the retina reported. That is the first rung of the ladder, and every rung above it repeats the very same trick: take the answers below, combine them, and ask a sharper question.

Host: Say more about that ladder, because it is the episode's spine. What happens as you climb above the first map? Do the cells simply get better at edges?

Guide: They get bigger and fussier at the same time, and that double movement is the whole principle of hierarchical processing. Near the bottom of the ladder, a cell answers to a short edge of one orientation at one small place in the world. Climb a rung and the receptive fields widen, and the questions become combinations: a cell starts answering not to a single edge but to an arrangement of them, an edge that stops, a corner, a junction where two orientations meet. Climb further and the combinations become parts: a curve, a loop, the shape of a handle. Higher still, cells answer to whole objects, or to their signatures, and the cells feeding them have gathered votes from hundreds of simpler detectors below. A corner is edges arranged. An object is corners, curves and surfaces arranged. Nothing magical is added at any step. Each rung simply recombines the rung below into something more specific, which is why we call it progressive assembly, the logic of a bricklayer who never sees the cathedral. And notice what the climbing buys, because it is not free. A cell that answered to everything would tell the brain nothing. Specificity is the currency up here, and it is bought by widening fields and tightening preferences, rung by rung, in parallel streams that never merge back into one wire.

Host: Now a question about scale, because something has been bothering me. The retina compressed a hundred million photoreceptors into a little over a million output fibres. Does the brain stay that economical, or does the picture change at the cortex?

Guide: The picture reverses, and this is one of the underestimated wonders of the whole system. The first cortical map is enormously larger than its input. For every incoming fibre from the retina, the cortex fields a workforce of many, many cells, dozens upon dozens, each asking its own narrow question about the same small patch of the world. Where the retina had to spend its million fibres carefully, one reporter covering a whole neighbourhood of differences, the cortex spends generously, stationing a battery of specialists at every point: this one for vertical, that one for a slight tilt, another for a corner forming there. So the story of the visual system is compression, then expansion. The eye squeezes the world down to what matters, and the brain unpacks that summary into a vast parallel workforce, each member re-examining it from a different angle. It is as if a news agency sent one wire report to a city of analysts, and every analyst pulled a different fact out of the same page. The expansion is why the cortex can be fussy at all. Fussiness needs manpower, and the cortex has it, millions of narrow specialists working at the same instant.

Host: Two streams. Introduce them properly. What leaves the first map, and where do the roads run?

Guide: Two great roads leave the first map, and they divide the brain's visual work between two great questions. The first road runs downward and forward, along the side of the brain toward the temporal lobe, low and toward the temples. This is the what pathway, which anatomists call the ventral stream. Its question is identity. What am I looking at? Its work is the assembly of meaning from shape: this is a face, this is a letter, this is your mug, the one with the chipped rim. The second road runs upward and backward, toward the top of the brain and the parietal lobe, high and toward the crown. This is the where and how pathway, the dorsal stream. Its questions are location, motion and action. Where is it? Which way is it moving? How do I reach for it, guide my hand, time my step? Identity running downward, action running upward. Two questions the visual world never stops asking, answered by two streams that run at the same time, in parallel, from the very first map onward.

Host: Hold on, that division deserves a challenge. Why would the brain split the job in two? One stream asking everything seems simpler.

Guide: Simpler, and impossible, and the reason is already sitting in the retina. The two streams are not a new idea the brain thought up late in evolution; they are the continuation of a split that began at the ganglion cells. Remember episode nine of Season 6, Retinal Ganglion Cells: the retina ships parallel families. The midget family, four fifths of the output, small fields, sustained responses, fine detail, red against green: a private-line system built for discrimination. The parasol family, a tenth of the output, large fields, transient responses, thick fast axons: a system built for timing and motion. Those families travel the radiations in their sorted lanes and feed different cortical emphases, and the emphases grow into the streams. Discrimination traffic, the material of identity, feeds the what pathway. Timing and motion traffic, the material of action, feeds the where and how pathway. And the two jobs genuinely need different arithmetic. Knowing that the cup is your cup asks for exquisite shape comparison, detail held still and studied. Catching the cup as it slides off the table asks for position, speed and timing now, and would always rather have a coarse answer fast than a fine answer late. Two questions, two currencies, two streams. The retina's parallel channels were the streams all along, still running.

Host: Then let us walk one object through the whole machine, because parallel streams can sound abstract. Take my mug. What happens to it, stream by stream, in the moments I glance across the desk?

Guide: Your mug, in the first instants, is not yet a mug anywhere. It is a set of edges, each orientation reported at the first map, each location and each motion reported in its own lanes. Then the two roads take over at once. Downward, in the what pathway, the oriented strokes combine into the curved handle and the cylinder, the parts combine into the whole, and the whole is matched against everything you have ever seen, so the object becomes recognised: a mug, your mug, distinct from the bowl beside it. Upward, in the where and how pathway, the same edges are read for position and distance, the motion signals report whether it is sliding, and the stream hands the movement system what it needs: where the handle sits, how far the rim stands, when to close the fingers. Notice the elegant division of answers. The what pathway's answer is a name. The where and how pathway's answer is a plan. And both answers arrive together, seamlessly, every waking moment, which is why vision feels like one act when it is really a duet.

Host: One more property before we leave the mug, because it explains something patients never think to ask. The mug is recognised from across the room, up close, on the left shelf or the right, half-turned away. The identity never wobbles. How does a hierarchy of picky cells deliver recognition that is so stable?

Guide: By building stairs that end well above the sliver. Each rung's cells own narrower and narrower questions, but their answers get pooled as they climb, so the higher rungs stop caring about exactly where the mug sits, exactly how far away it is, exactly which way it faces. That is called invariance, and it is the what pathway's quiet triumph: identity delivered independent of position and size, within limits. The same strokes assemble to the same name from any shelf in the house. But notice the price, because it shows why the second stream exists. A representation that has thrown away position and size is wonderful for recognition and useless for action. To reach for the mug you need precisely what the what pathway discarded: where it is, how big it looks right now, when to close your hand. The where and how pathway keeps those quantities fresh, updated moment by moment as you and the world move. So the one object carries two simultaneous descriptions, each tuned to a different purpose: a label that ignores place, and a guidance system that lives on it. Vision runs both at once, and you only ever notice the label.

Host: Let us make the what pathway concrete for our listeners' daily work, because you said letters. Say more. What does an acuity chart have to do with any of this?

Guide: Everything, and this is the turn I most want an optometry audience to carry home. Every letter on the chart is an assembly of oriented strokes. An L is a vertical edge meeting a horizontal one. An X is four orientations crossing at a point. When a patient reads that chart, the first cortical map logs the orientations, the hierarchy assembles the corners and junctions, and the what pathway delivers the identity: that shape is a K. The whole journey we have drawn tonight, orientation to corner to object, runs behind every line of letters a patient has ever read. And the reason a letter stays legible only down to a certain size is that its stroke orientations eventually fall below the resolving power of the channels feeding the map. The chart is, quite literally, testing whether the ladder's first rungs can still be climbed for shapes that small. The same goes for reading a page: words are higher rungs built from letters as parts. Nothing about tonight is abstract. It is the physiology of the single most common task in your consulting room.

Host: And the where and how pathway in daily life? Give it its everyday portrait too.

Guide: It is the stream of every reach, step and catch. When you lift the mug without looking, thread a key into a lock, catch keys tossed across the room, or step off a curb while a cyclist passes, the where and how pathway is converting the retina's motion and position reports into guidance for your hands and your eyes. Episode seven, Motion Detection, taught the retina's motion machinery; this stream is where that machinery's output becomes usable: not just change reported, but change answered. Its signature is speed, and its delight is the present moment.

Host: You said its signature is speed. Make that concrete. Are the two streams literally running at different tempos?

Guide: They can be, and the tempo difference starts in the retina and never quite closes. The parasol family's axons are the thickest and fastest in the optic nerve, their responses transient and quick to rise and fall, and their traffic reaches the cortex measurably earlier than the midget family's finer, slower, sustained reports. Follow those lanes onward and the dorsal stream keeps its head start: position and motion are computed on the freshest data available, because a guidance system steering a hand toward a moving object is always acting on the recent past, and the shorter that past, the better the action. The what pathway trades a little of that urgency for thoroughness, holding the signal longer and comparing more carefully, because identity rewards deliberation. Neither stream is the better one. They are two answers to two clocks: the clock of the moment, and the clock of the considered answer. Everyday vision is the duet of the two, and your hand usually starts moving before the name is fully settled, which is exactly as it should be.

Host: Sweep the misconceptions now, the way we always do. Where do listeners most often go wrong about this material?

Guide: Four traps, one each. Trap one, the picture postcard. People imagine the eye mails a photograph to the brain and the brain unfolds it on a screen. No picture ever travels. What travels is a census of differences and angles, and the scene is rebuilt from reports. Trap two, the one cell that knows your face. The hierarchy grows very specific, and it is tempting to imagine a single cell somewhere holding the whole mug, one cell for every memory. Recognition is not stored in one wire. It is an assembly across populations of cells, and the specificity keeps growing without ever narrowing to a single point. Trap three, two separate minds. The streams sound like a seeing brain and a doing brain running side by side. In everyday vision they converse constantly, and every task borrows from both: recognising the mug uses its position; reaching for the mug uses its identity. Parallel does not mean solitary. Trap four, the passive receiver. The cortex is not a screen the eye projects onto. It is a workshop. The eye prepares; the brain constructs. Nothing about what you are seeing right now is simply received.

Host: Time for the oral examination. Give us the recap, slowly, the way a student should be able to say it back.

Guide: The list, in order. The visual signal leaves the eye as parallel channels inside the ganglion cells' output, crosses in its halves, fans out through the optic radiations, and lands at the first cortical map at the back of the head. The first map's cells are tuned to orientation: vertical, horizontal, every tilt between, the full dial covered for every patch of the visual world, so the cortex reads edges as shapes-in-progress. The hierarchy climbs by recombination: receptive fields grow larger and fussier, from edges to corners to parts to objects, each rung built from the one below, with the cortex fielding a large workforce for every incoming fibre. Two great streams leave the map: the what pathway, the ventral stream, running downward toward the temporal lobe, assembling identity; the where and how pathway, the dorsal stream, running upward toward the parietal lobe, assembling location, motion and action. The streams continue the retina's own split, midget-style discrimination traffic feeding the what pathway and parasol-style timing traffic feeding the where and how pathway, with recognition made stable by pooling and action kept fresh by speed. And nothing replaces the retina's work. Every rung inherits it. That is visual information processing in seven sentences.

Host: One last teaching turn before the tease. Across all of this, which single detail do you find students underestimate the most?

Guide: That no cell in the entire system ever sees the whole scene. Not at the retina, not at the first map, not at the top of either stream. Every neuron, at every rung, answers to a sliver: one patch, one angle, one property. The seamless world you seem to look at directly is assembled, in real time, out of thousands of narrow specialists reporting in parallel, and it is assembled so fast and so smoothly that it never feels like assembly at all. Students keep waiting for the cell that holds the whole picture, and it never comes, because the picture is not held anywhere. It is performed, moment by moment, by a workforce that never once sees what it is building. That is the quiet astonishment of tonight's material, and it is worth sitting with before the finale.
