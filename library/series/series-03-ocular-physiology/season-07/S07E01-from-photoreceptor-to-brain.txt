# Description: Season seven of Series 3 opens with the journey of sight itself, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks follows the retina's edited report out of the eye and into the brain, station by station. The million-plus ganglion axons leave as the optic nerve, the chiasm re-sorts the two eyes' reports into halves of the world, the lateral geniculate body acts as a regulating gateway rather than a passive relay, and the radiations fan the signal back to the visual cortex, where the map of the scene is rebuilt and edges, motion and colour begin to be assembled. Along the way we revisit last season's departure, when the retina shipped its curated, parallel, difference-based dispatch, and we set the theme of the season: every station transforms rather than copies. Made for optometry students, optical teams and curious listeners who want the visual pathway taught as physiology, not just anatomy.

Host: Welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology, and welcome to a brand new season. Last season, Retina Physiology, we went inside the deepest layer of the eye and found not camera film but a living outpost of the brain: photoreceptors catching single particles of light, a cascade turning each catch into a voltage, and circuits editing the result. By the finale, Retinal Information Coding, the retina was dispatching a curated, parallel, difference-based report, carried by roughly a million cables, describing the world as differences rather than as a photograph. With the report packed, this season follows it out the door. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to Season 7, Visual Physiology: ten episodes that walk the message from photoreceptor to brain. After today comes Retinal Neural Circuits, then Centre-Surround Processing, Spatial Resolution, Temporal Resolution, Contrast Processing, Motion Detection, Edge Detection, Visual Information Processing and, in the finale, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal. Today is the itinerary: the edited signal's journey out of the eye, through its stations and into the cortex at the very back of the head, watching what changes at every stop. Set the frame. What, exactly, left the ganglion cells at the end of last season, and what kind of journey is it?

Guide: Thank you, good to be back. The frame is the departure, because this season is the story of what happens after it. Last season we learned that the retina does not send a picture: around one hundred and twenty million rods and six million cones report to the million-plus output cells of episode nine, each ganglion cell carrying an edited account of a small territory, a centre that reports and a ring that argues, sorted into parallel streams for fine detail, for motion and timing, and for blue against yellow. And here is the theme that organises all ten episodes: no station on the route simply passes the cargo along. At every stop something happens to it: re-sorted, re-weighted, re-encoded, gated, and finally taken apart and rebuilt as a map. The eye hands over a million difference-reports; the brain assembles a scene. Today we walk the whole route from above, so every later episode knows where it stands on the map.

Host: Then give us the route in plain words first. Before we stop at any of the names, optic nerve, chiasm, lateral geniculate body, radiations, visual cortex, sketch the trip as a whole: the stations in order, and roughly where each sits in the head.

Guide: The trip has five stations, almost every one sitting deep in the middle of the head. Station one: the optic nerve. The ganglion cell axons leave through the optic disc, the doorway we called the blind spot last season, and run as one thick cable per eye toward the brain. Station two: the optic chiasm, the crossing point where the two nerves meet and partially swap their contents. Station three: the lateral geniculate body, a six-layered nucleus of the thalamus, close to the geometric centre of the brain. Station four: the optic radiations, broad fans of fibres sweeping backward through each hemisphere. Station five: the visual cortex, the folded sheet at the very back of the head, where the map of the scene is rebuilt and the assembly of features begins. Two road signs. First, anatomy: every station was mapped in Series 2's neuroanatomy season, and the whole route was drawn end to end in Series 2, episode ten of season ten, Complete Visual Pathway - Retina to Cortex. That episode is the map; this season is the traffic and the customs houses. Second, history: the library walked this road once before, in Series 1, episode ten of season two, Following the Path of Vision; today we walk it again with the physiology filled in. And the journey is short: a few centimetres from the back of the eye to the back of the head, a few hundredths of a second, and possibly the busiest short trip in the body.

Host: Station one, then: the optic nerve. We spent a full episode on the ganglion cells last season, so today I want the departure itself. What physically leaves the eye, how much of it, and what has already changed about the signal by the time it is in the nerve?

Guide: What leaves is the axons of the ganglion cells, about one point two million of them by the classic count we quoted last season, gathered into a single cable about as thick as a pencil lead. The cable's anatomy, its course, its canal, its coverings, belongs to Series 2, episode one of season ten, Optic Nerve; today we watch the traffic, and two things have changed by the time it is in the nerve. First, the code. Most retinal neurons speak in graded voltages, easing up and down like a dimmer. Ganglion cells translate that into action potentials, brief identical spikes whose rate carries the message, and spikes regenerate along the axon, so the report travels without fading. The retina's output cells are the journey's first translators: from analog to digital. Second, the territory. The optic nerve is not a peripheral nerve that happens to connect to the brain; it is a tract of the brain itself, and we heard why in episode one of last season: the retina grew out of embryonic brain tissue, and the nerve is the stalk that never separated. When the cables leave the eye they are not leaving home; they are moving from one room of the brain to the rest of it. The departure in one sentence: the eye ships a parallel, digital, difference-based report, and it ships it on brain infrastructure.

Host: There are two such cables, one per eye, running in parallel until the crossing. One footnote: the doorway they leave through is the optic disc, the blind spot, and nobody notices the hole. Hold that thought and take us to station two. What actually happens at the optic chiasm?

Guide: The hole is worth holding: each retina misses a patch, the brain never shows the gap, and the filling-in is one of the transformations further down the route. Now, the chiasm. It sits at the front of the brain, where the two optic nerves meet, and its anatomy was laid out in Series 2, episode two of season ten, Optic Chiasm. What happens there is the first great transformation of the journey, and it changes order, not content. Up to the chiasm, every cable is sorted by eye: the left nerve carries the left eye's report, the right nerve the right eye's. At the chiasm, the axons from the nose-facing half of each retina cross to the opposite side, while the axons from the temple-facing half stay put. Follow the consequence, because it is beautiful. Beyond the chiasm each cable, now called the optic tract, carries a mixture of the two bundles, and the mixture is not random: each tract now carries exactly one half of the world, as seen by both eyes together. Remember that the image reverses on the retina, so the world's left half paints itself onto the nose-facing half of the left eye's retina and the temple-facing half of the right eye's, and at the crossing those two contributions join. The sorting has flipped from whose eye reported to where things sit in the world.

Host: Stay on that flip a moment, because it is the strangest manoeuvre on the journey. The eye reports eye by eye; the brain insists on world half by world half. Why? What does the re-sorting buy, and what does the two eyes' overlap have to do with it?

Guide: Because the two eyes are not two independent cameras whose pictures merely need stacking. They are two viewpoints on one scene, and across the overlapping middle both eyes report on the same objects. The brain's task is to combine those reports into one stable scene with depth, and combination only works if matching information can be brought together. Sorting by world-half does exactly that: the two eyes' accounts of one half of the scene now travel side by side in one tract, ready to be compared cell by cell in the cortex. The chiasm does not combine anything; it arranges the reports so combination becomes possible. That is the transform at this station: the address system changes. Before the chiasm, an axon's identity is eye and retinal territory; after it, a tract's identity is a half of the world. And notice the elegant side effect: each hemisphere reads the half of the scene it is not on, the left hemisphere taking the right half of the world. One bookkeeping detail: the swap is partial. Only the nose-facing half of each retina crosses, roughly half the fibres in each nerve, and beyond the interchange the two tracts run a short distance along the underside of the brain.

Host: Which brings us to station three, the lateral geniculate body. Old textbooks introduce it almost apologetically, as a relay station on the way to the cortex. You have always resisted that word. Make the case: what is it actually for, and what changes when the signal arrives?

Guide: Start with what it is: a nucleus of the thalamus tucked deep in the middle of the head, and the first station that is unambiguously brain; the retina was brain-adjacent, the nerve was brain tract, and now the report arrives inside the brain itself. Its layered anatomy belongs to Series 2, episode four of season ten, Lateral Geniculate Body; the physiology is ours. The six layers are not decoration. Four fine, cellular layers receive the fine-detail stream from the midget ganglion cells of last season; two thick layers beneath receive the motion-and-timing stream from the parasol cells; between them sit interlaminar sheets carrying the blue-against-yellow stream from the small bistratified cells. Last season's three shipping families arrive at three separate classes of desk. Each layer takes input from one eye only, so the two eyes' accounts still live on separate floors. But the floors are stacked in register: the map in every layer aligns with the maps above and below it, point for point, so the same patch of the world has the same address on every floor. The two eyes' reports of the same object sit one floor apart, waiting for the cortex to compare them. The sorting the chiasm began, the geniculate completes: each half of the world is filed as six registered copies, one per stream and eye, each still carrying its own edited story.

Host: And yet a filing cabinet is still a relay under another name. Your reason for resisting the word was control. Tell us about the traffic running the wrong way: how much of the wiring there comes down from the cortex rather than up from the eye, and what does the gate do with it?

Guide: Here is the number that changed how everyone thinks about this station: by the classic estimates, fibres descending from the cortex outnumber the fibres arriving from the retina by roughly ten to one. For every cable bringing the report up, about ten come down to talk about it. The geniculate is not a lobby the signal walks through; it is a gatehouse whose staff receive more instruction from upstairs than cargo from the eye. What does the gate do? It regulates. Pathways from the brainstem carry the brain's general state, arousal, alertness, attention, and the geniculate uses them to set the gain on what passes through. Drowsy, and throughput is damped; alert and searching, and it opens. The same retinal report can leave this station emphasised or quietened depending on the state of the brain about to receive it. The gate also sharpens: the centre-versus-surround logic the retina built is strengthened here, and that logic is the material of episode three, Centre-Surround Processing, so I only name it today. Hence the honest name: regulating gateway, not passive relay. The transformation at this stop is control, and how loudly the report speaks on the next leg is decided here, in dialogue with the brain it is about to enter.

Host: From the gatehouse, the signal still has to reach the back of the head. Station four is the fan of fibres called the optic radiations. The thalamus sits near the centre of the brain, the cortex at the very back. Give us the geography, and what, if anything, changes inside the fan.

Guide: The fan is real. The axons leaving the lateral geniculate body spread into a broad sheet sweeping backward through the white matter of each hemisphere toward the occipital lobe, and the sheet is not one road but several: fibres for the upper half of the scene arc one way, those for the lower half sweep another, as the complete-pathway episode drew them. What changes in the fan? Two things, neither computation. First, fidelity: the map is carried intact, point for point, because the cortex at the far end will rebuild the scene as a map, and a map cannot arrive shuffled. The radiations are cabling with a promise attached: nothing gets scrambled on our watch. Second, budget. The fan is not apportioned equally. Remember last season's census: at the fovea, one ganglion cell serves a single cone, a private line. That privilege persists along the whole route. The small centre of the scene, the few degrees you point at, occupies the lion's share of the radiations and, as we will see, of the cortex itself. The fan is where the retina's priority ruling is honoured in cable: most of the wire is centre. So the transformation here is subtle: no new editing, but the map's proportions are rehearsed one more time. On this route information is not spread evenly; it is budgeted. The centre of your vision is expensive, and you pay for it in fibre.

Host: And then the signal arrives at the visual cortex, at the very back of the head, the moment listeners have waited for since last season: the report is delivered. But I suspect delivery is not the end of the work. What does arrival look like when the spike trains reach the cortex?

Guide: Arrival looks like a map being born. The primary visual cortex is a folded sheet at the back of the brain, and its first achievement is to rebuild the scene as a map across its surface: each small patch of cortex answers to a small patch of the world, with the centre hugely enlarged, as we said. Classic recordings, made with fine electrodes in this sheet while animals watched simple patterns, found something unexpected. The cells are quiet for plain, even light; flood the map with a diffuse glow and almost nothing responds. But show a bar, an edge, a line at a particular angle, and specific cells fire hard, only for their angle. Shift the bar, and the firing shifts to neighbouring cells. The cortex, in other words, is not interested in light as such; it is interested in structure: where illumination changes, which way it runs, how sharp it is. That is the deepest transformation on the route: the report stops being about places and starts being about things, edges first of all. And it keeps going: some cells answer to their edge only at one exact spot, others anywhere in their patch, some fire for motion in one direction and fall silent for the opposite. Step by step, the cortex assembles descriptions: not this much light here, but a boundary here, running this way, moving that way.

Host: Edges, and you slipped motion in as well. Two threads remain before we close the route. What happens to colour, and what happens to the two eyes, whose streams we have kept carefully separate since last season? Does the mixing finally begin here?

Guide: Both threads are live, and both are fenced, because their full stories belong later in the series. On colour: the red-against-green and blue-against-yellow comparisons built in the retina travel in their own streams through every station we have named, and beyond the cortex they become the beginnings of colour experience; that construction belongs to Season 8. On the two eyes: the streams stay separate through nerve, tract and geniculate. In the cortex the two maps finally lie close enough that single cells begin to receive from both eyes at once, and the point-by-point comparison that depth is made of begins here; how two reports become one scene with depth is another season's story. What I will claim today is the shape of the thing. By the time the signal has passed through nerve, chiasm, geniculate, radiations and cortex, the million difference-reports that left the eye have been re-sorted by world-half, filed in registered copies, gated by attention and arousal, rebuilt as an enlarged map, and read for structure: edges, angles, motion, boundaries. Nothing has been copied. Everything has been transformed. And the season walks these transformations one at a time: the retinal circuits that build the differences, the centre-surround logic behind them, resolution in space, resolution in time, contrast, motion, edges, and the assembly of it all in the finale.

Host: There is the theme, and it is the season's spine, so let me press on it, because it is strange. At no station does anyone hold a picture: the retina ships differences, the chiasm re-sorts, the geniculate gates, the radiations carry, the cortex shreds into edges and motion. So where is the picture? If nobody downstream of the eye ever holds a photograph, what exactly am I looking at right now?

Guide: You are looking at a construction, and the honest answer is that no station holds a finished picture waiting for an inner viewer. That expectation, a small screen somewhere showing the scene to a small self, is the oldest trap in thinking about vision, and today's route is the evidence against it. What exists is a chain of transformations, each station handing the next a further-edited report, and seeing is what it is like to be the system running that chain. The picture is not shown to the brain; the picture is what the brain does. Hold the two ends side by side. At the front, a retina with a hundred million sensors and a million cables, already reporting differences, already parallel, already edited. At the back, a cortex painting the scene as a map and shredding it into edges, angles and motion. Between them, stations that re-sort, register, gate and carry. Nowhere a copy; everywhere a transformation. And that is why this season exists. Series 2 drew the road; Series 1 walked it as a story. This season asks the physiology question at every stop: what changes here, and why must it?

Host: Let us bring this to the chair, the way we always do, and stay strictly inside normal function. An optometrist examines eyes all day and rarely thinks about the back of the head. Name the ordinary pieces of practice that quietly depend on the pathway we walked today.

Guide: Four, and every one of them normal function. First, acuity. When you measure a letter chart you measure the end product of the whole route: the fovea's private lines, the centre-heavy budget of the radiations, the enlarged cortical map. Letters are read by the middle of the map because the map was budgeted that way long before the cortex. Second, the blind spot. Every person carries a hole in each retina and nobody sees one, because the cortex completes the scene; demonstrate it to a student and watch it dissolve into background, and you are watching a transformation at work. Third, the two-eye handshake. In every normally sighted person the two reports arrive at the cortex in register, thanks to the sorting at the chiasm and the stacking at the geniculate; when you assess how the eyes work together, you are probing the success of that arrangement. Fourth, attention, the one patients feel. The geniculate gate means vision is not a fixed pipeline: the same scene passes differently when a person is alert, drowsy or searching. Anyone who has missed a name written in front of them while thinking of something else has felt the gateway narrow. None of this is exotic; it is an ordinary morning of testing, and every part of it was built at a station we named today.

Host: Time for the misconception sweep. Four statements a listener might walk in with: the eye sends a picture to the brain; the lateral geniculate body is a simple relay; the brain merges the two eyes' images like stacking two photographs; seeing is finished at the primary visual cortex. Take them one at a time: the correction, and the station where the truth lives.

Guide: Statement one: the eye sends a picture. Correction: it sends about a million edited, parallel difference-reports; no picture is ever sent because none is ever held. The truth lives at every station, starting in last season's circuits. Statement two: the lateral geniculate body is a simple relay. Correction: it is a regulating gateway, wired roughly ten to one from above, setting the gain of what passes according to the brain's state. The truth lives at station three. Statement three: the brain stacks two photographs. Correction: the two eyes' reports are never merged as wholes. The chiasm re-sorts them by world-half, the geniculate files them on registered floors, and only in the cortex do single cells begin to listen to both eyes at once, point by matching point. The truth lives at stations two, three and five. Statement four: seeing is finished at the primary visual cortex. Correction: that cortex is the first staging ground, not the last; the deeper work, objects, faces, places, the scene as meaning, continues forward into the rest of the cortex, well beyond today's route. Four corrections, one moral: at no point does anyone merely pass a picture along.

Host: Then let us do the oral-examination version, the way we close every episode. Say the route back, station by station, and give me one transformation sentence per stop, crisply, the way a student should say it aloud.

Guide: Station one, the optic nerve: about one point two million ganglion axons leave as one cable per eye, and the transformation is the code, graded voltages re-encoded as regenerating spikes on a brain tract, so the report travels without fading. Station two, the optic chiasm: the nose-facing half of each retina crosses, and the transformation is the address system, streams re-sorted from eye by eye to world half by world half, each tract carrying one half of the scene as seen by both eyes. Station three, the lateral geniculate body: six layers, four for fine detail, two for motion and timing, interlaminar sheets for blue against yellow, one eye per layer, stacked in register; the transformation is control, gain set by attention and arousal and the streams kept separate for separate fates. Station four, the optic radiations: the fan sweeping back through each hemisphere; the transformation is fidelity and budget, the map carried unscrambled with the centre over-represented, exactly as the retina ruled at the fovea. Station five, the visual cortex: the sheet at the back of the head; the transformation is construction, the scene rebuilt as an enlarged map and read for structure, edges, angles and motion, with the two eyes' maps finally neighbours and the feature assembly begun. Five stations, five transformations, and not one of them a copy.

Host: One last question before the tease, our usual corner: across the whole route, which single detail do students most underestimate?

Guide: The pairing. Every station we named today exists twice. Two nerves leave two eyes; one chiasm mixes them; two tracts leave it; two lateral geniculate bodies; two fans; two cortical maps, one in each hemisphere. Students picture a single road down which the two eyes' traffic merges early; it never merges as a whole. The brain runs the entire route twice, in parallel, once for each half of the world, and it keeps the two half-reports in a correspondence so tight that every station can compare its copy with its twin. And the duplication is not wasteful: it is the raw material of depth and stability, a single solid scene built from two slightly displaced viewpoints, with the comparison arranged by geometry all along the route. The underestimate in one sentence: the visual pathway is not a wire from camera to screen; it is a doubled, symmetric machine whose two halves are built to be compared. Keep that in mind all season, because half the transformations ahead are comparisons too, centre against surround, detail against context. The route's habit is to report differences, and it begins by comparing its own two copies of the world.
