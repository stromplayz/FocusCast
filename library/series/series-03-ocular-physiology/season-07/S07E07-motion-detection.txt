# Description: Motion is not simply seen; it is computed, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks opens the machinery that catches movement inside the retina. Meet the direction-selective ganglion cells, wired with help from a star-shaped amacrine interneuron to fire hard for an edge sweeping one way and stay silent for the opposite sweep. Learn the two great uses of that machinery: reading optic flow, the outward stream of the whole scene that reports your own speed and heading, and reading events, as the rod-rich, parasol-rich periphery acts as a tripwire that summons the fovea to anything that moves. Understand why motion is the retina's oldest news service and the fovea's spotter. Made for optometry students, practising optometrists and curious listeners who want movement perception explained for the ear.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology. We are seven episodes into Season 7, Visual Physiology, and the season has been following the visual signal outward, station by station. Episode one walked the message from photoreceptor to brain. Episode two mapped the retinal neural circuits that carry it. Episode three taught the centre-surround argument, episode four the limits of spatial resolution, episode five the timing of responses in Temporal Resolution, and episode six taught contrast, ending with a promise: from there the season would turn from space toward time, and the world would start to move. Tonight it does. This is episode seven, Motion Detection. Here is the hook. A frog will starve in a field of dead flies, because its retina reports almost nothing that does not move, and the moment something flies past, its tongue is already out. Your eye is subtler than the frog's, but it obeys the same law, and tonight we follow that law into the wiring. Motion, you will learn, is not simply seen. It is computed. Before we open the machinery, our guide sets the frame. What does it mean, exactly, to say that motion is computed rather than seen?

Guide: Let me start with the smallest picture I can. At any single instant, the photoreceptor mosaic is a still photograph: every receptor reports one quantity, how much light is landing at its address, and nothing in that report says anything about movement. A receptor cannot know that the light it receives used to sit somewhere else, and it cannot know that something else is on its way. Movement exists only when you compare different places at different times. Someone has to hold the earlier sample against the later one and ask what changed where, and that holding-and-comparing is a computation. The proof that your visual system performs it is sitting in every cinema you have ever entered. The screen shows two dozen still frames every second; nothing on that screen moves, not ever; the motion is manufactured entirely inside the viewer, and the manufacturing begins in the retina, before the nerve. That is the frame for tonight, and it is an old frame, older than colour, older than fine detail, because in the history of eyes the first urgent question was never what a thing looked like but whether it was approaching. The retina is not a camera cable. It is a news service for change, and motion is its oldest bulletin. In Season 6 we learned that the retina edits before it ships. Tonight we watch one of its oldest edits being made.

Host: Then show me the machinery. Which cells do the computing, and how did anyone manage to catch them at it?

Guide: The story begins with a classic set of experiments from the middle of the last century, in which researchers recorded from single fibres of the optic nerve in the rabbit while patterns drifted across a small screen. Most fibres answered light the way you would expect: brighter here, dimmer there, an onset burst for a flash. But some answered only movement. A steady pattern, however bright, however sharp its edges, left them cold, and the moment it slid, they spoke. And within that group sat the discovery of the night: fibres that answered movement in one direction only. Drag an edge across the cell's field from one side to the other and the fibre fired a storm of impulses. Drag the same edge back along the same path, same speed, same contrast, and the fibre fell almost silent. Not a weaker report. A different verdict. Physiologists named the two sweeps the preferred direction and the null direction, and the vocabulary matters, because it tells you what the cell is. It is not a light meter that happens to be jostled. It is a small judge, wired in advance with an opinion about which way the world moved, and its opinion is delivered as firing or as silence.

Host: Same edge, same speed, opposite verdicts. That sounds like wiring, not sensing. Build the filter for me. How does a cell come to hold an opinion about direction?

Guide: Start with what every ganglion cell owns, its receptive field, the small territory of the visual scene within which light changes its firing, which we built carefully in episode nine of season six, Retinal Ganglion Cells. A direction-selective cell receives its inputs across that territory through two kinds of route. One route is fast and direct: excitation arriving from the relay cells with very little delay. The other route is a detour: a braking, inhibitory signal that travels by way of a star-shaped amacrine interneuron, a cell with a crown of branches radiating outward like a starfish, each branch acting as its own side-road with its own small delay. Now run the movie twice. An edge sweeping in the preferred direction reaches the fast, direct arm first, and the cell begins to fire; the brake is still out on its detour, and by the time it lands, the moment has passed. The verdict: fire hard. Sweep the same edge the opposite way and the order flips. The brake arrives first, slams the gate shut, and the excitation that follows finds the door already closed. Near silence. A delay placed on one side of the field turns a movement detector into a direction detector, and notice the economics of it: the brake costs the retina almost nothing to run, yet it buys a categorical answer. The amacrine family, remember, is the lateral mixing tier we met when we studied retinal circuits; the star-shaped members are the specialists this circuit cannot do without.

Host: One cell, one direction, though, would leave the world three quarters unheard. Where do the other directions come from?

Guide: From families. The retina does not rely on a single judge; it deploys sets of direction-selective ganglion cells tuned to the four compass headings, one group answering an upward sweep, one downward, one leftward, one rightward, with subgroups that specialise further: some answering a bright edge, some a dark one, some both. Tile those panels across the whole field of view and every position of the scene carries its own committee: at this spot, a watcher for each heading, each silent for its null and vocal for its preference. Now you can see how the direction of a movement is actually reported. It is not written in any single fibre. It is written across the panel, in the pattern of who is firing and who is quiet, like a chorus whose message is which members sing. And something of speed comes free from the same logic, because the delay arms overlap differently for a crawl and for a dart, so the shape of the response carries something of the pace as well as the heading.

Host: A question presses here, and I want it answered before we leave the circuit. The whole scene streams across your retina every time you move, as we will discuss shortly. When everything moves together, does this panel of watchers simply cry wolf everywhere at once?

Guide: An honest question, and the answer reveals a second layer of order. When one patch moves against a still surround, a few watchers fire and the rest hold their peace: a local solo, easy to hear as an event. When you walk, the entire field streams and every panel fires in concert, and the concert itself is the message: global motion of that orderly, outward kind is the signature of your own travel through the world. So the same machinery reports two different stories, a lone alarm and a general chorus, and the retina ships both honestly, without deciding for you which is which. Pulling global chorus and local solo apart, the self-movement from the world's movement, is downstream work, and the retinal job is to deliver the pattern cleanly. Keep that in view, because it saves tonight's machinery from a trap: the panel is never fooled into thinking the world is full of events just because you went for a stroll. It reports the stroll, in flow, as flow.

Host: Now I want to pin all of this to a name our listeners met recently. Where do the parasol cells fit into tonight's story, and what had that episode already told us about them?

Guide: Episode nine of season six, Retinal Ganglion Cells, gave us the family portrait. The parasol cells are about one tenth of the ganglion population, named for dendrites that spread wide and flat like an umbrella, gathering from big pooled fields, answering in transient bursts at onset and offset rather than holding a sustained note, and carrying their cargo down the thickest, fastest axons in the nerve. Their cargo, we said, is motion, flicker and timing, the when of the visual world; their country, we said, is the periphery. Tonight you can see why that family was always going to be the motion family. A transient, change-hungry temperament is exactly the raw material a direction filter needs: a cell that only speaks when something changes is a cell worth wiring for opinions about how it changed. In subsets of the large-bodied cells and their relatives, the retinal circuits add the delayed brake and turn change detection into direction detection. And here is the sentence I most want you to keep. The photoreceptors ship evidence: how much light, at which addresses. A direction-selective ganglion cell ships an opinion: a verdict about which way something moved. That is the difference between a camera and a correspondent, and the retina employs correspondents. The brain elaborates these verdicts further downstream, in stories for later conversations; tonight belongs to the retina's own contribution, and it is not a small one, because the verdicts are already stamped into the nerve.

Host: Good. You promised us two great employments for this machinery, and I want them in order. Take me outdoors first. I am walking down a street toward a door. What does my retina report about my own walking?

Guide: It reports a river. As you move forward, the entire visual scene streams across your retina in a pattern so orderly that it has earned its own name: optic flow. Here is the pattern in plain words. Everything ahead of you slides outward from one point that stays put, the point you are heading toward. The doorknob swells and slides toward the edge of view; the lamppost beside it sweeps outward faster; the tower in the distance creeps; the pavement below pours outward at your feet. Near things stream fast, far things stream slowly, and at the very centre of the pattern sits one stationary point, which students of vision call the focus of expansion. Now read the field like an instrument, because that is what it is. The still point tells you your heading. The rate of outflow tells you your speed. The tilt and wobble of the whole pattern tell you how your body is swaying as it walks. The world streams because you move, and the motion field, read correctly, is a report about yourself. Your own travel is written across the scene, in flow, and the machinery we built in the first half of tonight is the instrument that reads it.

Host: So the still point walks ahead of me like a destination. If I steer toward that door, what happens to the pattern?

Guide: Turn your walk and the still point swings to your new heading; walk a truer line and you keep it parked on the target. Drift off course and the point slides off the door, so veering is visible in the flow before you have consciously noticed it. Distance is in the field as well: anything you are closing on expands in your view, and the rate of that expansion tells you, without one deliberate calculation on your part, how soon you will arrive. That is the signal a fielder uses under a falling ball, and the same signal a walker uses at the edge of a stair, adjusting the foot before the mind has finished the arithmetic. Even posture leans on the field: the small jiggles of an upright body jiggle the flow, and the visual system reads those tremors continuously to help hold you steady, which is why balancing is measurably harder in a room where the walls themselves appear to move. None of this is decoration. The flow field is a measuring instrument for self-motion, and it is being read in the background every time you stand up and go somewhere.

Host: Now the awkward question, and I have been saving it. My eyes are never still; they swing across the scene all day long. Does their own movement not scramble this beautiful flow field?

Guide: It adds to it, and the honest answer is that separating your own glance from the world's own movement is a genuine computation, one the visual system performs constantly, because otherwise every flick of the eyes would read as the world lurching. But the machinery that tags the eye's own travel, compensates for it, and keeps the flow field readable through every glance is motor machinery, the oculomotor control systems, and that ground is owned by Season 9, whose episode nine, Oculomotor Control, and episode ten, Coordinated Eye Movements, will walk it properly. Tonight I will only signpost: the retina supplies clean direction verdicts and honest timing, and the disentangling happens downstream. Keep our lane and our fence in view. This season owns how movement is caught; Season 9 owns how the eyes are moved.

Host: Then to your second employment, and my favourite. Take me to the brush, and show me why movement breaks camouflage.

Guide: Picture a rabbit frozen in the brush. Its coat matches the twigs around it in brightness and in texture; the boundary of its body is a rumour, not a fact; and to a retina reporting light at addresses, the patch where the rabbit sits is nearly indistinguishable from the patch beside it. The cues that let you name an object, its edges, its shading, its colour, are all cancelled. Now let the rabbit hop. In that instant, one patch of the scene moves while its surround stands still, and no amount of colour-matching can hide relative movement. Motion is a channel of its own, separate from shape and shade, and it ignores appearance: pattern can lie, texture can lie, but a moving patch cannot stay silent while it moves. This is why freezing works as a strategy across the animal kingdom, and why it fails at the first twitch. And this is why your own periphery is built the way it is. The far field is rod-rich, as the distribution map in Series 2, episode four of season eight, Photoreceptor Distribution, showed, and its convergence is the deepest in the retina, hundreds of receptors funnelled onto single relay lines, the pooling arithmetic taught in episode three of season six, Rod Physiology. Series 1, episode nine of season two, Peripheral Vision, called that region blurry but brilliant, rich in motion and poor in detail. Add its wealth of parasol cells and you have the perfect tripwire: poor at naming what moved, superb at knowing that something did.

Host: And the moment something moves out there, my eyes are pulled toward it before I have decided anything. Describe that summoning.

Guide: A movement verdict from the wide field arrives with priority, because it is exactly the kind of report the system was built to interrupt itself for. Attention snaps outward. Within a fraction of a second the eyes swing so that whatever moved lands on the fovea, the only ground in the retina where detail is resolved, and only then does identification begin: friend, leaf, or nothing. Notice the division of labour, because it is the design of human vision in one sentence. The fovea owns detail; the periphery owns surveillance; and motion is the hand-off between them, the signal that says leave the word you are reading and look here. The retina, in this exchange, is the spotter: it flags the event and hands over a direction. The steering of the eyes themselves is motor machinery with its own season, and we have already fenced it to Season 9. Hold the frame instead: motion is the fovea's spotter, the sentinel that decides where the high-resolution instrument gets pointed next.

Host: Before we leave the periphery, give me the demonstration anyone can run tonight. Dim the lamp almost out, and what happens to this tripwire?

Guide: You will catch motion when you cannot catch anything else, and it follows directly from the wiring. In near-darkness the pattern channel starves: the rod world, as we learned three episodes into Season 6, is built for sensitivity, not for resolving still detail, and a stationary hand in a dim room can sit below the threshold at which its form can be told from the background at all. But sweep that same hand slowly through the scene and fresh receptors are crossed one after another, whispers sum where the hand passes, the change channels light up, and there it is: a shape you cannot see until it moves. Motion is the last channel standing in the dusk. It is the same reason a star you cannot fix by looking straight at will show itself in the corner of your eye, the old trick of averted vision, and the same reason a faint thing at night seems to shimmer and vanish while it holds still. The tripwire never sleeps, because the tripwire was never asked to resolve; it was asked to notice.

Host: Let us bring all of this to the chair, the way we always do, in strictly ordinary function. Where does motion physiology show itself on an unremarkable working day?

Guide: In three places you already visit. First, the limits of the acuity chart. The smallest letters a patient resolves are a foveal, midget-stream achievement, and the chart says nothing at all about the parasol tripwire; a person can read the line beautifully and still have a cupped hand waved at the edge of view seize their attention from across the room. Those are two different performances from two different populations, and tonight names the second one. Second, the wiggle-finger habits of everyday examination. When a finger wiggles at the edge of vision and the patient's eyes flick toward it, you are interrogating the tripwire, compass panel by compass panel, exactly as we built it tonight; movement is the question, and the flick of gaze is the answer. Third, ordinary life away from the chair. The cyclist who enters the edge of your view and claims it before you ever turn, the cat that ignores a still toy and detonates at a dragged one, the flicker of a screen in the corner of the room, whose timing side belongs to episode five of this season, Temporal Resolution: all parasol reports, all retina before brain. Motion physiology is not an exotic corner of the syllabus. It is running the whole day in the background, and it has been since the first hour of your life.

Host: Before the recap, the usual sweep. Give me the commonest misconceptions about motion vision, and correct them.

Guide: The first is the video recorder. Students picture the eye taping the scene, motion included, the way a camera does. But the mosaic only ever delivers still photographs; the movement is manufactured by comparison, and the comparison begins in retinal circuitry, with star-shaped brakes and compass panels. The recording analogy dies at the photoreceptor. The second is the fully central motion detector: the belief that movement is only worked out in the brain, after the image has left the eye. Direction verdicts are already stamped into the optic nerve. The fibres that carry them were wired for their preferences before the brain added anything at all.

Host: Two more, quickly.

Guide: The third is the empire of the fovea: the assumption that the centre is best at everything. It is best at detail, and it loses the motion contest to the periphery, where pooling buys sensitivity and the parasols run the watch; each region leads at its own question, and vision needs both. The fourth is the smear: the idea that optic flow is visual noise, an unfortunate blur to be ignored while you get on with looking at things. The flow field is among the most informative signals the eye ever ships: speed, heading and time of arrival, all in one continuous report. And one small teaser under the same roof. Stare at a waterfall for half a minute, look away to the cliff, and the rock briefly crawls upward. That aftereffect is normal adaptation in motion channels, proof of how seriously the system commits to movement, and a story for a later conversation on perception.

Host: Here is a thought experiment to press the frame harder before we recap. Suppose the world truly stopped moving, and suppose your eyes stopped with it. What would the news service report?

Guide: Almost nothing, and that is the deepest lesson of the night. If an image could be held perfectly still on the retina, frozen to the receptors, the scene would not merely become boring; it would fade, because the channels that carry vision are change channels, and a report with no changes in it approaches silence. The reason the world does not fade while you read this is that your eyes never actually hold still: they drift, they dart, they tremble minutely, keeping the image wandering across the mosaic so the comparison machinery always has fresh material. That rescue squad is eye-movement machinery, and its own story is told where the steering is told, in that later season. But the moral belongs to tonight: motion is not an accessory bolted onto seeing. Seeing is built on change detection from the ground up, and a moving world is not a distraction from vision. It is the condition vision was built for.

Host: Take me through tonight's list now, slowly, the way you would want a student to say it back in an oral examination.

Guide: With pleasure, and this list is worth memorising outright. The mosaic is still photographs: motion is computed by comparing places across times, and the comparison begins in the retina. The direction-selective ganglion cell: fires hard for an edge sweeping its preferred direction and falls nearly silent for the null direction, same edge, same speed. The wiring: fast direct excitation plus a delayed brake arriving through the branches of a star-shaped amacrine interneuron; the detour is the trick, and the brake is half the machine. The families: compass-tuned panels for upward, downward, leftward and rightward sweep, with bright-edge and dark-edge specialists, so that direction is reported as a pattern across the panel. The parasol connection: one tenth of the ganglion population, big transient fields, the fastest axons, motion and timing as cargo, per episode nine of season six. Global and local: a lone watcher firing is an event, every panel firing in concert is your own travel. Optic flow: the whole scene streaming outward from the still point you are heading toward, near things fast and far things slow, your speed in the rate of outflow, your heading in the position of the still point. Event reading: movement breaks camouflage, because a moving patch betrays itself against a still surround. The periphery: rod-rich, deeply convergent, parasol-rich, blurry but brilliant, the tripwire that summons the fovea to anything that moves. The division of labour: fovea owns detail, periphery owns surveillance, motion is the hand-off, and the steering of the eyes belongs to Season 9. And the frame: motion is the retina's oldest news service, and the fovea's spotter.

Host: One last question before the tease. Across all of this, which detail do students underestimate most?

Guide: The silence. Students remember that direction-selective cells fire hard for the preferred sweep, and they gloss the other half of the finding: the near-silence for the null. That refusal is the achievement. We learned this grammar back in the rod and its dark current, where light works by subtraction and a signal can be the shape of a pause; the direction filter speaks the same language fluently. A cell does not merely shout leftward. It proves the point by refusing rightward, and a filter is defined by what it refuses just as much as by what it passes. If the brake ever failed, the cell would answer both directions equally and opinion would collapse back into evidence, which is a fancy way of saying the retina would stop being an analyst and become a camera again. The underestimate beside it: how much of your daily competence, walking a crowded street without colliding, catching a set of keys tossed across a room, is read from flow you never once notice. The most important instrument in the room is the one you cannot hear.
