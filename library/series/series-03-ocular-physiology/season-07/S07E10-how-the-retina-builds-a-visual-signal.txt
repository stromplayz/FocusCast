# Description: This season finale of Focus Cast by focuslinks follows a single glance on its complete journey, from the moment photons enter the eye to the moment the brain recognises a friend's face across the room. Trace the whole pipeline in one continuous story: the photoreceptor mosaic catching light, the transduction cascade turning that catch into a quieting, bipolar and horizontal circuits comparing and classifying, amacrine circuits timing and gating, ganglion cells compressing the scene into parallel difference-coded streams, and the gateway and cortex rebuilding position, orientation, motion and identity. The episode distils the season's three great themes, that vision is differences, vision is parallel, and vision is constructed, then recaps all nine earlier episodes by title and honours the Series 1 episode where this journey was first sketched. Along the way it teases the next season, Colour and Contrast Vision, and its opener, Physiology of Colour Vision. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want the physiology behind every glance, explained for the ear.

Host: Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to a finale. This is Series 3, Ocular Physiology, season seven, Visual Physiology, and this is episode ten, How the Retina Builds a Visual Signal. Nine episodes ago this season asked a deceptively small question: what happens, physically, in the instant you look at something? We answered it station by station, one episode per station, until the whole pipeline lay mapped. Tonight we do what only a finale can do: we follow one single glance through every station at once, end to end, without stopping, and watch the signal being built. And I mean one glance. Not a day of vision, not a lifetime of looking, just one moment of the kind you have spent hundreds of today without noticing. Guide, set the stage for us. What glance are we following, and what does it mean to say that a visual signal is built?

Guide: Let us give the glance a hero, because synthesis needs something to hold on to. You are across a room, and you glance at a friend's face. The glance lasts about a fifth of a second, and that is the honest unit of vision: nobody ever sees a whole day at once, we see in snapshots, and each snapshot is this journey. In that fifth of a second, an unbroken chain of events runs without a single pause. Photons leave her face, cross the room, enter your eye and are focused into a tiny inverted image. They burrow through the retina's transparent circuitry to a mosaic of light-catching cells. The catch becomes chemistry, the chemistry becomes a change in voltage, the voltage is compared with neighbours, timed against the recent past, classified and compressed. About a million edited reports leave each eye in parallel, are sorted at a gateway, and then, in the cortex at the back of your brain, are rebuilt into position, form, motion and finally recognition: that is her face, and that is her smile. Here is the claim of the whole season, stated once more, plainly. A visual signal is not received, it is built. Nothing in that chain is a photograph. Every station is a workshop, and none of the workers ever announces itself, which is why the machinery hides in plain sight and why a whole season was needed to expose it. Tonight we walk the factory floor from the loading dock to the finished product.

Host: Then start at the front door, before any receptor is involved. The light is on its way in. Who shapes it, and where does it actually come to rest?

Guide: The optics answer first, and they answer in less time than it takes to describe them. The cornea does most of the bending, because light slows sharply when it crosses from air into tissue, and the lens behind the pupil fine-tunes the focus, thickening for a page in your hands and relaxing for her face across the room; we learned both trades in season three and season five of this series. The payoff is a small, sharp, upside-down image laid down across the curved sheet at the back of the eye. Now the photons face the oddity of the retina's inverted design: to reach their sensors they must tunnel through the very circuitry that will later edit their report, past the ganglion cells and the bipolar cells and their sideways neighbours, until they arrive at the floor that actually catches them, the photoreceptor mosaic. The trip is short, the retina is thinner than a millimetre, and the crossing is clean because the retinal neurons are transparent and arranged like a stack of glass shelves. The mosaic itself is the season's founding census: roughly one hundred and twenty million rods for dim and moving worlds, six million cones for daylight detail, with the cones crowded shoulder to shoulder in the fovea at the visual centre and the rods vacating that same spot entirely. Series 2 gave us this ground as geography, and the first episodes of this season gave it back as physiology. And the glance delivers a flood to that census: an ordinary lit room pours trillions of photons into each eye every second, so the catch we are about to describe is not one event but a storm of them, falling across the mosaic in the exact pattern of her face, bright here and shadowed there. The photons stop travelling here. From this moment onward, everything is construction.

Host: Stop at the catch, then. One photon lands on one molecule of photopigment in one outer segment. What happens at the very bottom of the entire system?

Guide: The catch is the only quantum event in the whole journey, and it is over almost before it begins: the photon is absorbed, and the pigment's bent chromophore snaps straight in about a fifth of a trillionth of a second. That snap arms the opsin, and the armed opsin becomes an enzyme-switch, waking hundreds of transducin molecules, each of which wakes a demolishing enzyme that snips the messenger molecules holding open the channels in the outer segment membrane. The channels close, the steady inflow of sodium and calcium sags, and the photoreceptor's voltage slides down from about minus forty millivolts toward minus sixty-five. The arithmetic of amplification is the wonder we marvelled at in season six, episode five, Phototransduction, where we walked this relay leg by leg, so tonight we pocket the result and move: one caught photon, multiplied through the cascade into a change the cell can read, is enough to be heard by a single rod, and a handful of caught photons is enough for you to know it. But notice what the catch actually produced. Not a spark, not a flash, not a little electric current switched on. A change in a current that was already flowing. The photon has been converted into voltage, the first translation is done, and the language it produced is quietness.

Host: There it is, the strangest thing this season taught. Light does not switch a photoreceptor on; it switches it off. Unpack that for us, because the whole downstream system hangs on it.

Guide: A stop-signal can be read with far more precision than a start-signal, and a stop only means anything against a running baseline. In darkness the photoreceptor never rests: the dark current flows, glutamate dribbles steadily out of the terminal, and the cells below receive a continuous report that says nothing is happening. Light makes that report thinner, and the thinning is the message. This buys two profound advantages. First, the system can report in both directions: a cell running at rest can be quieted below its baseline by light and can be lifted back above it when the light goes, so brighter-than-before and darker-than-before are both available as signals, from the very first cell. Second, the machinery is always primed: nothing has to be switched on from cold, so the retina answers in milliseconds, fast enough to serve a glance. A camera sensor that simply counts photons arriving could do neither trick. Remember this shape, a baseline and a deviation from it, because you will meet it again at every single station tonight, all the way to the cortex. It is the retina's oldest trick, and it is the seed of the first great theme of the season.

Host: The signal now leaves the receptor and drops one floor, to the first circuit layer. Introduce the bipolar cells and their reading of this quieting.

Guide: The bipolar cell is the first interpreter, and its question is beautifully simple: brighter than before, or darker than before? Two families read the same event in mirror image. The OFF bipolar keeps the photoreceptor's sign: glutamate in the dark excited it through a fast chemical gate, so when light thins the trickle, it too goes quiet, and it reports a darkening, an OFF response. The ON bipolar inverts the sign through a slower chemical receptor: glutamate held it down in the dark, so the light-liberated silence releases it, and it reports a brightening, an ON response. Some bipolar cells talk to the ganglion cells directly, and some route sideways through the neighbouring circuits first, so the retina even separates fast news from considered news at this floor. Every point of the visual field is covered by both families, a matched pair, so the retina carries brightening and darkening as separate parallel opinions rather than as one ambiguous wobble. And notice the classification that has already happened: a raw voltage change has become a judgement about change, brighter or darker, relative always to what came before. That is a first step from physics toward meaning, and it happened just one synapse away from the receptor.

Host: And beside that vertical path sits a sideways one. What do the horizontal cells add to the report?

Guide: Comparison across space. Horizontal cells pool from many neighbours sideways and feed that pooled influence back to the photoreceptor terminals and bipolar dendrites, so a bipolar cell ends up owning two antagonistic territories: a centre it reads directly from its own receptors, and a ring of surround it reads through the horizontal cells. Now the arithmetic of the whole retina snaps into focus. Flood the centre and the surround with the same uniform light, and the two influences cancel; the cell barely stirs. Light the centre brighter than its ring, or darken it below its ring, and the cell answers strongly. Her face against the shaded wall survives; the wall by itself does not. The retina, at only its second synapse, has become a difference engine: it deletes what is everywhere the same and keeps what changes from place to place. We learned the wiring in season six, episode eight, Retinal Signal Processing, and felt the perceptual consequences in episode three of this season, Centre-Surround Processing. Tonight, notice only that the theme is repeating on schedule: differences, not absolutes, first in time at the receptor, now in space at the circuit.

Host: Next floor. The retina also has to keep up with time, because a glance sweeps and the world moves. Who owns the timing?

Guide: The amacrine cells, the sideways talkers of the inner synaptic layer, own time. Where bipolar responses are sustained, many amacrine circuits are transient: they respond when something changes and fall silent when it stays, which is a large part of why the world does not fade when you hold your eyes still, and why change itself is the news. Amacrine circuits emphasise onsets and offsets, sharpen responses in time, provide gain control so the same circuits serve a dim room and bright noon, and run specialised loops that fire for motion in a particular direction, so that a shape drifting left excites different cables than the same shape drifting right. Their gates also decide what reaches the output floor at all, because a report that answers to everything carries nothing worth hearing. One specialised amacrine even carries the rod pathway's traffic across into cone-line cables in dim light, the crossover that lets one set of output cells serve two sensor systems. Episode seven of this season, Motion Detection, gave those loops their full hour. For tonight's single glance, hold one idea: by the time a signal reaches the output floor, it has been stamped not only with where and how much compared with its neighbours, but with when and how fast compared with the moment before. Time, too, has been folded into the report as a difference.

Host: Now the output floor, where the whole construction has to be packed for shipping. This is the compression this season kept promising. Take us through it.

Guide: The ganglion cells are the only retinal neurons whose axons leave the eye, and they receive a convergence the census makes stark: more than a hundred and twenty million photoreceptors feed roughly one point two million ganglion cells, about one hundred receptors for every output fibre on average. But the average hides the design. In the far periphery, hundreds of rods pool into one output cell, buying sensitivity at the cost of address. In the fovea the ratio inverts into luxury: roughly one cone, to one midget bipolar, to one midget ganglion cell, a private line preserving position to a fraction of a degree, and that private line is the hardware behind every letter you have ever read on a chart. The compression also explains a trade you already know from the inside: pooling buys sensitivity and spends address, which is why the periphery is blurred, why stars vanish when you look straight at them, and why the fovea sees what it sees. Every ganglion cell computes with the same centre-versus-ring field we met a moment ago: uniform light cancels, differences survive. And its report travels as a rate: a resting chatter that quickens or slows depending on whether its centre is brighter or darker than its ring, faster and slower again with motion and timing. Then look at the fleet, because the output is not one stream but several, already separated before a single fibre leaves the eye. The midget family, about four fifths of all output, carries fine detail and red-green comparisons through those private lines. The parasol family, about a tenth, pools widely and carries motion, flicker and timing on thick fast axons. The small bistratified family carries the blue-yellow comparison. One glance, many edited channels. That is the second great theme arriving exactly on schedule: vision is parallel.

Host: So the edited, parallel reports leave the eye as the optic nerve. Say the departure properly, because it is also the season's costliest sentence.

Guide: About one point two million axons gather at the optic disc and leave as the optic nerve, and because that doorway holds no photoreceptors at all, each eye carries a permanent hole in its map, the blind spot, which is both the price of the packaging and the daily proof that the report was edited before it shipped. Pause on what each fibre actually carries: not a pixel, not a brightness reading, but an edited difference report, this spot against its ring, this moment against the last, classified by stream. Those axons are genuine brain tract, the eye's own piece of the central nervous system already on its way home, and their thickness is budgeted by stream, with the timing stream given the fattest, fastest fibres so that motion news arrives while it still matters. The anatomy of everything that follows, the partial crossing at the chiasm where fibres from the two nasal halves swap over so that each half of the brain reads the opposite half of the world, and the stations from there to the cortex, was mapped in Series 2, episode ten of season ten, Complete Visual Pathway - Retina to Cortex. Tonight we ask a different question about the same road: what is the freight? And the answer is the season in miniature: a million cables, each carrying comparisons rather than quantities, already sorted into parallel streams, on their way to be rebuilt.

Host: Between nerve and cortex stands the gateway. What actually happens there, beyond forwarding the mail?

Guide: The lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus does two quiet jobs. It sorts: six layers, each receiving one eye only, with the midget reports and the parasol reports kept in their own layers and the blue-yellow reports in the thin layers between, and with all the maps held in precise register so the two eyes' pictures sit correctly on top of one another. And it regulates, because the gateway is gated. Retinal fibres drive only a minority of its synapses; the rest come from the cortex looking back down, and from brainstem systems carrying arousal and attention, which is why the same glance lands differently when you are alert, drowsy or searching for someone. That proportion tells you the scale of the downward conversation: the gateway weighs the mail far more than it moves it. The briefing is not simply forwarded; it is counter-signed and weighted. This is the first place the brain begins talking back to the signal on its way up, and it will never stop talking back from here on.

Host: Then the final act: the cortex. Rebuild the glance for us.

Guide: The primary visual cortex receives the reports as maps, position preserved point for point, so the cortical sheet is a distorted but faithful atlas of the retina; a pinhead of cortex serves a pinhead of the scene, and the atlas is deliberately lopsided, because the fovea, a speck of the retinal surface, commands an outsized share of cortical territory, so the glance itself owns more brain than the rest of the room combined. There, for the first time, cells answer not spots of light but structure: bars and edges at a particular orientation, in a particular place, often with a preferred direction of drift. Position, orientation and motion direction are now explicit signals in the traffic. From there the streams separate further: one line of stations specialising in where things are and how they move, another in what things are, form, colour and identity, until the cascade ends in cells that answer faces and familiar objects. And then, with no further fanfare, the construction is finished and simply delivered: her face, upright, smiling, three metres away, known. The binding question, how the parallel streams that stayed separate almost the whole way home become one single experience, deserves its honest answer: there is no single place where the picture is pasted together. The reunification is a process, carried by the coordinated outputs of the separated streams and sealed by attention, and the parallelism is preserved precisely because separation is power; each stream solved its own problem cleanly and the brain combines the solved answers. The brain was handed a million difference reports and it built a person. Nothing anywhere along the way was a picture. The picture is the brain's conclusion.

Host: Guide, the season asked you to keep three sentences alive all year. Say them now, one breath each, because tonight made all three visible in a single glance.

Guide: First: vision is differences, not absolutes. From the photopigment to the cortex, the question asked at every station was never how much light, but how much light compared with what came before and what lies beside. Second: vision is parallel, not one wire. The glance never travelled as a single picture on a single cable; it left the eye as a fleet of edited streams, detail, motion, brightness comparisons and more, travelling together but kept separate, to be combined only in construction. Third: vision is constructed, not received. The brain is handed a briefing of comparisons and timings, and it builds the room, the face and the smile; seeing is the last step of an engineering process, not the first step of looking at one. Three sentences, one glance, no exceptions anywhere on the line.

Host: And there is one more callback a finale owes. Take us back to where this whole journey was first drawn, long before the physiology had names.

Guide: Series 1, episode two of season two, From Light to Vision. If you were there, you remember the sketch: light enters the eye, is focused, is caught by the retina, travels as a signal to the brain, and seeing happens. A clean line with arrows, the foundations of everything that followed, drawn for students meeting the journey for the first time. Now look at what that line has become. The catch has players and timings: a chromophore snap in a fifth of a trillionth of a second, a cascade with named runners and counted amplification. The cable is a million edited difference reports in parallel streams, not one wire. The brain's arrival is a construction with stations for orientation, motion and identity, gated at a gateway that talks back. The foundations sketch is now the engineering blueprint, and that, in one sentence, is what this entire series exists to do: replace arrows with machinery, one season at a time.

Host: Bring it to the chair for the last time this season. Why does holding the whole pipeline change what an optometrist does on an ordinary Tuesday?

Guide: Because every measurement you take is a probe of one station of tonight's chain, and the skill of reading performance back to a station depends on knowing the chain. A letter chart interrogates the fovea's private lines and the cortex that uses them. A visual field map interrogates the mosaic and its cables and their layout. A pupil finding or a smooth pursuit movement speaks for stations between eye and brainstem. You do not need to re-teach any of those tests here; you need the map behind them. When a patient's complaint is about seeing, the complaint is about one or more stations of a signal that is built, and normal vision is not the absence of trouble but the sound performance of an entire factory. Tonight you have walked every floor of that factory, and the walk is what turns a list of tests into an understanding of the patient.

Host: Before we close the season, the ritual. Which myths about seeing does tonight's single glance finally kill?

Guide: Three. First, the camera myth: the eye does not take photographs; the retina edits before anything leaves, and the brain builds afterwards, so the camera analogy survives only for the optics at the front. Second, the one-wire myth: vision is not one picture travelling down one cable to one screen somewhere; it is a fleet of parallel difference streams, and the richness of seeing lives in their parallelism and their recombination. Third, the passive-receiver myth: nothing in this system waits passively to be stimulated, from the always-running dark current to the gateway's gated attention to the cortex's reconstruction; the visual machine works hardest exactly where it seems most still. Hold those three refusals and you hold the season.

Host: And the detail students will underestimate longest?

Guide: The pause. After a season of molecules and circuits, the deepest thing in the retina is a silence: light arrives as a stop signal, the current that was running all along quiets, and that quieting, read against a baseline that never sleeps, is the beginning of sight. The most important event in the retina is something not happening. Whenever a measurement seems to show the eye doing nothing, remember that in this system stillness is a report, and quiet is how it says light.

Host: One question, deliberately, we never answered this season: the friend's face has colour, and tonight's glance was colourless. Why did we leave it, and where does it go?

Tonight you learned that wavelengths of light enter the eye as ordinary physics, and that three kinds of cone catch them in overlapping bands; next season shows how colour comes out the far end as an experience the physical world never contained. How a brain builds redness from overlapping agreements, and contrast from comparisons, is the story of the next ten hours, and it begins exactly where tonight's glance stopped asking.
