# Description: Every living tissue in the eye must breathe, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks maps where each ocular structure draws its oxygen in normal physiology. Learn how the cornea takes its oxygen almost entirely from the air through the tear film, why the epithelium is the best-fed layer and the endothelium leans on the aqueous, and how the whole cornea shifts to a quieter low-oxygen economy when the lids close in sleep. Meet the lens in its deliberately quiet pocket between aqueous and vitreous, the retina's dual supply from the retinal circulation and the choroid, and the conjunctiva and lids that sample air and blood alike. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want the eye's normal physiology explained for the ear.

Host: Every living tissue in the eye has to breathe, and tonight we map exactly where each one draws its breath. The cornea carries no blood vessels and still never starves. The lens sits sealed inside a capsule and is fed from two quiet reservoirs. The retina runs two supply lines into one tissue, and its busiest hours are spent in the dark. Tonight we ask the supply question behind that story: where does the oxygen come from, tissue by tissue, in the normal eye?

Guide: Thank you, and let me set the frame, because it holds for the whole series. This is normal physiology only: how the healthy eye runs, with no disease detours. We will not re-teach anatomy that Series 2 built for us; when we lean on it, we will point back to the exact episode. And tonight is a supply map, drawn tissue by tissue, organised by one idea: the eye is a transparent instrument living in the open air, and transparency forbids blood vessels in the very tissues that burn the most oxygen. So the eye uses what surrounds it. Air, tear film, aqueous humour, vitreous body, and blood vessels parked neatly out of the light: those are the reservoirs, and every ocular tissue shops at one or more of them. We will walk the cornea first and meet the open-eye and closed-eye regimes, then the lens in its quiet pocket, then the retina with its two supply lines, then the conjunctiva and lids, and we will finish with the whole map recited in one breath, the way you would want to say it back in an oral examination.

Host: Before we shop, justify the need. Why does the eye need oxygen at all? What is oxygen actually paying for inside these tissues?

Guide: The same bill every tissue in the body pays, with eye-specific stakes. Cells make their usable energy by breaking down glucose, and oxygen is what allows the cell to extract the full value from that fuel. We built the machinery in episode four, Cellular Function of Ocular Tissues, and spent it properly in episode five, Ocular Metabolism; tonight treats those two as given and asks about delivery. What makes the eye special is what the energy buys. The corneal endothelium runs pumps that push water out of the cornea, and those pumps are a large part of why the cornea stays clear and compact rather than bloated with fluid. The lens spends energy holding its fibres in good order and keeping its interior transparent. The photoreceptors of the retina spend energy restoring the charged state that light disturbs, moment by moment, all day long. Cells that move water against a gradient, and cells that reset their chemistry thousands of times a second, are cells with a serious appetite. Throttle their oxygen and the pumps falter first. That is the entire stakes of tonight: oxygen keeps the pumps running, and the pumps keep the eye clear and working.

Host: Now the paradox at the centre of it. Blood vessels deliver oxygen everywhere else in the body, so why can the eye not simply be plumbed with vessels through its clear tissues?

Guide: Because the clear tissues are optical instruments. Light entering the eye must pass, in order, through the cornea, the aqueous humour, the lens and the vitreous before it reaches the retina. Anything in that path has to be transparent, and blood absorbs and scatters light; a cornea or a lens carrying vessels would be a frosted window. So the design rule for the front of the eye is blunt: transparency demands avascularity. The cornea carries no vessels. The lens carries no vessels and is even wrapped in a capsule, a sealed transparent bag. The vitreous carries essentially none of its own. And that rule creates a second rule, the one that gives tonight its title: avascularity demands creative plumbing. If the tissue cannot carry a supply line, the supply must come to it instead: dissolved out of the air, dissolved in the tears, dissolved in the aqueous, dissolved in the vitreous, or delivered by vessels that stop just short of the optical zone. Five reservoirs, in other words, and every ocular tissue is a customer of at least one. Now let us watch the arrangement work, starting at the very front.

Host: Start with the cornea, the tissue that breaks the rule most boldly. It is about half a millimetre thick, completely without vessels, and permanently exposed to the room. How does it breathe?

Guide: Through the air, almost entirely, and the courier is the tear film. The atmosphere around us is about one fifth oxygen, and the whole atmosphere presses with a pressure of about seven hundred and sixty millimetres of mercury at sea level. At the surface of the open eye, the oxygen available to dissolve into the tears carries a pressure of about one hundred and fifty-five millimetres of mercury. Hold those figures; they describe the open-eye regime, and they are among the most useful numbers in ocular physiology. Oxygen from the air dissolves into the tear film, that thin salty lake resting on the corneal surface, and from there it diffuses inward, always down its pressure gradient, layer by layer. The corneal epithelium, the outermost cellular sheet, sits closest to the source and takes the richest share, which is why the epithelium is the best-fed layer of the cornea. Beneath it lies the stroma, the thick clear scaffold that makes up most of the cornea's bulk, a little over three quarters of it being water, and it is quiet tissue, sparse in cells, so it consumes little of what diffuses through. But by the depth of the endothelium, the single cell layer at the back, the air-borne supply has largely been spent on the way in. And the endothelium, remember, is the layer running the water pumps that keep the whole cornea clear. The busiest pump room in the tissue, sitting at the bottom of the gradient with the weakest access to the air. The cornea's answer is a second source, and it comes from inside the eye.

Host: The aqueous. So the endothelium breathes from behind. Tell us how that inner supply works, and where it comes from.

Guide: From behind, yes. The aqueous humour is the clear fluid filling the space between the cornea and the lens, made by the ciliary processes behind the iris out of blood, filtered and secreted across a barrier so fine that almost no cells or large proteins ride along. What the aqueous does carry, delightfully for tonight's story, is dissolved oxygen. It picks that oxygen up from the blood that made it, and gathers a little more across the iris, whose vessels lie bathed in the fluid they help fill. So the anterior chamber is not merely an optical space; it is a slow-moving delivery route, and the corneal endothelium sits directly upon it like a dock. The endothelium draws oxygen from the aqueous at its posterior face, enough to run its pumps and to serve the deepest stroma from behind, while the air serves the front. One cornea, two sources, fore and aft. The oxygen pressures in the anterior chamber sit in the tens of millimetres of mercury, well below the air value, but steady and dependable, a supply that never switches off day or night. Two episodes of Series 2 belong beside everything I have just said. Series 2, episode six of season four, Corneal Endothelium, showed you the pump room itself, cell by cell. And Series 2, episode eight of season four, Corneal Blood and Nutritional Supply, walked the limbus, the vessel ring parked around the cornea's edge, close enough to assist at the periphery and kept firmly out of the clear zone.

Host: Now the switch I have been waiting for. The open eye drinks from the air. What happens at bedtime, when the lids close and the air is shut out?

Guide: The eye quietly changes economies, and this is one of the loveliest built-in rhythms in the whole body. Close the lids and you close the main supply. The closed eye is a sealed chamber: the trapped tear film holds a little dissolved oxygen, but that reserve is spent within moments, and from then on the cornea lives on what the lids can trickle in. The inside surfaces of the lids are lined with the palpebral conjunctiva, a thin membrane richly threaded with vessels, and it becomes the night shift: oxygen diffuses from those vessels across the lid's own tissues into the trapped tear layer, and from there to the cornea. The numbers tell the story plainly. Where the open-eye tear film sat near one hundred and fifty-five millimetres of mercury, the closed-eye value falls to roughly a third of that, around fifty-five. And the cornea responds exactly as a well-designed system should: consumption is throttled to match the thinner supply, gradients relax, and the tissue runs its slower night-time ledger. This is normal nightly physiology, not a nightly emergency. On waking, the lids lift, the tear film is refreshed with every blink, and the open-eye regime resumes within moments. How contact lenses, transparent devices worn on the eye, share this nightly budget is a story we are saving for a future series of its own; tonight the eye stands bare, exactly as it comes.

Host: I want to slow down on something you just said: the cornea throttles to match. Why is this nightly dip not a problem the design still needs to solve?

Guide: Because it is the solution. Think of the closed lid as a metering valve the body built in front of its most exposed tissue. During waking hours the cornea works hard in a rich, near-atmospheric oxygen environment. During the night, demand falls, the pumps idle down, and the economy of the tissue matches the thinner supply that the lids provide. The system is designed with two regimes, not one: a daytime regime near one hundred and fifty-five millimetres of mercury, and a night-time regime near fifty-five, each of them self-consistent and each of them normal. The morning eye is measurably a slightly different cornea than the evening eye, a touch thicker, its gradients a touch relaxed, and it returns to its waking baseline quickly after the lids open. Students often meet that fact first as a curiosity, so tonight I want it understood as physiology: the cornea spends roughly a third of every twenty-four hours in a deliberately low-oxygen mode, by design, and comes back from it cleanly every single morning. Hold on to that twin-regime picture. It is the single most useful idea in tonight's map, and we will see echoes of it at the back of the eye before the hour is out.

Host: That twin picture is tonight's property, and I suspect it is deeper than the cornea. Before we leave the front, where do we send listeners who want the cornea's oxygen economy told layer by layer, in full detail?

Guide: To episode seven of Season 3 of this very series, Corneal Oxygenation, which is where the cornea's supply story gets the full treatment it deserves: the fluxes and gradients measured layer by layer, the roles of the limbus and the tear film laid out properly, and the whole night-and-day economy assembled in one place. Tonight is deliberately the whole-eye map, so the cornea gets only its headline here: air in front, aqueous behind, epithelium best fed at the top of the gradient, endothelium docked on the aqueous, lids as the night shift. One more Series 2 pointer rounds out the front-of-eye picture before we go in: Series 2, episode four of season two, Tear Film Anatomy, built the tear film itself, that three-layered film doing double duty tonight, an optical surface and an oxygen lake at the same time.

Host: From the cornea, in through the pupil. The lens: no vessels, no nerves, sealed inside its capsule. Who feeds it, and what does it breathe?

Guide: Two quiet reservoirs, one on each face. In front, the aqueous humour bathes the lens. Behind, the vitreous body, the transparent gel filling the large chamber of the eye, presses against its back surface. The lens is enclosed by its capsule, but a capsule is a thin transparent sheet, not a vault door: oxygen and nutrients diffuse across it readily, and the lens draws what it needs from the fluid on either side. Now notice the kind of neighbourhood the lens lives in. The aqueous in front carries oxygen at pressures in the tens of millimetres of mercury, and the vitreous behind is quieter still, because the retina beyond it is consuming as the gel simply sits there. The lens occupies the two least oxygen-rich rooms of the eye, and it likes it that way. Its consumption is low by design: the mature lens fibres have cleared out their mitochondria, the oxygen-burning machinery of the cell, and only the thin epithelium on its front surface keeps any; most of the lens runs its energy economy on glycolysis, which needs no oxygen at all. We walked that biochemistry in episode five, Ocular Metabolism. The routes themselves were built in Series 2, episode seven of season six, Lens Nutrition, and they are worth revisiting before next week, because next week we ride those routes with cargo aboard.

Host: Why is the lens built to want so little? Everything else on tonight's map seems greedy by comparison.

Guide: Because the lens has one contract with the body: stay clear for a lifetime, and never grow a blood supply. Its fibres are packed with crystallins, the crystal-clear proteins that make the lens a lens, and a quiet, low-oxygen economy is part of how that clarity is defended across decades of service. A tissue that barely breathes also barely leaks the reactive by-products of heavy breathing into its own interior. So the design hands the lens the least reactive corner of the eye: a stable fluid bath on each side, a metabolism that sips rather than gulps, and a capsule to keep the household in order. Supply matched to demand, with demand deliberately kept low. That is the theme of tonight's entire map, and the lens is its purest expression. Where the cornea improvises brilliantly around a hard constraint, the lens simply arranged never to have the constraint bite.

Host: Now the retina, the tissue at the back that actually catches the light. It sits behind the vitreous, behind the lens, behind the aqueous, as far from the air as an ocular tissue can be. How is it fed?

Guide: With two supply lines, and this is the retina's signature arrangement. The inner layers of the retina, the side facing the vitreous, are fed by the retinal circulation: the central retinal artery arrives inside the optic nerve and spreads across the inner surface as a fine tree of vessels feeding the inner tissue. The outer layers, the side pressed against the back wall, are fed by the choroid, the dense vascular layer lying between the retina and the sclera, with its own mattress of capillaries, the choriocapillaris, lying directly beneath the outer retina. And the outer retina is precisely where the photoreceptors live, the rods and cones that turn light into the first drafts of sight. Their inner segments are among the most mitochond-packed stretches of cells anywhere in the body, rows upon rows of tiny power plants. The anatomy of those two circulations, vessel by vessel and layer by layer, was built in Series 2, episode one of season eight, Retinal Blood Supply; tonight we take that arrangement as given and read its logic instead. Two circuits serving one tissue, each matched to the half it feeds, and both threaded through and behind the tissue so that the light's path stays as clear as the design allows.

Host: Then here is the fact I find wonderful and slightly eerie: the hungriest part of the whole eye works hardest when the lights go out. Explain that.

Guide: Happily, because it reverses the intuition most students carry into this room. In darkness, the photoreceptor is not resting; it is running flat out. In the dark, channels in the photoreceptor's outer segment stand open, a steady inward flow of charged particles, the dark current, runs constantly, and the cell must pump those particles back out, without pause, just to hold its ground. That pumping costs energy, and the bill lands on the mitochondria crowded into the inner segments. When light finally arrives, it closes those channels, the current falls, and the cell's workload actually drops. So in normal physiology, photoreceptors consume more oxygen in darkness than in light. The retina breathes hardest at night, quietly, behind closed lids. Now set tonight's two ideas side by side and admire them. The lids close, and the cornea steps down to its frugal night-time economy at the front of the eye. At the very same hours, at the back, the photoreceptors are running their hungriest shifts of the entire day. One eye, two regimes, front and back, running in opposite directions through the same darkness. Details like that are why this series runs a hundred episodes long.

Host: And the choroid, the supplier of that hungry outer retina. What kind of plumbing does a job like that take?

Guide: Extravagant plumbing. The choroid is among the most richly perfused tissues, gram for gram, in the entire body; blood streams through it at rates that look wasteful until you see what the flow is buying. Such generous flow keeps oxygen plentiful on the choroid's side of the exchange, which keeps the pressure gradient into the outer retina steep and steady, so the photoreceptors are never left waiting on delivery. The generous flow also carries warmth away, quietly steadying the temperature of the busy outer retina. The design logic is the same one running through the whole of tonight: where demand is extreme, supply is made extravagant. And note where the plumbing sits: behind the photoreceptors, against the back wall of the eye, serving the retina without ever standing in the image. The inner circulation feeds the inner retina from within; the choroid feeds the outer retina from behind; and between them they answer the deepest appetite in the eye.

Host: Let us not forget the front-of-eye small fry: the conjunctiva and the lids. Where do they fit in the oxygen map?

Guide: As samplers, and as the night shift we already met. The conjunctiva, the thin transparent membrane lining the lids and draping across the front of the sclera, holds a privileged position: its outer surface faces the air, and its own tissues carry vessels. It samples air and blood alike, drawing on whichever reservoir is nearer and richer at the moment, and its surface shares the tear film with the cornea, so the oxygen dissolved in that film serves both tissues from one lake. The lids are the hidden half of the cornea's story: lined with the vessel-rich palpebral conjunctiva, they become the cornea's overnight supply line, trickling oxygen through the closed-eye tear film hour after hour. By day they sweep and renew that same film with every blink, recharging the surface lake thousands of times a day. The front-of-eye tissues look like bit players until you see them as one marketplace: air, tears, conjunctiva, lids and cornea, all trading the same dissolved oxygen across the thinnest of borders, with the terms of trade changing the moment the lids close.

Host: Recite the map for me now, the way you would want a student to say it back in an oral examination. Tissue by tissue, source by source, numbers included.

Guide: With pleasure, and slowly, because this list is worth memorising outright. The cornea: no vessels of its own; fed almost entirely by the air through the tear film, about one hundred and fifty-five millimetres of mercury at the open-eye surface at sea level; epithelium best fed at the top of the gradient; stroma quiet in the middle; endothelium supplied from behind by the aqueous; and every night the closed lids drop the whole economy to about fifty-five, roughly a third, returning it by morning. The aqueous humour: a clear delivery fluid, carrying dissolved oxygen from ciliary blood to the corneal endothelium and to the front of the lens. The lens: avascular and capsuled; fed by aqueous in front and vitreous behind; consumption kept low by design, most of its energy glycolytic, sitting deliberately in the eye's quietest oxygen pocket. The vitreous: a transparent gel with essentially no vessels of its own, more reservoir than spender. The retina: dual supply, the retinal circulation for the inner layers and the choroid for the outer; photoreceptors among the most oxygen-hungry cells in the body, and hungriest of all in darkness. The choroid: perfusion so generous it holds the outer retina's gradient steep and steady. The conjunctiva and lids: air and blood alike by day, the cornea's trickle supply by night. Reservoirs, routes and gradients, and not a single vessel where the light needs to pass.

Host: Which part of this map do students underestimate most?

Guide: The air itself. Students arrive believing blood vessels are the body's only serious oxygen supply, so they hunt the cornea for plumbing that is not there and overlook the source sitting in plain sight: the atmosphere, delivered through a film of tears. The second miss is the switch. Because the open-eye and closed-eye regimes are both silent and both perfectly normal, learners memorise one number, the waking value, and never notice that the eye spends a third of every day in its other economy. An optometrist who owns the switch understands the morning eye and the sleeping eye at once, and holds the design logic behind every transparent device that will ever share the cornea's air supply, all from one idea.
