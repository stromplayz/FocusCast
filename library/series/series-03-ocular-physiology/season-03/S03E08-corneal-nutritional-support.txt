# Description: The cornea has no blood vessels of its own, so every working day it must be fed from two directions at once, and this episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks walks both delivery routes. Learn how glucose, the cornea's staple fuel, arrives mainly from the aqueous humour through the endothelium and secondarily as a trickle from the tear film through the epithelium, and how amino acids are hauled inward by active transport to keep a surface that renews itself. Meet the vitamins, from ascorbate concentrated into the aqueous at levels far above blood to the B family riding along for the enzyme machinery, and hear how the tears deliver epithelial maintenance signals such as epidermal growth factor with every blink. Water and ions complete the grocery list, and the episode closes with the design lesson that a two-front supply system exists because a single supply line would be a single point of failure for transparency. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want corneal nourishment explained clearly for the ear.

Now ask a stranger how that window stays alive, and the question sounds absurd, because glass is not alive. The window at the front of your eye is different in exactly one way: it is a living window, a slab of cells and fibres about half a millimetre thick that must be fed every hour it stays clear, and it owns no blood vessels to do the feeding. Host: Then start with the design problem, because it still surprises people. Most tissues of the body are threaded with capillaries, fed from within a hair's breadth of every cell. The cornea sits in the middle of a vascular neighbourhood: the sclera around it, the conjunctiva over it, the iris behind it, all richly supplied, and yet the cornea stays bloodless. Remind us why, and what that costs it?

Guide: The cornea is avascular on purpose. Clarity is its entire job description, and a blood vessel is an obstruction to light; a network of them would frost the window from within. So the cornea keeps its interior free of vessels and pays the price: no local delivery service. The blood that feeds your skin and your liver flows past those organs' own cells; the cornea's cells live like a town beyond the last road, and everything they need must be brought in from outside the tissue. The anatomy side of that bargain belongs to earlier conversations: why the arrangement stays clear we asked in Series 2, episode nine of season four, Corneal Transparency, and the vessels that skirt the cornea's rim at the limbus are the territory of Series 2, episode eight of season four, Corneal Blood and Nutritional Supply. Tonight is the physiology that follows from that anatomy: if there are no pipes inside, the pipes must run to the borders. And the borders are two. One faces the aqueous humour behind. One faces the tear film in front. The cornea is fed from both at once.

Host: Two borders, then: an inner door and an outer door, and every substance crossing either one obeys some transport rule. You have a whole vocabulary for this from way back. Before we open the doors, put the vocabulary in working order for us: what are the ways a substance can cross a cell layer?

Guide: Three ways matter tonight, and we built all three in our own Season 1, episode seven, Nutrient Transport in Ocular Tissues, so here is the refresher in one breath. Simple diffusion is drift: a substance wanders from where it is concentrated to where it is scarce, needing no machinery, only a gradient. Facilitated diffusion is drift with a doorman: the substance still moves down its gradient, but a carrier protein helps it across a membrane it could not traverse alone, and the doorman can saturate and can select. Active transport is hired labour: the cell spends energy, usually drawn from adenosine triphosphate, to pump a substance against its gradient, from scarce toward concentrated, which is how a tissue accumulates a substance its supply fluid carries only dilutely. Keep those three in your pocket: drift, drift with a doorman, hired labour. Every item on tonight's list crosses one border or the other by one of those three, and which one it uses tells you a great deal about why the cornea needs it. There is also a third variable hiding behind the vocabulary: distance. Diffusion is effortless over a cell's thickness and punishing over a millimetre, so the geometry of the cornea, thin in front, flooded from behind, is itself part of the supply design, as you are about to see with the first and biggest item.

Host: Now the list itself, taken in order of tonnage, starting with the big ticket: glucose. Why does glucose head the list, and how much of it are we talking about?

Guide: Glucose heads the list because it is staple fuel and construction material at once. Episode six covered the destination chemistry, so one sweep of recap: corneal cells burn glucose for energy, and the epithelium in particular runs much of it down side pathways that feed its constant rebuilding. The numbers are ordinary for a tissue and extraordinary for a window. Glucose in the aqueous humour sits at roughly half the concentration found in blood, enough to work with, never a huge surplus, so supply must be continuous; the cornea stores almost none. And consumption is lopsided: the epithelium, the busy outer layer, takes by far the largest share, which is worth noticing, because the epithelium is the layer farthest from the inner supply. The hungriest layer living farthest from the main pantry is one of the quiet themes of tonight, and it is why the cornea bothers to keep a second, smaller pantry at its front. But the main route first, taken slowly.

Host: Take it slowly then. The inner route: from the blood, into the aqueous, across the back surface of the cornea, onward to the cells. Walk me through it, step by step.

Guide: Step by step. First, the ciliary body, the ring of secreting tissue behind the iris, filters blood and adds its own secretions to make aqueous humour, the clear fluid filling the space behind the cornea. Aqueous is blood's tidied cousin: proteins mostly removed, but glucose, amino acids, vitamins and ions all aboard, at concentrations that differ from blood in instructive ways, as the ascorbate story will show. Second, that fluid circulates, flowing from the posterior chamber through the pupil and forward, bathing the back of the cornea. Third, it meets the cornea's inner door, the endothelium, the single cell layer we mapped in Series 2, episode six of season four, Corneal Endothelium. The endothelium is arranged so that glucose crosses by facilitated diffusion, the doorman arrangement, into the stroma. Fourth, the stroma, the thick watery collagen middle layer, lets glucose simply drift by simple diffusion, following the gradient from behind to front. Fifth, at the front door, the epithelium takes its share through carriers of its own. The gradient never flattens, because the aqueous is continuously replaced and the cells continuously consume: high behind, low within, flow forever. And the aqueous does not merely sit beside the cornea; it is a flowing bath, drained and rebuilt steadily through the day, so the consignment the endothelium sees is always fresh, never a stagnant pool that the tissue could slowly deplete. That is the inner supply line, and it is why the deepest layers of the cornea are its best-fed real estate.

Host: One inner route, five steps, and the tissue drinks from behind. So why bother with a second route at all? The tears look like a thin, changeable film, and the aqueous sounds like a proper reservoir. What can the outer door honestly contribute?

Guide: A trickle, honestly, but a trickle with a purpose. Glucose exists in tears too, at a far lower concentration than in aqueous, a small fraction of blood levels, and the outer epithelial cells can take it up across their outward-facing membranes. By tonnage it is a minor road; by design it is not, and here is why. The epithelium, the hungriest layer, is the layer the tear route reaches first: the inner route must supply the whole tissue from behind and let diffusion do the long haul to the front, while the outer route drops provisions directly at the door of the neediest neighbourhood. The two routes also keep different hours. Close your eyes, and the tear film stops renewing, its small reservoir stagnating and thinning, while the aqueous behind keeps flowing regardless; the inner line never sleeps. And the traffic of the outer line changes with what sits on the porch: a contact lens, as a normal-function example, is an added layer the outer supply must work across, while the inner line carries on undisturbed. Two routes, different timetables, different vulnerabilities. The lowness of tear glucose is itself a lesson worth pocketing: the film is thin, drained away to the drainage ports and rebuilt with every blink, so nothing in it accumulates, and a courier that empties its satchel every few seconds can never carry a warehouse. The cornea does not need the tears to carry much; it needs them to carry often. Hold that thought; the design lesson at the end is built on it.

Host: Item two, then: amino acids. Glucose is fuel, mostly. Amino acids are bricks. Why does the cornea need constant brick deliveries, and why can it not simply drift them in the way glucose drifts through the stroma?

Guide: Because in this tissue the builders never clock off. Recall the renewal schedule: the corneal epithelium replaces itself completely, from base to surface, every seven to ten days, comfortably inside a fortnight, and we walked that conveyor in episode four, Corneal Epithelial Renewal. A surface rebuilding that fast is permanently in the market for protein: structural protein for new cells, enzymes for the chemistry of episode six, receptors, carriers and junctions for the barrier work of episode five. Amino acids are the raw material, and here is the catch: the cornea cannot make most of them from scratch, and the concentrations it needs inside its cells are higher than the concentrations its supply fluids carry. Drift moves substance from plenty toward scarcity, and the cornea needs the opposite, gathering building blocks from dilute fluids into concentrated cytoplasm. So it hires labour. Sodium-driven active transport hauls amino acids inward across both doors, with families of carriers sorted by cargo: one set for the neutral amino acids, one for the acidic, one for the basic. Both the aqueous and the tears carry the cargo, so both doors load bricks, and the energy spent is the price of building faster than drift would ever allow. Notice, too, what the carrier families imply: selectivity. The surface does not take up amino acids at random; it takes what its building programme calls for, which is why the census of carriers in a cell tells you what the cell is trying to build.

Host: Item three: vitamins, and I know you have been saving a favourite, because the first of them is the one substance on the list that gets delivered concentrated, not dilute. Set up ascorbate for us.

Guide: Ascorbate, vitamin C, is the showpiece of the inventory, the one cargo that arrives richer than it left. Blood plasma carries ascorbate at a modest level. The ciliary body, the same secreting ring that builds the aqueous, actively hauls ascorbate across into the posterior chamber against the gradient, hiring its transport labour, and the result is aqueous humour with ascorbate at levels several times higher than the blood it came from. That is the hired-labour vocabulary we refreshed earlier tonight from Season 1, and ascorbate is its finest ocular example. Why bother concentrating it? Because of where the cornea lives: front and centre, bathed in light, and light with oxygen and time is the recipe for oxidative wear, reactive species nudging lipids and proteins out of true. Ascorbate is the tissue's antioxidant ration, arriving from behind through the endothelium and stroma to stand guard through the depths, and the tears carry a smaller amount too, so the front gets its share from the outer door. Aqueous ascorbate far above blood is one of the cleanest demonstrations in the eye that the body does not merely accept what the circulation sends; it curates. And the ciliary body pays for the privilege, spending transport energy on every drop, a standing tax on the circulation collected for the sake of the tissues that live downstream in the light.

Host: From the antioxidant ration to the mechanic's toolbox: the B vitamins. They never get the headlines, yet I suspect nothing on the list works without them. What exactly are they doing in there?

Guide: The B family are the cofactors, the small molecules the enzymes of episode six must bind in order to function, and without steady deliveries the machinery slows no matter how much glucose and oxygen arrive. Run the roll call lightly. Thiamine sits at the doorway of the chemistry that first processes glucose. Riboflavin and niacin carry the electrons that the burning reactions hand off. Pantothenic acid sits inside the energy currency chemistry itself. Pyridoxine works the amino acid traffic, fitting bricks together. Folate and cobalamin serve the DNA-building work that epithelial renewal depends on. Now the delivery problem: these vitamins are water-soluble, the body stores little of any of them, and the cornea stores less than most tissues, so supply must be continuous, riding the aqueous from behind and the tears from the front. For the B family there is no warehouse anywhere in the plan, only a pipeline; the machinery of episode six runs on deliveries as they arrive, which is one more reason the two supply lines must never both falter. One more arrival completes the shelf, and it bridges to tonight's next item: vitamin A, carried in the tears, which the surface needs not as fuel but as a signal, and signals are where we are going next.

Host: Signals, then. We have spent tonight on feeding, but you have been hinting that the tears do something subtler: they talk. Set up the growth factors. What are they, and what are they saying to the surface?

Guide: Growth factors are the chemical conversation of the ocular surface, and the tears deliver the mail with every blink. The name to know is epidermal growth factor, and it travels with friends: hepatocyte growth factor, nerve growth factor, the regulatory transforming growth factors, and a supporting cast of messenger molecules. These are proteins present in tears in tiny amounts, far beneath the tonnage of glucose or water, and they are neither fuel nor building material. They are instructions. An epithelium renewing itself weekly needs coordination: which cells to move, when to divide, how to mature as they migrate outward, how to knit their junctions into a barrier. The tear film carries those instructions and brushes them across the surface at fifteen to twenty blinks a minute, so the mail is not only delivered; it is refreshed, each blink printing a new copy of the message over a surface that never holds yesterday's instructions for long. The fluid's credentials come from our own Season 2: episode six of that season, Tear Secretion, showed how the lacrimal glands manufacture it and load it with proteins, and episode four of that season, Aqueous Layer Function, inventoried its cargoes riding a few micrometres deep. Tonight the inventory gains its quietest item: the tears are not only the surface's lunchbox and lung; they are its foreman, coaching the epithelium chemically, blink after blink.

Host: Which leaves the last items on the list, the ones so ordinary we forget they are items at all: water and ions. What are they doing on the grocery list of a tissue that is, by weight, mostly water already?

Guide: They are on the list precisely because that water fraction is not free; it is managed. Water is the solvent every other cargo travels in, the medium the stromal lattice is swollen with, and the quantity held must sit inside a narrow band. Recall episodes two and three: episode two, Corneal Hydration, set the band, the hydration at which the collagen spacing stays regular and light passes unscattered; episode three, Corneal Endothelial Pump Function, showed the machinery that holds it there. Tonight, notice only the supply side of that story: the water the cornea manages arrives with the aqueous from behind and the tears in front, and it brings passengers. Sodium and chloride set the osmotic pull that water follows. Potassium works inside the cells, charging the membrane batteries the pumps spend. Bicarbonate buffers the acidity of a tissue whose chemistry never stops. The ions are not an afterthought; they are the reason the water goes where it is needed and no further. And there is a courier-side detail: the tears arrive with their own saltiness, tuned near the osmolarity we studied in our tear season, so the outer bath pulls gently outward on the surface cells, while the aqueous behind sets the deep bath the endothelium manages against. Supply sets the table; regulation, the work of episodes two and three, keeps the seating honest.

Host: There is the whole list: glucose, amino acids, vitamins, growth factors, water and ions. Now the design lesson you have been saving, the one about two front doors. Every other organ feeds from one direction, the blood. Why does this tissue insist on two supply lines at once?

Guide: Because on this one organ a single supply line would be a single point of failure, and the failure would be visible from across the room. Walk the counterfactual. Suppose the cornea fed only from behind, from the aqueous alone. The front layers, epithelium and front stroma, would sit at the far end of a long diffusion haul, the slowest-fed real estate in the tissue, and anything that came between the front of the eye and its outer supply, a stagnant tear film between blinks, a lens resting on the surface, a closed lid thinning the outer reservoir overnight, would throttle the front line first. Now the reverse: suppose the cornea fed only from the front, from tears alone. The tissue's nutrition would hang on the most changeable, most renewable, most interruptible fluid the body makes, a film rebuilt every few seconds and abandoned every night. The eye refuses both bargains. It feeds its window from inside and outside at once: an inner line that never sleeps, and an outer line that renews with every blink, each covering the other's weak hours. Even the vessels at the rim join the design, standing at the limbus as a border patrol rather than a supply line, close enough to manage the frontier, far enough away to keep the window clear. Redundancy is not waste here; it is the price of keeping a living tissue glass-clear, and the cornea pays it twice.

Host: Bring that to the chair, carefully, inside our normal-function fence. Our listeners examine corneas every working day. When they look at a healthy cornea at the slit lamp, what are they actually looking at, in tonight's terms?

Guide: They are looking at a supply system visibly succeeding. Start with the obvious: a clear cornea is the report card. Transparency is what the whole arrangement delivers, and every time the slit beam passes through and the iris detail beneath is crisp, both supply lines did their work that hour. Next, the outer line is the one you can watch working: a complete, stable tear film with a smooth surface between blinks is the outer delivery schedule running on time, and the blink itself is pump stroke and delivery van in one, which is why our tear season taught you to read the blink as part of surface physiology. The inner line runs silent: you cannot see the aqueous meeting the endothelium, but you infer it every time the stroma holds its proper thickness and its hydration band through a long day. And the umbrella example completes the picture: a contact lens on the eye is an added layer across the outer porch, the outer supply slightly renegotiated while the inner line continues undisturbed. None of that is disease talk. It is the healthy design read at the chair, and it makes an ordinary clear cornea look like what it is: an achievement, renewed minute by minute.

Host: One last teaching question, the one we always ask. Of everything on tonight's list, which detail do you find students and clinicians underestimate the most?

Guide: The tears, every time. Students file the tear film under wetting and comfort, a lubricant, and miss that it is a supply route and a messaging channel of its own. Hold tonight's facts together and the scale lands: the cornea's hungriest layer takes most of the glucose, and the tears are the only line that delivers at that layer's own front door, while at the same time brushing chemical instructions across the surface fifteen to twenty times a minute. A second quiet surprise is worth carrying out: the cornea mostly eats from behind. Intuition puts the food where the food is visible, in the tears, when the main pantry is the aqueous, a fluid most patients have never heard of. An organ fed chiefly by a fluid nobody can see, topped up in front by a film everyone can see and nobody credits with chemistry: that inverted picture is tonight's real lesson.
