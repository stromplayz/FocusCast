Host: Every season of this series ends with a handover, and tonight the handover is the most literal one we have ever attempted. For nine episodes we have lived inside the retina, watching light become electricity one molecular step at a time, and tonight we finally walk to the loading dock and read the shipping list: what does the retina actually send to the brain? The answer is not the picture most people imagine, and that gap between imagination and delivery is the whole story. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome to the season finale of Retina Physiology, the tenth and last episode of Season 6 in Series 3. Nine times this season our guide has walked us through the retina's engine rooms, and tonight our guide reads out the manifest and seals the last crate. Before the farewells there is real teaching to do, and it may be the most useful conversation of the season. Set the frame for us: when the last ganglion cell has fired, what has the eye actually produced?

Guide: Thank you, and what a season to close. Let me draw today's boundary first, because it holds to the very end. Everything we have taught so far happened inside the retina: photons caught by pigment, currents bent, signals pooled, filtered and shaped. Today we stand at the boundary and watch the finished product leave. And the finished product is not what most people expect. There is no image in the optic nerve. Nothing resembling a photograph leaves the eye. What leaves is a coded stream, a telegraph of spikes, organised into parallel channels, each carrying a different edited report about the world. You will remember from episode one, Why the Retina Is a Neural Tissue, that the retina is a piece of the brain parked in the back of the eye, and tonight that fact pays its full dividend: brain tissue does not forward pictures, it writes dispatches. Tonight we read five of those dispatches, the five headline transformations of retinal coding, and then we walk the whole season one last time before we open the door to the season ahead.

Host: Before the transformations, ground us in the raw material, because I want the contrast with the output to be sharp. What does the retina receive from the optics, and in what currency does it first speak?

Guide: It receives an image: inverted, miniature, focused by the eye's optics onto the receptor mosaic. And inside the retina the early currency is not spikes at all. Photoreceptors, bipolar cells and horizontal cells speak in graded potentials, slow analogue shifts whose size tracks the stimulus, brighter or darker, larger or smaller. The conversion to the nerve's universal currency happens at the last step. The ganglion cells of episode nine are the first cells in the visual pathway to fire true action potentials, and they fire them for a practical reason: only spikes survive the journey. A graded whisper would fade along a cable tens of millimetres long; a spike renews itself as it travels and arrives exactly as it left. So hold the sequence: light into graded whispers, which was the work of episodes two through five, and whispers into the staccato of spikes, which is tonight's departure lounge. Beyond the eye, the entire brain speaks only that staccato language.

Host: Now the arithmetic that frames everything, because the numbers alone tell the story. How many sensors feed this system, and how many lines leave it?

Guide: The retina carries about one hundred million photoreceptors, the rods and cones we met in episode two, and the overwhelming majority are rods, the night specialists of episode three. The entire output of that enormous array is roughly a million and a half ganglion cell axons, the fibres that gather at the optic disc and pour down the optic nerve. Sit with that ratio for a moment: on average, something like sixty sensors for every outgoing cable, and the average hides extremes in both directions. In the far periphery, hundreds of rods may pool into a single cable; at the foveal centre, allocation approaches one cable for a handful of cones. That mismatch is not a bottleneck by accident, and not by poverty of design. It is the largest single decision the retina makes: one hundred million sensors, a million and a half cables, and therefore everything the brain will ever learn about light must arrive already edited, already compressed, already prioritised. The retina is a living compression and editing studio, and the optic nerve is the courier that carries only the highlights. Tonight's five transformations are the five moves of that editing.

Host: Take them one at a time, then. Transformation one: brightness becomes contrast. Unpack that, because it sounds like a slogan until you see the machinery.

Guide: It becomes machinery the moment you ask what a cable can honestly report. Consider the range the world demands. From a starlit night to full noon sunlight, the light available to the eye varies by a factor of roughly ten billion. No cable can span that range with any finesse: a line tuned to report noon would be deaf at midnight, and the reverse. So the retina does something cleverer, and it does it at every layer. Each ganglion cell compares its small territory against its immediate surround, the centre-surround arrangement built in episode eight, Retinal Signal Processing, and it rescales its own sensitivity to the recent average, the discipline of episode seven, Light Adaptation. What survives to be reported is the difference: how much brighter or darker this patch is than its neighbours, and than itself a few moments ago. The retina ships contrasts, not levels, and that single decision is why the same million and a half cables serve us from midnight to midday.

Host: And that is why this page looks white under a dim lamp and white again under the noon sun, although the light actually entering the eye differs by orders of magnitude between the two cases.

Guide: Precisely. The absolute light changes enormously; the contrast between page and ink barely changes at all. The world's stable, informative quantity is not how much light there is but how light is distributed differently across surfaces, and difference is exactly what the retina preserves. Call it standardisation: every broadcast is normalised to local conditions before it is sent. The same grey patch sitting on white and the same grey sitting on black are two different dispatches, though they reflect identical light. Downstream, the brain will reconstruct the impression of brightness from such comparisons, and that reconstruction belongs to next season. Tonight, notice only the transformation: light arrived as a level and leaves as a comparison.

Host: Transformation two: steady scenes become change reports. Say it plainly, and then convince me, because on the face of it the retina seems willing to ignore half of reality.

Guide: The plain version is that ganglion cells care about what moves or flickers, and go quiet about what holds still. Present a cell with a steady uniform patch of light and its firing surges briefly, then settles toward a modest baseline. Flick that patch, flicker it, sweep an edge across it, and the same cell fires vigorously. The output layer is built to answer change: onsets, offsets, movement, flicker. And the logic is economic. In a stable world, a second report of the same scene carries no new information; it is bandwidth spent on nothing. But when something moves, something has happened, and something is precisely what a survival organ exists to notice. The retina is a news desk, not an archive. What stays the same is old news, and old news is dropped.

Host: Then here is my objection, and I suspect our listeners share it. If the retina only reports change, why does the world not fade whenever we fix our gaze on a stationary object?

Guide: Because the gaze never truly fixes. The eye drifts and trembles and makes tiny involuntary jumps all the time, movements so small you never notice them, yet large enough to keep the image jittering across the receptor mosaic, and that jitter refreshes the change detectors many times each second. The rock-steady world you experience is, at the retinal level, a scene under constant gentle agitation. Physiology proved the point in the most elegant way available: if an image is artificially stabilised on the retina, moved with the eye so that the receptors receive an unchanging pattern, the scene dissolves within seconds into a featureless grey. Steady input is literally invisible to a system built for change. The full choreography of those eye movements is a story for a later season; tonight they appear only as the retina's loyal assistants, keeping the news arriving.

Host: A skeptic's question about the arithmetic, and I want it asked because students always ask it: if the brain wants detail, why not simply grow more cables? Who sets a million and a half as the budget?

Guide: Cables are not free, and the bill is paid in anatomy. Every ganglion cell axon needs room at the optic disc, metabolic support along a journey of tens of millimetres, and downstream machinery to read it. A million and a half fibres already make the optic nerve the thick white trunk you see at the fundus; double it and you crowd the disc, thicken the nerve, and multiply the brain's reading costs. Evolution builds organs inside budgets, and the eye is a fixed ball of space where every square millimetre given to wiring is taken from sensors or from optics. So the design question was never how to send everything. It was how much to send, and from where. The answer, a lavish centre and a summarised surround, is a budget allocated, and every species draws its allocation to match its life: the map in an eagle's nerve is sketched differently from ours. Compression is not the retina's compromise. It is the retina's craft.

Host: Transformation three, then: one huge image becomes parallel channels. This is where the editing gets ambitious.

Guide: This dispatch destroys the last vestige of the camera analogy. A camera sends one copy of the scene. The retina sends many, simultaneously, and each copy answers a different question about the same image. Start with the oldest split, the one that opened episode nine, Retinal Ganglion Cells: the ON and OFF channels. One family of cells fires faster when a patch brightens beyond its surround, another when it darkens. Increments and decrements are reported by separate desks, so every boundary in the world is stamped twice, once as a place where light rises and once as a place where light falls. Then the grain of the reporting splits. Some ganglion cells own tiny territories and report sustained, fine detail: the form channel, the one that reads print and faces. Others own wide territories and answer briskly and transiently: the motion channel, the one that flags movement at the edge of vision. And running through both, the colour comparisons of episode four, Cone Physiology: red weighed against green, blue weighed against yellow, colour shipped as differences between cone types rather than as three absolute intensities. One scene; several simultaneous reports.

Host: Bright against dark, fine against coarse, still against moving, red against green. Why split the work so finely? Would one thorough copy not do?

Guide: One copy cannot do, because the questions have conflicting physics. Detail demands small territories and patient, sustained reporting, which is slow and covers little ground. Motion demands wide territories and instant, transient response, which is coarse and blind to fine grain. Ask one cable to be both and you get a channel that is mediocre at everything. So the retina hires specialists and runs them in parallel, and the same logic repeats for contrast polarity and for colour. It is also the escape from tonight's arithmetic: a million and a half cables could never carry one full uncompressed picture, but they can carry several sharply edited streams at once. A fence before we go on: the circuitry that builds these channels, the bipolar and amacrine cells beneath every split we have named, was episode eight's material, and next season we will walk those circuits again with the brain in view. Tonight they are a shipping decision; next season they are a wiring diagram.

Host: Transformation four: light becomes location-tagged spikes. That sounds almost bureaucratic, but I suspect it is the quiet foundation under everything else.

Guide: It is the foundation. Every ganglion cell owns a territory: the patch of receptor mosaic its circuitry listens to, the receptive field we defined in episode nine. Whatever happens in that patch, brighter, darker, redder, moving, is reported as spikes that carry the address with them. The cell cannot say this light is over there. It simply fires, and the firing means what it means only because of where its cable sits in the bundle. The consequence is beautiful: the map of the retina travels intact into the nerve, neighbouring territories sending neighbouring cables, so the spatial arrangement of the world is preserved point by point all the way back. Anatomists call the principle retinotopy, and it does not stop at the nerve's far end. We traced its whole journey in Series 2, episode ten of season ten, Complete Visual Pathway - Retina to Cortex, where the map arrives at the visual cortex still arranged like the retina that made it. Where, not just what, is the retina's quiet gift to the brain.

Host: But you said the territories are unequal. Tell us how the map is distorted, because I imagine it is distorted on purpose.

Guide: On purpose is right. At the foveal centre the map is nearly lavish: a single ganglion cell may serve only a handful of cones, and at the very centre nearly one, so each cable reports from a pinpoint address and the middle of the visual field is rendered in extraordinary detail. In the far periphery the opposite holds: hundreds of rods pool into one cable through the convergence circuits of episode three, Rod Physiology, so each line reports from a large and vague territory. The result is a deliberately uneven map, a capital city whose suburbs are summarised a line at a time. Location is tagged, but the tagging is budgeted. The retina spends its cables where the owner of the eyes looks, and summarises the rest finely enough that anything moving out there is flagged and can be inspected by turning the eyes toward it. When you explain to a student why acuity falls so steeply away from the centre of gaze, this allocation is the whole explanation.

Host: Which brings us to the fifth transformation, the one the arithmetic promised. Enormous raw data becomes a compressed, prioritised stream. Do the sum properly, and then tell us what is thrown away.

Guide: The sum first. About one hundred million photoreceptors catch the light; roughly a million and a half ganglion cell axons leave the eye. On average that is a shrinkage of something like sixty to one, and the average is honest only because the extremes are extreme: the foveal centre compresses almost not at all, the far periphery compresses hundreds to one. Now the philosophy of the compression, and it is the philosophy of a good editor. Redundancy is dropped. Neighbouring receptors mostly report the same thing, so the predictable part of the signal, the local average, is subtracted at the source by the surround circuitry of episode eight. What survives is the difference, and differences are what cannot be guessed. Surprises are amplified: an unexpected edge, a sudden flicker, a novel contrast earns the firing, while the expected stays quiet. And priority is enforced: the centre of the gaze has first claim on bandwidth, and the periphery is served from what remains. The stream that leaves the eye is not a smaller copy of the image. It is a summary that has kept every surprising, local, moving, contrast-bearing fact, and paid for them by silencing everything that could have been predicted.

Host: Say the economy of it plainly, because I think that is the sentence our listeners should carry out of this episode.

Guide: Here it is in three rules. Bandwidth is precious: a million and a half lines is all the optic nerve will ever have, so no line is wasted re-reporting what is predictable. Redundancy is dropped: neighbours agree, and agreement is subtracted before the signal leaves the retina. Surprises are amplified: the unexpected difference, and only the unexpected difference, earns spikes. One hundred million sensors in, a million and a half cables out, every cable carrying edited, addressed, prioritised news. That is the shipping list. The brain does not receive the world. It receives a dispatch about the world, written in the only language a nerve can speak.

Host: You keep saying that language is spikes, their rate and timing. Spell it out once, because it is easy to hear and hard to picture.

Guide: A spike is a brief electrical pulse, and here is the remarkable part: every spike a given nerve fibre ever fires is identical, the same shape whether the world is noon or midnight. Since the pulses themselves do not vary, the information must live in their pattern: how many arrive per second, and when. A ganglion cell at rest ticks over modestly; a strong brightening in its territory may multiply that rate; a strong darkening may silence it. A million and a half fibres do this simultaneously, and the brain reads the chorus, comparing rates and timings across the whole bundle. That is the entire vocabulary: one word, the spike, spoken at varying volume and moment. It sounds impoverished, and it is anything but, because the choice of what the word is about has been made so carefully upstream.

Host: Let me tie this to where our story properly began, because Series 1 promised this moment and I want the promise honoured.

Guide: It is honoured tonight. Back in Series 1, episode ten of season two, Following the Path of Vision, we walked the route in outline: light enters, the retina catches it, the optic nerve carries it, the brain reads it. That walk was a map with the engine rooms unopened. This season opened every engine room along the retinal stretch of the route, and tonight we stood at the last door and watched the consignment sealed: contrast-coded, change-sensitive, split into parallel channels, location-tagged, compressed. And notice whose handiwork the parcel is: the ganglion cells of episode nine, whose axons are the nerve itself. What Series 1 called the path of vision is now, for us, a working pipeline with named machinery at every stage.

Host: Now bring it to the chair, because our listeners test eyes for a living. How does the shipping list change what an optometrist notices in ordinary testing?

Guide: In the most practical way imaginable: every classic test is a conversation with one of tonight's channels. Read a letter chart and you are interrogating the foveal allocation, the lavish centre of the location-tagged map, and you are asking the detail channel to report fine contrast, which is why acuity is as much a contrast task as a spacing task. Run a contrast sensitivity chart and you are testing the difference-encoder directly, the first transformation, the one that turns brightness into comparison. Watch a patient notice a flicker at the corner of vision while the shape there stays vague, and you are seeing the motion channel, wide territories and brisk reporting, doing exactly what it was hired to do, which is detection without detail; it is also why something glimpsed in the periphery is easier to see than to name. And a perimetry target is literally an addressed letter, a spot of light placed at one location, asking one small territory whether it can still see and reply. The tests look the way they look because the retina codes the way it codes. That is chairside physiology in a single sentence.

Host: Before the farewell formalities, the misconception sweep. First suspect: the retina sends a picture to the brain.

Guide: Guilty as charged, and tonight is the correction. The retina sends a coded stream: differences, changes, parallel channels, addresses. No image travels in the nerve. The impression of an image is assembled later, from the dispatch, and that assembly is next season's opening story, so we will not pre-empt it tonight.

Host: Second suspect: brighter objects simply make the cells fire faster.

Guide: Half guilty, which is the dangerous kind. A cell in the ON channel does fire faster when its territory brightens, but a cell in the OFF channel fires faster when the same patch darkens, and both are reporting relative to surround and recent past rather than to any absolute scale. The same grey patch can raise firing in one cable and lower it in its neighbour, depending on context. What firing rate reports is the difference and the direction of contrast, never raw brightness.

Host: Third suspect: the optic nerve is a passive cable, a simple wire from eye to brain.

Guide: Not guilty, emphatically. By the time spikes enter the nerve, the heaviest editing has already happened: adaptation has rescaled sensitivity, inhibition has subtracted the predictable, channels have been split, priorities assigned. The nerve is the exit of a newsroom, not a raw feed. The nerve even carries proof of the editing inside its own footprint: it leaves the eye at the optic disc, where there are no receptors at all, and nothing in the dispatch announces the gap. The completion of that gap is downstream work, and it belongs, with everything else the brain adds, to the season we are about to meet.

Host: Recap time, in our usual oral-examination style. Take me through the five transformations once more, slowly, the way a student should say them back.

Guide: With pleasure. One: brightness becomes contrast. The retina reports how a patch differs from its surround and from its own recent past, never the absolute level, which is why the same cables serve us from starlight to sunlight. Two: steady scenes become change reports. Output cells answer onsets, offsets, movement and flicker, and let the unchanging fade, because news, not status, is worth bandwidth. Three: one huge image becomes parallel channels. ON and OFF, detail and motion, red against green and blue against yellow, several edited streams leaving one small nerve at once. Four: light becomes location-tagged spikes. Every ganglion cell owns a territory, every spike carries an address, and the map rides intact toward the cortex, lavish at the centre, summarised at the edges. Five: enormous raw data becomes a compressed, prioritised stream. About one hundred million sensors into roughly a million and a half cables, redundancy subtracted, surprises amplified, the fovea first. Over all five stands one economy: bandwidth is precious, redundancy is dropped, surprises are amplified. That is the shipping list of the human retina.

Guide: Gladly, and what a structure it turns out to be. Episode one, Why the Retina Is a Neural Tissue, moved the retina out of the camera and into the brain: a sheet of nervous tissue, three layers of neurons, doing genuine computation at the back of the eye. Put the nine together and tonight's title writes itself: a living compression and editing studio, taking in light and sending out sense.
