# Description: Deep inside the retina, between the photoreceptors and the optic nerve, sits an editing workroom where raw light signals are sorted, compared and sharpened before they leave the eye. This episode of Focus Cast by focuslinks walks through the retinal circuits that perform that editing: bipolar cells as the main bridge, horizontal cells as the sideways negotiators of the outer retina, and amacrine cells as the modulators of the inner retina. Hear the elegant ON and OFF story, in which photoreceptors dim their glutamate in light and two families of bipolar cells read that dimming in opposite ways, creating two parallel streams from one neurotransmitter. Follow the rod pathway, the detour by which the whispers of night vision join the cone bipolar system through a dedicated amacrine relay. Made for optometry students and practising optometrists who want retinal physiology explained clearly for the ear.

Host: Every camera you have ever used hides one quiet truth from you: the picture it hands you has already been edited. Light strikes the sensor, and before anything leaves the box, circuits have sharpened the edges, hushed the background and sorted bright from dark. Your retina does exactly the same, except it does the editing with living cells, before the brain ever hears a word. Welcome to Focus Cast by focuslinks, and welcome back to Series 3, Ocular Physiology. We are deep in Season 6, Retina Physiology, and today is episode eight, Retinal Signal Processing. Episode five, Phototransduction, showed us how a photoreceptor converts light into a raw electrical whisper. Episodes six and seven, Dark Adaptation and Light Adaptation, tuned that whisper for night and for day. Now we walk into the retina's inner workroom, where those whispers are sorted, compared, inverted and relayed before they leave the eye. Our patient guide has walked this season with us from the first photoreceptor. Open the door for us. What kind of room is this workroom?

Guide: Think of it as a newsroom sitting between the reporters and the printing press. The reporters are the photoreceptors at the back wall, and they file only raw impressions of light. Everything an observer would call seeing happens after the editors have been at work, and the editors are today's subject. The retina is a piece of the brain parked inside the eye, which is exactly what episode one of this season argued, and which Series 1 greeted long ago in The Retina as the Sensory Layer. Today we meet the working staff. There are three main families of editor, stacked in the middle of the retina's thickness, between the photoreceptors and the cells that carry the final message out. The bipolar cells are the bridge, the through road from photoreceptors to the output. The horizontal cells are the sideways negotiators of the outer half, forever comparing one neighbourhood with the next. The amacrine cells are the modulators of the inner half, adjusting timing, gating traffic, adding emphasis. Learn those three names well, because they are the cast of everything that follows.

Host: You said stacked, and that word deserves grounding before we meet the staff one by one. Where exactly does this editing sit in the retinal shelves, and what did our anatomy series call them?

Guide: Turn back to Series 2, episodes six and seven of season seven, Retina: Anatomical Overview and Retinal Layers, and you will find the shelving already drawn. The retina stacks the cell bodies of its neurons in three layers, and between them sit two layers made almost entirely of connections, which anatomists call plexiform layers, from words meaning a braid or a network. The outer plexiform layer is where photoreceptor terminals meet the dendrites of bipolar cells and the branches of horizontal cells. That is the front office, where raw light reports are first handled. The inner plexiform layer is where bipolar cell axons meet ganglion cell dendrites and amacrine cell branches. That is the back office, where edited copy is prepared for dispatch. And notice the direction of travel. Light passes through nearly the whole thickness before it is caught at the back, but the electrical story flows forward, from photoreceptors to bipolars to ganglion cells, gathering edits at both plexiform layers on the way out.

Host: Let us take the bridge first, then. Why are the bipolar cells the main crossing, and what makes them different from every other editor in the room?

Guide: Because every message that leaves the retina is built on a bipolar cell's copy of a photoreceptor's report. The ganglion cells, the output neurons, take their direct input from bipolar cells and amacrine cells, and the bipolar cell is the only class that carries the photoreceptor's graded signal forward in its original form, unpolluted by spikes, from the outer shelf to the inner one. A bipolar cell gathers contacts onto one or many photoreceptor terminals in the outer plexiform layer, then sends a single long process inward to deliver its edited summary in the inner plexiform layer. At the photoreceptor terminal it reads from a ribbon synapse, a beautifully engineered release site that pours messenger continuously, so the bipolar cell can read the rate of the pour itself. And there are many kinds of listener. Mammalian retinas hold at least a dozen bipolar types. Some are party lines, pooling from many cones at once. Some, in the fovea, are private lines, taking one cone each. Same bridge, many lanes.

Host: You slipped in a phrase that deserves its own minute. You said unpolluted by spikes. The bipolar cells do not fire impulses?

Guide: They do not, and neither do the photoreceptors, and neither do the horizontal cells. The entire front half of the retina speaks in graded voltages. A neuron classically signals with an action potential, a spike, an all or nothing pulse, but a graded cell simply slides its membrane voltage up or down, like a dimmer, in proportion to the message. That is perfect for this job, because the retina must carry fine gradations, and a one per cent change in brightness must mean something. A dimmer carries such subtleties easily where a switch would crush them. It is also quiet and cheap over the short distances inside the retina. The spikes begin only where the message must travel a long distance, at the ganglion cells, whose axons bundle into the optic nerve. That is episode nine's territory, and I will hold it there. For today, hold this: the whole editing room whispers in grades, and the shouting is reserved for the exit.

Host: So the bridge carries whispers inward. Now the sideways negotiators. What exactly are the horizontal cells negotiating in the outer half?

Guide: They are negotiating the price of contrast. Horizontal cells sit in the outer plexiform layer, right beside the bipolar dendrites, and their branches spread sideways across a wide patch of retina. Neighbouring horizontal cells are joined by gap junctions, direct cytoplasmic doorways, so their influence pools across an even wider neighbourhood, and along the way they collect from many photoreceptor terminals. Then they answer back, both ways at once: they feed messages forward onto the bipolar dendrites, and they feed messages back onto the photoreceptor terminals themselves. The question they answer for a bipolar cell is not how much light is falling here, but how much light is falling here compared with the average of the surroundings. A bipolar cell therefore learns about differences. Uniform light washes through and leaves the comparison flat. A spot of brightness against a darker surround, or a shadow against light, throws the comparison wide. That comparison is the seed of the retina's most famous computation, and I will be disciplined about the fence: the full centre versus surround geometry belongs to next season's episode three, Centre-Surround Processing. Today we stay at the circuit level: sideways pooling, feedback, and a signal that reports differences rather than absolutes.

Host: Differences rather than absolutes. Say that once more as the thesis of the whole episode, because I suspect it is the hinge everything else turns on.

Guide: It is the hinge. The retina is stingy with redundancy and generous with news. A level of light that sits still is discounted, because it tells you nothing new; a change in that level, an edge, a motion, a flicker, is amplified and forwarded. Three great edits run through tonight's circuits, and you can keep them as the episode's spine. The first edit is the splitting of every signal into parallel bright and dark streams, the ON and OFF pathways. The second is the rod detour, the route by which the sensitive night cells borrow the daylight wiring. The third is the timing and gating done by amacrine cells, which keep the retina reporting change rather than steadiness. And notice what the editing buys. Roughly a hundred million photoreceptors funnel outward through about a million fibres in the optic nerve. That compression is not loss. It is the workroom's whole point: keep what matters, and let what does not dissolve on the way.

Host: Then let us meet the third family properly. The amacrine cells, the modulators of the inner half. Who are they, and what do they modulate?

Guide: They are the most various family in the retina and the least easy to summarise, which is itself the summary. Amacrine cells branch within the inner plexiform layer, side by side with bipolar axons and ganglion dendrites, and they talk sideways there the way horizontal cells talk sideways in the outer layer. Most of them are inhibitory, using glycine or GABA, and their jobs fall into a few great themes. Some gate signals, letting traffic pass only when conditions warrant. Some shape timing, converting a lingering response into a brief transient, so the retina keeps noticing change instead of settling into it. Some specialise extraordinarily, and one famous type, the starburst amacrine, is central to how the retina senses the direction a thing moves, a story we will fence to episode ten, Retinal Information Coding. And one type releases dopamine instead of glycine, and acts like a broadcast system, a slow global memo that helps reconfigure the whole retina when illumination changes. That memo is part of the light adaptation machinery, which is why episode seven, Light Adaptation, is its natural home.

Host: A gate, a timer, a broadcast desk. And I am told the inner plexiform layer is not a tangle but a filing system. Give us the two decks.

Guide: The order is astonishing. When bipolar cell axons deliver their messages in the inner plexiform layer, they sort themselves by identity. The OFF bipolar cells, the family we will define in a moment, terminate in the upper deck of the layer. The ON bipolar cells terminate in the lower deck. Ganglion cells then reach their branches into whichever deck matches their own nature: an OFF ganglion cell reads the upper deck, an ON ganglion cell reads the lower. And within each deck the channels stay tidy, the private midget lanes running in parallel with the party line lanes, shelf upon shelf, without mixing. So the inner half of the retina is a two deck switchboard, and the decks are not decoration. They are the physical guarantee that bright news and dark news travel in separate wires all the way to the exit.

Host: Which brings us to the story I have been waiting for all evening. How can one messenger mean opposite things to two different listeners? Take us to the scene.

Guide: First you must accept the strangest fact in retinal physiology, and episode five of this season earned it: the photoreceptor is most talkative in the dark. Phototransduction taught us that light hyperpolarises the photoreceptor, closing its channels and quieting it, while darkness leaves it depolarised and steadily pouring the excitatory messenger, glutamate, into the outer plexiform layer. So the glutamate tap is wide open at night and throttled back in the light. Now invert everything. To a naive listener, glutamate would simply mean darkness. But the retina contains two families of bipolar listeners, and they read the same chemical in opposite ways. The puzzle that occupied physiologists for years was precisely this: if the messenger goes quiet when light arrives, how does the retina ever report light? The answer is one of the elegant tricks in the nervous system, and it was found by pushing microelectrodes inside single retinal cells.

Host: Then walk us through the trick itself, slowly, the way you would want a student to repeat it back. What do the two families of listeners actually do?

Guide: The OFF bipolar cell is a direct reader. Its glutamate receptors sit in the membrane, catch the messenger, and excite the cell. So in darkness, glutamate pours and the OFF bipolar is held active. When light arrives, the pour dims and the OFF bipolar falls quiet. It is called OFF because light switches it off; its activity faithfully tracks darkness. The ON bipolar cell is an indirect reader. Its glutamate receptor is metabotropic, meaning it works through a chemical cascade inside the cell, and in the dark that cascade holds the cell's channels shut, keeping it quiet. When light arrives and the glutamate pour dims, the cascade relaxes, the channels open, and the ON bipolar depolarises. Light turns it on. So one neurotransmitter, at one synapse, produces two opposite readings, and physiologists call the first connection sign conserving, glutamate excites, and the second sign inverting, glutamate inhibits. In daylight the ON stream rises and the OFF stream falls. In a shadow, the reverse. Two parallel streams, born from one messenger, giving the brain simultaneous maps of light and dark.

Host: Two parallel streams. Defend the design for me. Why would evolution run bright and dark on separate wires instead of one wire that simply reports the level?

Guide: Because edges live in the pairing. Consider what the visual world actually does: it turns lights on, and it casts shadows. A lamp clicking on is news of one kind, best reported by a channel whose activity rises with light. A shadow sliding across the desk is news of another kind, best reported by a channel whose activity rises with darkness. By running both streams at once, the brain receives two simultaneous maps, a bright map and a dark map, and every boundary in the scene lands sharply in at least one of them. Nothing is dulled by averaging the two, and no edge waits its turn behind a single wire. The symmetry also doubles the retina's vigilance: any flicker, brightening or dimming, is guaranteed to lift one stream or lower the other, and the comparison downstream reads it instantly. And thanks to the two decks we described, the separation is preserved through the inner shelf, so bright and dark arrive at the exit as cleanly labelled cargoes.

Host: You said the trick was found with microelectrodes inside single cells. Honour the discovery properly. What did those early recordings actually see?

Guide: Through the nineteen fifties and sixties, physiologists drew fine glass capillaries down to a tip finer than a cell and slid them into individual retinal neurons, first in fish retinas and then in amphibian ones, one cell at a time. What they saw overturned the tidy assumption that the retina simply forwards the photoreceptor's story. Some cells responded to light with slow graded shifts rather than spikes. Then came the divide that names our pathways: when a small spot of light landed on the retina, some cells surged upward while others plunged downward, and the same pattern held all the way to the ganglion cells, which came in matching ON and OFF varieties. By the end of the nineteen sixties, recordings from bipolar cells themselves showed the two families reading the same synapse in opposite ways, and the vocabulary of sign conserving and sign inverting connections entered physiology. No single afternoon made the discovery. It was a ladder of small recordings, each one climbing onto the last.

Host: Now the detour. The rods. Do the night cells use this same bridge, or do they have their own exit from the retina?

Guide: In one clean sentence: rods hand their whispers to a dedicated rod bipolar cell, which hands them to a specialist amacrine relay, which passes them into the cone bipolar system, whose axons deliver the message to the ganglion cells. Unpack it and the design glows. The rod bipolar is an ON cell, reading the same glutamate dimming trick we just learned. But rod bipolar axons do not reach ganglion cells directly. They terminate on a particular amacrine cell, the great relay of the rod pathway, which anatomists label A two. That relay cell is a junction box with two exits. Through gap junctions it passes the rod signal electrically into the ON cone bipolar cells, and through inhibitory chemical synapses it hands the same news to the OFF cone bipolar channels, with the signs arranged so that both streams still read the rod's message correctly. Either way, the rod whisper joins the cone road for its final miles, and exits on the same two decks that daylight traffic uses.

Host: A detour with a purpose, surely. Why do rods borrow the cone network instead of running their own highway to the brain?

Guide: Three reasons, and episode three of this season, Rod Physiology, set up the first. Rods already pool massively; many rods feed one rod bipolar, and many rod bipolars feed one relay cell, which is the arithmetic of sensitivity, many whispers summed into one audible voice. Given that pooling, a separate rod highway through to the brain would buy nothing and cost a duplicate cable. Second, the detour keeps the machinery economical: the retina maintains one set of exit lanes, and the rods simply ride them at night. Third, and most subtle, the interchange is switchable. The gap junctions along the rod route are under control, and that broadcast dopamine memo we mentioned helps retune the coupling as illumination changes, so in bright light the rod pathway quietens and cone traffic owns the road, while in darkness the rod whispers ride the cone lanes outward. Episode six, Dark Adaptation, told the sensitivity half of that switch; today you have its circuit diagram.

Host: Let us return to the private lines, because acuity is the optometrist's daily bread. How do these circuits explain why the fovea sees so much finer than the periphery?

Guide: This is the bridge's crowning arrangement. In the fovea the wiring is nearly one to one: a single cone passes to its own midget bipolar cell, and that bipolar passes to its own midget ganglion cell. Because both streams exist, there are two of these private circuits for every foveal cone, one ON and one OFF. A private line keeps the address label intact; whatever that one cone knows, the brain knows came from exactly that point in space, and fine detail survives the relay. Out in the periphery the arithmetic reverses: diffuse bipolar cells collect from many cones, ganglion cells collect from many bipolars, and hundreds of photoreceptors may share a single fibre in the optic nerve. That is a party line, superb for catching faint signals and motion in the corner of vision, hopeless for reading print. Where the private lines and party lines sit, and how the photoreceptor census runs across the retina, we mapped in Series 2, episode four of season eight, Photoreceptor Distribution. Tonight's circuits are why that map has consequences.

Host: Consequences at the chair, then. Bring the whole workroom into the consulting room. What does an optometrist see on an ordinary Tuesday that traces back to tonight's circuits?

Guide: More than students expect, and all of it normal physiology. When you ask a patient to read a dark letter on a bright chart, you are testing both streams at once, and the fact that a dark target on light ground is as legible as a light target on dark ground is the ON and OFF symmetry working for you. When you explain why night driving blurs fine print while the sense of movement survives, you are describing rod pooling riding the amacrine interchange. The electroretinogram puts the workroom on paper: the a wave reflects the photoreceptors, and the b wave, the large positive swing that follows, is largely the collective voice of the bipolar cells, so a recording that keeps its a wave and loses its b wave is a story about the bridge, not the reporters. And every automated perimetry grid is, at bottom, sampling the outputs of ganglion cells whose receptive fields were assembled from tonight's decks. That last connection belongs to episode nine, and I will hold it there.

Host: Hold it there indeed. But before we close the file, let us sweep out the misconceptions, the way we always do. What do students get wrong about this workroom?

Guide: Four, and each is worth catching early. First, the belief that photoreceptors talk to the brain directly. They do not; every leaving message is built through bipolar cells, with amacrine editors along the way. Second, the belief that light means ON for every cell in the retina. The opposite is half the truth: light quiets the messenger, and an entire family of listeners turns off when light arrives, because darkness is the photoreceptor's busiest hour. Third, the belief that the retina relays a picture the way a cable relays a signal. It edits before it sends: it splits bright from dark, compares each point with its neighbourhood, and times its reports to emphasise change. Fourth, the belief that rods and cones run separate roads to the exit. They do not; rods join the cone lanes at the amacrine interchange, and the two systems share the final wiring. Correct those four and the whole architecture falls into place.

Host: Then let us hear the architecture said back plainly, the way you would want it in an oral examination. The cast, the ground rules, the trick, the detour, in order.

Guide: Gladly. The cast: bipolar cells are the bridge from photoreceptors onward; horizontal cells are the sideways negotiators of the outer half; amacrine cells are the modulators of the inner half. The ground rules: photoreceptors, bipolars and horizontals speak in graded voltages, never spikes; spikes belong to the ganglion cells at the exit. The trick: photoreceptors pour glutamate in the dark and throttle it in light; OFF bipolar cells read glutamate directly and track darkness; ON bipolar cells read the dimming through a metabotropic cascade and track brightness; two parallel streams from one messenger, delivered on two separate decks of the inner plexiform layer. The detour: rods converge onto rod bipolar cells, relay through a specialised amacrine, and join the cone bipolar system for the exit, switchable with illumination. The refinement: in the fovea, private lines, one cone to one midget bipolar to one midget ganglion, twice over; in the periphery, party lines. That is the workroom.

Host: One last question before we open the next door. In all of this, which detail do you find students most often underestimate?

Guide: The busiest hour is the dark. Students picture the retina idling at night, since nothing much seems to be happening. In fact the darkness default means the photoreceptors are maximally depolarised, the glutamate taps are wide open, the OFF bipolar family is held active, and more messenger is spent per minute with the lights off than with them on. Light, the stimulus we instinctively treat as the active ingredient, is for the front of the retina a brake. That inversion is not a curiosity; it is the reason the ON pathway must exist, and it quietly explains why the retina's energy bill barely rests at night. When a student finally feels how much of this system is organised around reporting the absence of glutamate, the whole design snaps into focus. Absence is a message. The retina is built to say so.
