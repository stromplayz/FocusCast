What exactly is an output neuron, and why does the whole retina hang on this one family of cells?

Guide: Thank you, and let me draw tonight's boundary first, because it keeps the season tidy. Season 6 has taught the retina as three storeys doing one job. The ground floor catches light: the photoreceptors, rods and cones, and their chemistry of phototransduction. The middle floors edit: the horizontal, bipolar and amacrine cells whose wiring we walked in episode eight, Retinal Signal Processing. And the top floor decides what leaves: the ganglion cells. Here is the definition that unlocks the episode. A retinal ganglion cell is the only neuron in the retina whose axon actually leaves the eye. Its dendrites collect the finished, edited signals from the cells below. Its axon carries them out of the globe and off toward the brain. Every other retinal cell computes locally and hands its result across a synapse to a neighbour. The ganglion cell alone converts all of that local work into long-distance traffic. Remember, back in episode one of this season, we said the retina is a piece of brain that migrated to the wall of the eye. The ganglion cells are the part that stayed on duty as the embassy's courier service. Tonight we count them, watch one fire, map the territory each one owns, and meet the three work families that divide the labour. One preview thought before we begin: because these axons are the only ones that leave, the optic nerve you will study for years is, cell for cell, nothing but ganglion cells. Learn this one family and you have learned the nerve.

Host: Then let us start with numbers, the way we always do. How many of these couriers does one human retina employ? And I want the honest arithmetic against the photoreceptor count, because if I remember the census from Series 2, we are talking about a hundred million receptors feeding a much smaller exchange.

Guide: The census, in words. A human retina holds roughly one and a half million ganglion cells, and the honest footnote is that careful counting studies have landed anywhere from about seven hundred thousand to one point seven million depending on the method, so textbooks round differently. Hold the million and a half as the working figure. Each ganglion cell owns exactly one axon, every axon runs along the retina's inner surface, converges at the optic disc, and leaves the eye inside the optic nerve, whose classic fibre count is about one point two million. When Series 2, episode one of season ten, Optic Nerve, walked that cable fibre by fibre, that was its headline: about one point two million axons gathered from the ganglion cells, one cable per reporter, with the counting methods differing just enough that the two tallies never quite agree. Now set that against the other census, the one from Series 2, episode four of season eight, Photoreceptor Distribution: about one hundred and twenty million rods and about six million cones. More than a hundred million receptors funnel into a little over a million output cables. Across the retina as a whole, that is roughly one hundred photoreceptors for every output fibre. That funnel is the retina's great compression. A hundred million cameras condense into a million reporters. And as Series 2 argued, the compression is not careless shrinking, it is curation, and the budget is spent where vision is spent: at the fovea the wiring flips toward one cone to one midget bipolar cell to one midget ganglion cell, a private line with almost no sharing. Why compress at all? Because the cable is the bottleneck the whole design must live within: the nerve has to fit through a hole a couple of millimetres wide, and the brain could never feed a thousand times more incoming fibres. The retina is forced to choose what to keep, and a forced choice made well is exactly what editing means.

Host: A million private and shared lines, all leaving through one small doorway. Walk me through that exit. Where do the axons actually travel inside the eye, and how does a million-fibre crowd get out through one disc without tearing the receptor sheet behind it?

Guide: Picture the innermost film of the retina, the side that faces the vitreous. Lying over the whole receptor mosaic is the nerve fibre layer, a thin transparent sheet made of nothing but ganglion cell axons, all walking in ordered bundles toward one address. Axons arc around the fovea, above and below, nasal and temporal, the way roads from every suburb converge on one ring road. At the disc they turn through a right angle and plunge out through the sieve-like plate, and the nerve is on its way. The ordering is dignified: fibres from the outer retina run nearest the vitreous and must walk the longest arc around the fovea, fibres born near the disc take the short road, and the bundle from the macula heads straight for the door, the papillomacular bundle. Nothing crosses the receptor sheet on its way out. Series 2, episode ten of season eight, Retina-Optic Nerve Relationship, owns that map of bundles, and episode seven of the same season, Optic Nerve Head, owns the doorway itself, so I will only borrow one consequence tonight. The disc holds no photoreceptors at all, which is why every eye carries a normal blind spot, a small island of nothing that you have owned since birth and never noticed. And notice how far back this room reaches. Series 1, episode ten of season two, Following the Path of Vision, followed the visual message from its birth in the receptors toward the brain, and that message always had to pass through here. Tonight we are standing on the platform where the train finally departs.

Host: Good. Now the physiology itself, and I want it plainly. A ganglion cell receives an edited, graded signal from the cells below. What does it actually send up the cable? What is in the wire?

Guide: Pulses. This is the retina's last technical conversion, and it matters. Photoreceptors and bipolar cells speak in graded voltages: their signal is an analogue shift, bigger or smaller depending on the light, and that works beautifully across the few micrometres of a synapse or a short cell. But a graded voltage fades with distance, like a shout across a field. A ganglion cell's axon has a long journey, out of the eye, along the floor of the skull, deep into the brain, so it travels on the standard neural currency: the action potential, a full nerve impulse of fixed size that is regenerated all along the fibre and therefore never fades. A pulse is refreshed at every stretch of the cable, so a spike arriving at the brain is exactly as tall as the spike that left the cell, and it arrives within a few thousandths of a second of being sent. Digital traffic does not care about distance. Graded traffic cannot afford it. Once the cell fires, the message becomes a rhythm. Information rides in the rate of firing, and here is the detail students underestimate: a ganglion cell is never silent. Even in steady background light it ticks away at its own resting discharge, a spontaneous baseline. Light does not usually switch a cell on from nothing; it modulates the rhythm, pushing the rate up or pulling it down. Brightness arrives as a faster or slower drumbeat, change arrives as a change of drumbeat, and the brain downstream reads the deviations against that personal baseline. The retina does not send photographs down the nerve. It sends a million steady rhythms, and adjusts them.

Host: And that brings us to the idea that gives tonight its central concept, the receptive field. The phrase sounds dry, almost clerical. What does it actually mean for one ganglion cell to own a piece of the visual scene, and how would anyone go about proving it?

Guide: It is simpler than it sounds, and it is one of the most beautiful experiments in sensory physiology. Take one ganglion cell, record from its single axon, and listen to its rhythm while you touch tiny spots of light to different places on the retina. You will find that the cell answers only within a small territory, a handful of spots of the mosaic wide, and ignores everything outside it. That territory, the region of the visual scene within which light changes this cell's firing, is its receptive field. One small plot of the world is now that cell's entire responsibility. Map the territory cell by cell and a rule appears: fields are tiny at the fovea, where private lines rule, and widen steadily toward the periphery, where pooling rules, so the plot a cell owns doubles as a map of the retina's priorities. Now comes the twist that makes it brilliant. Within that plot, position decides the direction of the answer. Flash a spot in the middle of the field and the rhythm quickens: the centre of this field is excitatory, it turns the cell on. Then shine a ring of light that covers the outer part of the field but spares the centre, and the rhythm slows: the ring is inhibitory, it argues with the cell. Such a cell is called an ON-centre, OFF-surround ganglion cell, and its mirror twin exists in equal numbers, the OFF-centre, ON-surround cell, which is shut down by centre light and released by ring light. Two families of opposite report, roughly equal in the population, covering every plot of the retina in matched pairs. And the wiring behind it is exactly what episode eight taught: the centre is built from bipolar cells, whose ON or OFF sign was settled at the first synapse, and the ring is manufactured laterally, by amacrine cells pooling sideways across the neighbourhood. This is the first full sketch of the centre-surround story, and it is the retina's standard output template.

Host: So the ring exists to disagree. But why would the retina spend precious wiring on a structure whose whole job is argument? What does the antagonism actually buy?

Guide: It buys the ability to report differences, and that is the deepest habit of this retina. Follow the arithmetic. When light floods the whole field evenly, centre and ring together, the excitation and the inhibition arrive balanced, and the cell settles close to its baseline: uniform surfaces barely move it. Now let an edge cross the field, or a spot, or any boundary, so that centre and ring receive different lights, and the balance breaks: the cell fires well above or well below baseline. The cell, in other words, is close to indifferent to how much light there is, and exquisitely sensitive to how the light is distributed. It whispers about averages and shouts about differences. The baseline earns its keep here too. Because the cell rests at a middle rate rather than at zero, it can report in either direction: a quickening for one kind of light, a slowing for the opposite kind, both meaningful from the first moment. A cell that started from silence could only ever say more; a cell that starts from a resting rate can say more and less. Uniform illumination, the kind that drifts slowly through the day, is largely cancelled at the output; pattern, contour and contrast, the things that carry information, sail through. This is why a million and a half cells can carry a whole visual world: most of them are not busy describing raw brightness, they are describing where things change. And I want to be disciplined about the fence here. The perceptual payoff of this design, why edges look the way they look, why some shadows seem darker than physics allows, belongs to Season 7, episode three, Centre-Surround Processing, where we teach what this wiring does to experience. Tonight the cell fires. Next season we ask what you see because of it.

Host: Fields, centres, rings, two mirror families. But we keep saying ganglion cells as if they were one profession. Are all these plots the same size, and do all these cells keep the same working hours?

Guide: No, and the differences are the beginning of parallel vision. When Series 2, episode one of season ten, Optic Nerve, catalogued the families by anatomy, it sorted them by body size and dendritic tree. Tonight we sort them by temperament, by how they behave, and the two sortings match perfectly. The first family is the midget cell, and it is the giant of the population: roughly four fifths of all ganglion cells are midgets. They have small bodies, tiny dendritic trees, and therefore the smallest receptive fields, and they crowd the fovea. Remember the private line: one cone, one midget bipolar cell, one midget ganglion cell, almost no pooling. A small field, one report. Their physiology is sustained: shine a light into the centre of a midget field and the cell raises its rate and holds it, steadily, for as long as the light lasts. Their axons are thin and their conduction is slow by nervous standards, a fair trade, because a detail report wants stability more than speed. They are the retina's tonal instruments, and they carry two great cargoes. The first is fine detail, because a small field means a fine address. The second is colour comparison, the red against green bookkeeping, because their inputs keep the cone types apart. When a patient reads the smallest line on the chart, that achievement is a midget performance from first to last.

Host: Hold that word sustained for a moment, because it is doing real work. And then tell me about the opposite temperament. Who handles the fast lane of this exchange?

Guide: Sustained means the response lasts as long as the stimulus. The midget cell is a holder: it locks its rate to the light and keeps reporting until the light changes, which is exactly what reading demands, a stable signal for as long as the gaze rests on a word. The second family is its opposite in almost every respect. The parasol cell, named for its dendritic tree, which spreads wide and flat like an umbrella, gathers from a large territory: its receptive fields are big, pooling from many bipolar cells and therefore many receptors, and it makes up about one tenth of the population. Its physiology is transient. Flash a light into a parasol field and the cell answers in a brisk burst at the onset and another at the offset, then falls quiet while the light merely continues. It is a change detector. It reports that something happened, not that something is. Its axons are among the thickest and fastest in the nerve, which suits the cargo: motion, flicker, timing, the when of the visual world, the stream a fielder trusts to judge a flying ball and a driver trusts to notice a car pulling out. Big fields also mean a coarse address but great sensitivity, and that is why a moving leaf at the edge of vision, far outside any readable detail, still yanks your attention back. The periphery is parasol country.

Host: Midgets at four fifths, parasols at one tenth. That leaves a small remainder. Is the third family just a junior version of one of these two, or is it something else again?

Guide: Something else again, and it completes the colour story. The third family is the small bistratified cell, a much smaller population, named because its dendrites branch in two separate tiers of the inner retina, receiving from different bipolar signs at different depths. Its cargo is the blue against yellow comparison. Where the midget system keeps red and green apart, this family compares the short-wavelength cones against the combined long and middle wavelength story, and that difference is what reads to you as blue against yellow. The wiring is sparser and the fields larger than the foveal midgets, which is one reason the blue-yellow sense is the coarser of the two colour comparisons, but it runs its own parallel cable through the nerve like the others. And for honesty's sake, a footnote: beyond these three workhorses live a handful of specialist sets, including a tiny family of cells that contain their own light-sensitive pigment and act as the retina's built-in light meters, helping time the pupil and the body's daily clock. That story keeps its own appointment in the pupillary episodes, so tonight we name it and move on.

Host: Three families, three cargoes, one cable. Put the whole nerve together for me. When I imagine the optic nerve as a single object, what is actually travelling through it at this moment, in every seeing eye on earth?

Guide: Traffic. Several edited streams at once. The optic nerve is not one wire carrying one picture, and it is nothing like a camera cable. It is about a million cables in a shared sheath, and at this moment, in any sighted eye, they are filing separate reports simultaneously. The midget stream sustains the addresses and the red-green colours of whatever the gaze rests on. The parasol stream times every motion and flicker across the wide field. The small bistratified stream holds its blue-yellow comparison steady. Each report is built from receptive fields, each field is a centre arguing with a ring, and each rate is modulated above or below its personal baseline. Here is the frame to carry out of tonight: the retina ships not raw pixels but a curated, parallel-edited summary. The brain never receives the scene itself; it receives a million expert reports about the scene, already sorted into detail, motion, colour and change, already weighed, already compressed with intent. Seeing, in that sense, is edited before it is experienced. And the editing keeps its order: each stream leaves the eye with its own thickness, its own speed and its own question, and each keeps its separate appointment at the relay stations of the brain, a journey Series 2 opened and later seasons of physiology will finish. And that shipping metaphor is a promise I intend to keep: episode ten, our season finale, reads the shipping list.

Host: Let me bring this to the chair, but staying strictly inside our lane of normal function. On an ordinary Tuesday, with a healthy patient in front of me, where should tonight's cells show up in what I see and measure?

Guide: In four ordinary places. First, the acuity chart. The smallest letters a patient resolves are a midget achievement, small fields at the fovea riding private lines, which is why acuity falls away so steeply outside the centre, where the fields widen and the lines are shared. Second, the limits of that same chart. A letter chart asks only the sustained, detail question, and it says nothing about the parasol stream, which is a separate cable; reading the chart well and catching motion well are different performances from different populations. Third, everyday attention. A cyclist entering the edge of your view, a hand waved at the side of your vision, the flicker of a screen in the corner of the room: those are parasol reports from parasol country, big pooled fields with fast axons doing exactly the job their wiring bought. Detail lives where the midgets live, at the centre; motion lives everywhere, and especially where the parasols live, in the wide field. Fourth, the fundus view itself. The glistening streaks arcing across your view toward the disc, the ones students sometimes mistake for artefacts, are the nerve fibre layer itself, ganglion cell axons on the final stretch of their walk to the door. You are looking at tonight's population every single time you look in.

Host: Before we close, give me the misconception sweep. What are the four things students most often get wrong about the retina's output?

Guide: Four, and each one is a door back into the episode. First, the eye sends a picture. It does not. It sends about a million pulse trains, none of which is a pixel, all of which are edited reports; the picture is assembled far downstream, and even then from summaries rather than photographs. Second, ganglion cells are light sensors. Almost none of them catch light at all; they catch finished signals from bipolar and amacrine cells, and their entire skill is reading what the retina below has decided. The handful with their own pigment are the exception we already fenced. Third, ON and OFF are one cell in two moods. They are not. They are two mirror families, wired from the first synapse to opposite signs, running side by side in matched fields, because the retina wants both the news that light arrived and the news that light left; missing a departure would be as misleading as missing an arrival. Fourth, compression wastes detail. It cannot afford to, and it does not. Pooling buys sensitivity where light is scarce and detail does not live; private lines buy acuity where detail is everything; and the fields themselves scale to match, small where you look and large where you keep watch. The compression is the strategy, not a concession. The retina spends its million cables the way a good newspaper spends its reporters.

Host: Now the oral examination, and tonight's list is a satisfying one. Give me the numbers and the definitions, slowly, the way a student should say them back.

Guide: Slowly, then. Population: roughly one and a half million ganglion cells per retina, with careful counts from about seven hundred thousand to one point seven million. One axon each, gathered in the nerve fibre layer, converging at the disc, forming an optic nerve of about one point two million fibres. Compression: about one hundred and twenty million rods and six million cones funnelled so that roughly one hundred receptors feed every output fibre across the retina, but near one to one at the fovea through the midget private line. Families: midget cells, about four fifths, small sustained fields carrying fine detail and red against green; parasol cells, about one tenth, large transient fields carrying motion and timing; small bistratified cells, the small remainder, carrying the blue against yellow comparison. Fields: a receptive field is the territory of the visual scene within which light changes the cell's firing; it has an excitatory centre and an antagonistic surround, in two mirror families, ON-centre with OFF-surround and OFF-centre with ON-surround, roughly equal in number. Output: action potentials at a rate that rises and falls around a never-silent baseline. And the frame, one last time: the retina ships a curated, parallel-edited summary, not a picture.

Host: One last question before the finale, and it is my favourite kind. Of everything we taught tonight, which detail do you find students most consistently underestimate?

Guide: The silence that is not silence. Students imagine that in the dark, or while staring at a blank wall, the optic nerve rests. It never rests. At this moment, in steady grey light or in deep dimness, about a million cables are ticking away at their baselines, and everything you will ever see arrives as a change to that ongoing storm: a quickening here, a slowing there, a burst, a pause. The retina holds the channel open all day and all night, and it speaks by modulation. That is why the receptive field idea matters so much. The cell's whole report is never the light itself; it is the difference the light makes against the cell's own resting rhythm. The first draft of sight is written before the message ever leaves the eye, and the ganglion cells hold the pen. Even the pause is theirs: when nothing changes, the steady baseline is itself a report, the sound of a healthy channel kept open.
