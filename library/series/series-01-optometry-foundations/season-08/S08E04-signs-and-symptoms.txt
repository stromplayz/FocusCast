# Description: Season eight of Focus Cast by focuslinks reaches one of the cleanest distinctions in clinical work. The Host and the Guide separate symptoms, what the patient feels and reports, from signs, what the clinician observes and measures. You will hear why each stream of evidence has its own job, how the two streams correct and confirm each other, and what it means when they disagree. One stubborn myth about symptoms being mere complaints gets taken apart along the way.

Host: Consider one red, watery eye, sitting in the middle of one ordinary Tuesday. The patient looking at it says, it stings, it waters, and the world through it looks like a fogged window. The clinician looking at it says, the surface shows a particular pattern, the reaction is mild, and here is what I can measure. Same eye. Same room. Same five minutes. Two completely different realities, and the visit depends on both. So here is today's question: which of those two accounts is the actual clinical finding? Hold your answer. We are going to earn it.

Guide: The honest answer is that both are, and they are different kinds of both. Every clinical encounter carries two streams of evidence. One stream is what the patient feels and reports, and we call those symptoms. The other is what the clinician observes and measures, and we call those signs. Keeping those two streams distinct, respecting what each one can and cannot tell you, and joining them at the end: that is one of the cleanest, most load bearing distinctions in all of clinical work. Today we take it apart slowly, and we put it back together properly.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, episode four of season eight of Optometry Foundations, our Clinical Communication and Reasoning season. Last time, Listening to the Patient, we trained the receiving craft: the mirror, the pause, the question behind the question. Well, today that listening lands somewhere. Everything the patient tells you flows into one stream, and everything the clinician observes flows into another, and today we learn to tell the streams apart and make them work together. This is Signs and Symptoms, and it might be the most useful vocabulary lesson of the season.

Guide: Here is the road map. First, the symptom: what it is, and why the patient's own words are precious. Second, the sign: what it is, and why observed evidence needs careful handling. Third, why the distinction matters at all, because two streams doing different jobs protect each other. Fourth, symptoms as genuine data, not noise. Fifth, signs as evidence that still needs context. Sixth, the joining: what happens when the streams agree, and what it means when they argue. Then a myth that does real quiet damage: the idea that signs are real and symptoms are just complaints. Let us begin with the patient's side.

Host: Before we define the words, a framing question. Clinicians clearly care about this vocabulary a great deal. Why does the profession lean on this distinction so hard? What does it buy that a casual approach never would?

Guide: It buys organization, and organization buys thinking. When experiences and observations are filed in separate drawers, patterns become visible that hide inside one mixed pile. It also buys a shared map across the whole team: the person at the desk, the clinician in the chair, and whoever reads the record next year all know which drawer is which. And it disciplines the conversation, because naming the stream you are standing in keeps the question honest. Are we talking about what you feel, or about what can be shown? That single sorting habit quietly improves every visit that follows it.

Host: The patient's side, then. Symptom. Everyone has used the word, few could define it cleanly. Give us the clean definition, and then tell me why the patient's own wording matters more than a tidy translation of it.

Guide: A symptom is what the patient feels and reports, in their own words, from the inside of their own experience. The stinging, the watering, the fogged window, the ache at the end of a reading day: nobody else can feel these, which makes the patient the only instrument in the world that can detect them. And their own wording matters because it is precise in a way translations are not. When someone says it is like looking through a shower door, they have given you the character of the blur, the texture of it, better than any standard phrase. The first rule of symptoms is: capture the words before you convert them.

Host: One boundary check on that definition. Do symptoms have to be about the eyes themselves, or can the stream carry things that point somewhere else entirely?

Guide: The stream carries whatever the person reports, and people report more than eye feelings. Headaches at the end of the school term. Dizziness when standing up in a hurry. A tiredness that arrives with screen work and leaves on holiday. These are reports from the person, and they belong in the first stream even when they do not sit neatly inside the eye. The eyes connect to everything else a person is, and a good clinician listens for symptoms that travel beyond them. The rule stays the same. Capture the words, hold the whole person, and let the record stay honest about where the report points.

Host: Capture the words before you convert them. I notice that puts the listening episodes to work immediately. The patient's own vocabulary becomes part of the record. So what is the clinician's side of this? Define the sign for us, and tell me what makes it a different kind of creature.

Guide: A sign is what the clinician observes or measures, from the outside, with trained eyes and instruments. The redness itself, the pattern of the surface, the way light reflects where it should not, the numbers that come back from a check. Signs are the observable half of the encounter. Two of their qualities matter most. They can be seen again: another clinician can look, and the record can be checked against the eye in front of you. And they can be described without the patient's interpretation, which makes them a kind of common ground for everyone who will ever be involved in this person's care.

Host: Here is a question that widens the observation stream. Who besides the clinician can actually see signs? Because families notice things too, do they not? A parent spots a squint across the dinner table before anyone books an appointment.

Guide: They do, and those observations matter, with one careful label attached. A parent who notices a head tilt, a teacher who reports a child sitting closer and closer to the board, a partner who says one eye drifts when you are tired: these are genuine observations from people who see the patient for more hours than any clinic ever will. They feed the observable stream, but they are recorded as reports from a second observer, clearly attributed, kept distinct from what the patient feels and from what the trained eye measures. Three sources, honestly labeled. That is how a record stays clean while still welcoming everyone who looked.

Host: Seen again, described without interpretation. That common ground idea is bigger than it sounds, isn't it? It means the sign is the part of the visit that outlives the visit. Okay, so we have the two definitions. Now make the case: why does keeping them distinct actually matter? Why not just call it all information and move on?

Guide: Because the two streams answer different questions, and mixing them up breaks both answers. Symptoms tell you something is happening to a person, and how much it matters to their life. Signs tell you what can be verified, weighed, and watched over time. A record that keeps them separate lets each stream do its job cleanly. The story stays honest about experience, the observations stay honest about evidence. And here is the practical magic: each stream protects the other. The symptoms stop the clinician from dismissing the person. The signs stop everyone from floating on impressions alone. Apart, each is vulnerable. Together, they hold.

Host: Paint the failure picture for me, then. What does a record actually look like when the two streams get tangled together by a rushed writer? Give me the damage in living color.

Guide: You see verbs go dishonest. The note says the patient denies pain, which is courtroom language dressed as shorthand, when what happened is the patient reported none. It says the eye looked angry, which smuggles an interpretation inside an observation and dares the next reader to untangle it. Whole paragraphs end up describing a mood instead of a finding, and the patient's actual words, the ones worth preserving, get flattened into generic phrases. The repair is small and total: report what was reported, show what was seen, think separately from both. Honest verbs, honest drawers. Ten seconds of discipline at the desk, every time.

Host: Each stream protects the other. I like that framing a lot, because it makes the distinction sound like a partnership instead of a hierarchy. Now, a listener question that goes straight at the edge of this. Kayo writes, can there be a sign with no symptom at all? Something the clinician sees and the patient feels nothing about?

Guide: Yes, and it happens more often than people expect. Eyes are famously quiet: changes can sit inside an eye for a long time without sending any feeling up to the person living with it. That is exactly why the observable stream exists as its own stream, because experience is not the whole story of the body. A person can feel perfectly fine while a clinician observing carefully sees something worth watching. That discovery is not a contradiction of the patient. It is the other stream doing the job only it can do, finding what feeling has not yet reported.

Host: Does the quietness of eyes surprise students, by the way? Because most of us grow up assuming the body always announces its trouble, loudly, like a smoke alarm with opinions.

Guide: It surprises nearly everyone, and it is a genuine turning point in clinical education. People arrive expecting that anything important will hurt, or itch, or announce itself somehow. Learning that parts of the body can change in silence, and that the observable stream exists precisely because experience is not the whole story, reshapes how a young clinician listens. It makes them kinder, actually. The patient who feels nothing is not being careless, and the clinician who looks carefully is not being fussy. The two streams simply cover different territory, and silence from one says nothing about the other.

Host: Eyes keeping quiet, and the clinician's stream being the one that notices. Which flips the question around for me. Priya asks the mirror image: can there be a symptom with no sign? Someone feels something real, and the clinician looks and measures and finds nothing to point at. What happens then?

Guide: Then the symptom still stands, and it deserves full respect. Absence of a finding is not absence of an experience. People report real things that a particular visit, on a particular day, with particular instruments, simply does not capture, and that happens in every field of healthcare. The honest response is neither dismissal nor invention. It is taking the report seriously, describing clearly what was and was not observed, and keeping watch: if the feeling persists or changes, we want to hear about it. A symptom with no sign today is a thread left deliberately in the record, not a loose end to be embarrassed about.

Host: So the record keeps faith with the person, in other words. That thread in the chart is almost a promise to the next visit, is it not?

Guide: Exactly the right word: a promise. The thread that says, the person reports this, nothing was observed today, watch with us, is continuity written down. Next visit, whoever sits in the chair starts from that thread instead of from zero. The patient does not have to rebuild their story from scratch and wonder whether anyone believed them. Communication is usually taught as people talking, but records talk too, across months, to strangers who become teammates. Keep faith in the file, and the file keeps faith with the person. That loop is one of the quiet glories of clinical work.

Host: A thread left deliberately in the record. Good. Now, symptoms as data. You have been arguing all episode that the patient's reports are not noise around the real evidence. Make that case at full strength. In what sense is a feeling a measurement?

Guide: In the sense that a well told experience carries information that behaves like data: it has timing, it has severity, it has triggers, it has a pattern across days. The blur that arrives at noon and clears by supper is telling you something structured. The ache that follows long reading sessions has a dose response, more reading, more ache. None of that is vague, even when the words are imperfect. It is the body's own reporting system, running continuously, powered by the one instrument that is always switched on. The clinician's job is to record it faithfully, the way you would record any reading you could not repeat on demand.

Host: Can experience be graded, though? You keep calling it data, and data suggests numbers, and numbers suggest rulers. Can a feeling sit on a ruler without us pretending it is something it is not?

Guide: It can be graded by convention, with the limits stated out loud. Scales and comparisons exist for exactly this: rate the ache against your usual days, tell me if today is better, worse, or the same as last week. Those numbers are agreements, not measurements of nature, and everyone using them should know that. Used honestly, they do something precious: they let experience be tracked across time, so improvement and change stop depending on memory alone. Grade to follow the story, never to rank the person. A number on a feeling is a tool with a label, and the label says, handle with respect.

Host: The body's own reporting system, always switched on. Then the flip side of that duty is on us, the listeners. If the patient has never been given the words, their data comes out garbled. Do good clinicians actually teach vocabulary? Is that a real part of the job?

Guide: It is one of the quiet, generous parts of the job. After the patient has tried in their own words, you can offer a gentle menu when they are searching: people often describe this as sandy, or gritty, or like an eyelash is in play, does any of that fit? You are not putting words in their mouth; you are holding up a wardrobe and letting them choose. The right word unlocks memory: the moment someone accepts the word throbbing, three more details usually fall out with it. Vocabulary is an instrument of listening, and like any instrument, it can be handed over.

Host: Now the signs' side gets its second look. Signs are evidence, but you have been careful all episode to say evidence needs context. What did you mean? Why is an observed finding not automatically a settled fact?

Guide: Because observation and interpretation are two different floors of the same building. The observation is what is there: the redness, the reflection, the number. The interpretation is what it might mean, and interpretation needs the story around it. The very same appearance can sit calmly in one person and matter enormously in another, depending on the history wrapped around it. That is why signs are recorded as carefully as possible, in the observed language, so that whoever weighs them next can do their own weighing. A sign is a strong fact about what was seen. What it means is a separate question, and it is never automatic.

Host: And that opens a door you hinted at in planning. Two trained clinicians look at the same eye, see the same thing, and walk away weighting it differently. Is that a scandal, or is it just what interpretation is?

Guide: It is just what interpretation is, and the system is built to absorb it. Two minds can agree completely on the observation and still differ on emphasis, because each brings a different stock of experience to the weighing. This is not a flaw to be embarrassed about; it is exactly why records matter. The observation is written down clean, so a third clinician, or the same clinician next month, can re-weigh it fresh. Humility lives in the chart. What I saw, separated from what I think it means. When those two are tangled together in a note, nobody downstream can undo the knot.

Host: That makes the chart sound like a courtesy to strangers, which I find weirdly moving. Is that how experienced clinicians actually think about their notes, as letters to people they will never meet?

Guide: Many do, once the habit sets in. Every record is a message in a bottle, and the most common reader is future you, on a day when memory has quietly rearranged the details. Write the observation clean, label the interpretation as interpretation, and the bottle arrives intact. Write a tangled one, and you are sending a puzzle to someone who is busy and trusting you. There is a kindness economy running under all of this: clear notes are small gifts to colleagues, to patients, and to the version of you that will need the truth later. Generosity, in this trade, is spelled clearly.

Host: Which brings us to the joining, the part of the craft I have been waiting for all episode. The streams are defined, respected, and kept separate. Now what? What does it actually look like when a clinician puts the story and the evidence together on the table?

Guide: The joining is a conversation between the streams. When they agree, confidence rises: the account and the observation point the same way, and you can move forward on solid ground. When they disagree, that disagreement is itself information, and it earns attention rather than a verdict. The person says everything feels fine, and something small was noticed: the right response is curiosity. Look again. Ask again, differently. Watch over time. The disagreement might resolve into nothing, or it might turn out to matter, and you do not get to know which until you have honored it. Streams that argue are not broken. They are working.

Host: Show us what the joining sounds like out loud, then. If the clinician is narrating the match between the streams, what does that sentence actually look like in the room?

Guide: It sounds like generosity with the reasoning. You feel a fog in the mornings, and what I can see fits that account; here is what we do next. Or the braver version: what you describe and what I can see do not line up yet, so we are going to look again and keep asking. Notice what both sentences do. They show the patient their own words steering the visit, they model the difference between feeling and observation, and they make the thinking visible instead of magical. Patients do not need to see every gear. They need to hear that the gears mesh, and that both streams had a vote.

Host: A listener question fits right here. Dev writes, so when the streams disagree, who wins? If I have to bet on one, which stream is more important, the person's account or the clinician's observation?

Guide: Neither, and the wanting to bet is the trap. The two streams answer different questions, so one of them winning is not even a coherent outcome. It is like asking whether the needle or the map is more important for a walk: the needle tells you where you point, the map tells you where things are, and a good walker uses both and trusts neither blindly. In practice, the disagreement itself is the finding to act on: it means look again, ask again, watch longer. The moment you crown one stream as the judge, you have quietly blinded yourself to the other one's warnings.

Host: Okay, it is myth time, and this myth has a cruel streak: signs are real, symptoms are just complaints. The measurable stuff is medicine. The talking stuff is theater. Guide, I want this one dismantled so thoroughly it never bothers our listeners again.

Guide: Happily, because the myth misunderstands both words. A symptom is not a complaint. It is a report from the only instrument that can detect it, and as we said, some of the body's territory never sends feeling at all while other parts of life live entirely in feeling. Care built only from signs is care built without the person: you might address a finding beautifully while missing the thing that actually frightens them. And care built only from complaints floats free of what can be verified. Every good plan of action stands on both streams. The myth survives because measuring feels rigorous, but the report is evidence too. It is simply evidence of a different, equally real layer.

Host: Before we move on, where does this myth show up most subtly? Not in the loud dismissive version, I mean. Where does it sneak back in, even in people who think they have rejected it?

Guide: In small words that carry big posture. The patient complains of, as if reporting an experience were a character flaw. The patient claims the blur comes and goes, as if the account needed a lawyer present. Listen to the verbs and you can hear which writer quietly believes the myth. The clean alternatives cost nothing: reports, describes, notices. Language leaks assumptions, and patients feel the leak long before anyone says anything explicit. Rejecting the myth loudly is easy. Rejecting it one verb at a time, in every note, is the version that actually changes care.

Host: The report is evidence of a different, equally real layer. So if someone wants to carry this distinction into their week, what do they practice? Give us the drills for keeping the streams honest.

Guide: Three drills. One: when you record anything, silently ask, is this what they feel or what I see, and put it on the correct side; the question takes two seconds and sorts the whole chart. Two: capture at least one description in the patient's own words, before any translating happens, and let it keep its texture. Three: when you state an observation, say plainly what you saw, and keep what you think it means in a separate sentence. Do those three and your records become bilingual: fluent in experience, fluent in evidence, and clear about which is which at every line.

Host: Guide, before we wrap, zoom out for me. This season is building a picture of the clinician as a communicator and a reasoner. Where does today's distinction sit in that bigger picture? What is it actually for?

Guide: It is the hinge between the human half of the season and the judgment half that is coming. Talking, asking, and listening, everything so far, exists largely to fill the first stream honestly. Everything coming, judging what is normal, screening, deciding, spotting urgency, works on evidence, most of it observed. The distinction is the bridge between the two halves: it keeps the person's experience flowing in cleanly, and it keeps the clinician's observations honest and separate. Two streams, one river. Lose the distinction and either the person drowns in the chart or the chart ignores the person. Keep it, and the whole season holds together. Next time we start weighing what the observable stream produces, and the first tool on the bench is the idea of normal.

Host: Let us recap today's journey. We defined the symptom: what the patient feels and reports, in words worth capturing exactly. We defined the sign: what the clinician observes and measures, evidence that can be seen again. We saw why each stream protects the other, why feelings behave like data, why observations still need context and clean records, and why a disagreement between the streams is a finding in itself. And we retired the myth that signs are real while symptoms are just complaints.

Guide: Next time, we push the evidence stream one step further and ask a question that sounds simple and is anything but: what counts as normal? There is a whole architecture behind that word, reference ranges and natural variation and gray zones, and once you see it, you stop being fooled by single numbers. It is called Normal and Abnormal Findings, and it changes how you hear every value you will ever be shown.

Host: Normal and Abnormal Findings, next time. Bring your skepticism about averages; we are going to need it. Until then, keep both streams flowing, and let them argue in front of you.

Guide: Thank you for spending this time with us, and for guarding the patient's own words wherever you work. From all of us here at Focus Cast by focuslinks, keep noticing what people feel, keep naming what you see, and we will see you next time.
