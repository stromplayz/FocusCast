# Description: Season eight of Focus Cast by focuslinks continues with the craft of the question. The Host and the Guide explore how optometrists build a patient history, from wide open openers to precise follow ups. You will hear why one question at a time beats a double barrel, how leading questions quietly plant answers, and how a funnel shape turns chatter into clinical clarity. One stubborn myth about long checklists gets taken apart along the way.

Host: Here is a scene I cannot shake. Two clinicians, same clinic, same kind of patient, and the very same ten minutes at the start of a visit. One walks out of that room knowing exactly what is going on. The other leaves with a page of scattered notes and a vague feeling that something was missed. Same patient, same clock. What made the difference? Not the machines, which never got touched yet. The questions. Today we study the difference between asking and asking well.

Guide: Because questions are the first instrument of optometry, and they get picked up before anything on the trolley. A patient history is not collected like laundry; it is built, one question at a time, and every question either opens a door or closes one. The right question, asked at the right moment, changes what the patient remembers, what they mention, and what they feel safe enough to say. Ask carelessly and you still get words back, but they are the wrong words, neatly delivered. Ask well and the visit nearly runs itself.

Host: Welcome back to Focus Cast by focuslinks, where we are deep in season eight of Optometry Foundations, the season we are calling Clinical Communication and Reasoning. Last episode, Talking to Your Patient, was about the conversation itself: the greeting, the plain language, the close. Today we zoom into the sharpest tool inside that conversation, the question itself. By the end of this episode you will know why questions come first, what open and closed questions are actually for, and how to sequence them so they feel like a path instead of an interrogation. Let us go.

Guide: Here is the plan. First, why the humble question outranks every machine in the room. Second, the two great families of questions, open and closed, and the job each one does. Third, the discipline of one question at a time, and the trap of the double barrel. Fourth, leading questions, the polite little suggestions that plant answers. Fifth, follow ups, where the real treasure sits. And sixth, sequencing, the funnel that carries a conversation from wide open down to the one thing that matters most. Then a myth about checklists, and I have been waiting for that one.

Host: You have been visibly excited about the checklist myth all week, so we will get there. First things first, though. You keep calling the question an instrument, which sounds almost poetic. Make the case literally. If I put a question next to a lens on a bench, in what sense is it the same kind of tool?

Guide: Literally, then. An instrument is a thing you use to gather information you cannot get any other way. A question does exactly that, and it does it before anything else can, because the answers decide what happens next. What brings you in today? When did you first notice it? Does it change through the day? The answers to those lines decide where the whole visit looks, the way a compass decides where the whole walk goes. A blunt instrument gives sloppy readings. A blunt question does the same, and the patient feels it too.

Host: A compass for the visit. I like that, because a compass is small and cheap and yet everything about the route depends on it. So a sloppy question is not just style. It genuinely bends the clinical day. Walk me through one concrete way that a single question changes what happens next.

Guide: Take one line: when did you first notice the blur? The answer reshapes the visit instantly. Since childhood points one direction. Last Tuesday, suddenly, points somewhere very different. Two months of slow drift points somewhere else again. One short question, three different clinics, three different sets of priorities for the very same complaint. That is what I mean by steering. The question is not decoration around the examination. It is the examination, at its most concentrated, happening in plain sentences before anything is touched.

Host: Let me back up a step, because something is bothering me. Nobody sets out to ask bad questions. Everyone in that chair went to school, passed exams, cares about patients. Where do sloppy questions actually come from, in real clinics, with real people who mean well?

Guide: Mostly from hurry and fear, in that order. Under time pressure, we grab for the fastest wording, which is usually the closed, leading, double barrel wording, because it promises a quick answer. And under the fear of seeming unsure, we ask questions that sound expert instead of questions that learn, because saying I am not following yet feels risky. Curiosity is the antidote to both. A genuinely curious question is almost never clumsy, because it is aimed at the truth rather than at the clock or at the image. The best questioners in any profession are the ones still willing to be taught by the answer.

Host: Which brings us to the two great families you promised: open questions and closed questions. I know these words from school, but I suspect most of us were never actually taught when to use which. Define the families for us, and tell me what each one is for.

Guide: An open question hands the patient the microphone. Tell me about your eyes. What have the last few weeks been like? Walk me through a typical morning. There is no single right answer, so the patient builds the answer from what actually matters to them, and the order they choose tells you things no form ever could. A closed question takes the microphone back and asks for a fact. Does it hurt right now? Was it both eyes? Is it worse in the morning? One word or a short phrase, precise and quick. Open questions find the story. Closed questions pin the facts to the page.

Host: Here is a question about the two families themselves. Beyond accuracy, does one family do more for trust than the other? If a patient walks in anxious, which kind of question opens them up?

Guide: The open ones, almost every time, and for a structural reason. An open question hands over a small piece of control, and anxious people relax when they are handed control. Tell me what has been on your mind says, this hour belongs to you too. A rapid chain of closed questions, even a kind one, can feel like custody: answer this, and this, and this. Closed questions are still essential, but they land better once the patient feels heard. Trust first, precision second. That ordering is not politeness. It is technique, and it changes what people are willing to tell you.

Host: Find the story, pin the facts. So they are not rivals, they are teammates, and the mistake would be using only one of them the whole visit. How do the two families actually cooperate in a real consultation, in terms of order and rhythm?

Guide: The classic rhythm is open first, closed later. You begin wide and let the patient lay out their own map, because their opening minutes are the richest data you will collect all day. Then, as themes appear, you close in with precise questions to fix the details in place: which eye, how long, how often, what makes it better. Think of a camera. The open question is the wide shot that shows the whole scene. The closed questions are the close ups that capture the detail you now know matters. Zoom out, then zoom in, and never mistake one for the other.

Host: You said something in our planning that stuck with me. Faces ask questions too. What does that mean, and what does it have to do with the words coming out of the clinician's mouth?

Guide: Every expression invites or answers something. A raised eyebrow in the middle of a story says, really, whether you mean it or not, and the patient edits their next sentence accordingly. Surprise at the wrong moment can close a topic for the rest of the visit. The skill is a kind, steady face: interested without shock, warm without performing. You want the patient narrating to you, not managing you. Watch your own face the way you would watch a question, and save the visible reactions for moments you actually choose. The face is part of the asking, and it never stops talking.

Host: Time for our first listener question, and this one comes from a student drowning in history templates. Amara writes, how many questions are too many? Our handout has forty lines. I feel like a form with legs. At what point does a good history become an interrogation?

Guide: When Amara feels like a form with legs, she has answered her own question. There is no magic number, because the count is not what patients notice. They notice rhythm. Questions should feel like stepping stones across a river, each one landing where the last answer pointed. The moment you are reading lines off a sheet in a fixed order, you have stopped asking and started reciting, and the patient can hear the difference instantly. Some visits need twelve questions. Some need thirty. The count follows the conversation, never the other way around. A checklist is a safety net. It is not the fishing rod.

Host: One practical question to hand our listeners, then. If there is a single question worth asking at the very start of a visit, before any line of any template, what should it be?

Guide: Borrow this one: what would you like to get out of today? It takes ten seconds, and it does three jobs at once. It surfaces the real agenda, which is sometimes different from the booked reason for the visit. It hands the patient authorship of the hour. And it tells you how to spend your limited minutes, because a person who says I want to be sure nothing serious is going on needs a different emphasis than one who says I want new glasses this week. The goal question is the cheapest steering you will ever buy.

Host: A safety net, not the fishing rod. Hold that thought for the myth later, because we are going to spend proper time on it. Next family of trouble: the double barrel. You promised us a trap with a very specific shape, so spring it. What is a double barrel question, and why do they cause so much mischief?

Guide: A double barrel is two questions wearing one coat. Do you get headaches and feel sick when you read? Do the eyes water and itch in the wind? It sounds efficient, but watch what happens. The patient answers the half they remember most vividly, the other half evaporates, and you both walk away believing the matter is settled when half of it was never addressed. One idea per question, every time. It feels slower, and it is actually faster, because nothing gets dropped and nothing needs untangling later. Efficiency that loses half the cargo is not efficiency at all.

Host: And I suppose the coat comes off easily if you just hear yourself ask it out loud. If you catch yourself saying the word and in the middle of a question, that is the alarm bell. Beyond the double barrel, though, there is a subtler asking sin, one with a gentler reputation. Talk to me about leading questions.

Guide: A leading question quietly suggests its own answer. The light bothers you, right? So the pressure is worse in the morning, yes? These feel friendly, and they are poison for accuracy, because people are naturally agreeable. Faced with a confident suggestion, many patients will simply confirm it, especially if they are nervous, tired, or eager to be helpful. You end up with answers that came from your mouth, not their memory. The fix is neutral phrasing. How does the light feel? When is it worst? Ask like you genuinely do not know, because clinically, you genuinely do not.

Host: Let me widen the lens one more time. Does the clinician's curiosity about the person, not just the eyes, actually change the answers you get? Or is asking about work and hobbies just, well, small talk?

Guide: It changes everything, and here is the clinical reason. Lives contain causes. The accountant with a wall of screens, the baker with flour dust and early mornings, the new parent sleeping in fragments: their days are where their eye stories live. Ask about the day, and the day hands you the context that makes every other answer make sense. That is not small talk. That is data with a warm accent. A clinician curious about people asks better questions almost by accident, because curiosity widens the net, and a wider net catches more of what actually matters.

Host: Ask like you do not know. There is something almost humbling in that, because a confident tone can feel like competence, and here it is actively manufacturing bad data. Let me bring in a listener question that fits beautifully. Dev writes, what do I do when the patient answers a question I did not ask? Do I redirect, or let the train go wherever it wants?

Guide: Welcome the detour first, redirect second. Patients rarely wander for no reason; the tangent is often where the real worry lives, wearing a casual costume. If someone asked about dry mornings starts describing their new medication, there is a thread there worth pulling. The skill is naming the thread, not cutting it: that sounds important, let us come back to the eyes in just a moment. Then you actually come back, which is what makes the technique honest. A clinician who permits detours and steers gently gets more truth per minute than one who rails the conversation to a track.

Host: Quick technique check while we are near this topic. What about echo questions, where you just repeat the patient's last few words back with a rising tone? Is that a real tool or a lazy one?

Guide: It is a real tool when it is a door, and a lazy one when it is a reflex. Repeating the loaded words back, since Tuesday, you said, invites the patient to expand exactly there, with zero steering from you, because you added no content of your own. That is the beauty of it. Used mechanically, though, it becomes a parrot act, and people notice. Sparingly, at the moments where their voice speeded up or slowed down, is where the echo shines. It marks the spot where the story flinched, and flinches are always worth a visit.

Host: Name the thread, then come back to it. The come back is the part everyone forgets, I bet. Okay, follow ups. You said the real treasure sits here, which is a strong claim given that we have already covered openers and neutrals. What exactly makes a follow up the richest question in the room?

Guide: Because the first answer is almost never the full answer. It is the headline, and headlines are short. The follow up is where the story deepens: tell me more about that. When exactly does it happen? What makes it better, and what makes it worse? What have you already tried at home? Each of these takes one thread from the opening and weaves it out to its full length. Here is the lovely part: follow ups do double duty clinically and relationally. The patient hears that their first answer mattered enough to be pulled on, and people give richer answers to people who are visibly using what they say.

Host: The first answer is the headline, and the follow up reads the article. I noticed something in your examples, Guide. Several of them are barely questions at all. Tell me more is almost an invitation wearing a question mark. Is that deliberate?

Guide: Completely deliberate, and it is the secret of the genre. The best follow ups lower the barrier to speaking rather than raising it. Tell me more asks for nothing specific, so the patient supplies whatever is most important, unfiltered by your guesses. Compare that with, so the blur is constant? One of those sentences opens a window. The other installs a window shaped like your assumption. Invitations also buy thinking time. People often need a second to reach for the accurate version of their story, and a gentle prompt gives them that second without pressure.

Host: You mentioned precision earlier, so let me push on it. Patients are famously vague about their own lives. How do you ask for numbers, how much, how often, how long, without turning a warm conversation into a census form?

Guide: You trade precision for honesty, and you get there with anchors. How often is hard; most people just shrug. Compared to what is easier: more days than not? A few times a month? Would your best friend say you worry about this a lot? Anchors let people locate their experience without doing arithmetic. And when a number truly matters, offer a range and let them correct it. Rough but true beats precise but invented, every single time. The moment a patient starts guessing to please you, the numbers stop being information and become decoration, so guard the honesty first.

Host: Which sets up our last teaching segment perfectly: sequence. You called it a funnel. Explain the shape, because I want to know where the visit starts, where it should end, and what the walls of this funnel are made of.

Guide: The funnel starts wide and narrows with purpose. At the top, the broadest possible invitation: tell me what has been happening with your eyes. No steering, no forms, just the open door. As the story unfolds, the questions narrow along the strongest threads: that blur, when did it begin, which situations, how often. By the bottom of the funnel you are asking precise closed questions about one specific thing. And here is the move almost everyone skips: at the very end, come back out to the patient. Of everything we have talked about, what worries you most? The funnel ends with their agenda, not yours.

Host: Where do the pre-visit forms fit in all this, by the way? Most clinics hand over paperwork before anyone has said a word. Is that a rival to the funnel, or a helper?

Guide: A helper, if you use it well. The form is the patient talking to themselves on paper: it warms up memories before the conversation begins. But read it as a draft, not as finished data. Written answers are edited answers, sorted in private, stripped of tone. So ask about the form, not from it. I see you mentioned the headaches here, tell me more. The paper becomes a doorway instead of a destination. The trouble only starts when the form gets treated as business already concluded, which is exactly the trap we will dismantle at the end of the episode.

Host: That last question gives me chills, honestly. You can do everything right and still miss the thing they actually came in frightened about, unless you ask. Which brings me to our final listener question, and it is a brave one. Priya writes, how do you ask about things people find embarrassing? Some topics make patients shut down the moment they sense where the questions are going.

Guide: You go first, kindly. Before the delicate question, normalize: many people notice this, and it is one of the most common things we hear about. That single sentence tells the patient they are not a strange case and not on trial. Then ask plainly, with a level face and a steady tone, because a flinch or a raised eyebrow at the wrong moment can close the topic for the rest of the visit. No moralizing, no surprise, just professional calm. And give them an easy first step, like saying the word itself out loud. Privacy is largely a matter of tone, not locked doors.

Host: Okay, it is myth time, and today's myth is the one Guide has been circling all episode: a longer checklist means a better history. More lines on the form, more boxes ticked, more thorough the care. It sounds so reasonable. Guide, take it apart for us, gently but completely.

Guide: Gently, then completely. The myth is half right, which is what makes it so sticky. Structure does protect you; a checklist catches the things a busy mind forgets, and nobody should practice without one. But listen to what the myth claims: that the value of a history is the number of facts harvested. A history is not a harvest, it is a understanding, and understanding does not scale with box count. You can tick forty boxes and never learn that the patient is terrified of losing their driving license. The checklist is the safety net under the trapeze. The trapeze is the conversation. Skip the net and you are reckless. Confuse the net for the act and you are hollow.

Host: The net is not the act. That is the line, right there. So the checklists stay, but they work for us in the background while the actual history is a shaped conversation, wide at the top, precise at the bottom, ending on the patient's own worry. What should people practice this week to make this real?

Guide: Three habits, in order. Start open: whatever your template says, your first sentence is a genuine invitation with no steering in it. Ask single: one idea per question, and if you hear yourself say and in the middle, split it into two. End with their agenda: before you leave the history, ask what worries them most, and let the answer reorganize your day if it needs to. Master those three and the fancier technique, the neutral phrasing, the follow ups, grows out of them naturally. Ask with a shape, not a script, and the shape will remember itself.

Host: Last coaching question before we get to the myth. You have watched a lot of students learn this craft over the years. What is the most common asking mistake you see in people who are genuinely trying hard?

Guide: Asking to confirm instead of asking to learn. A student forms a quiet guess in the first minute, then spends the rest of the history collecting yeses for it. The questions look diligent. They are actually testimony for a verdict that was reached before the evidence came in, and the patient can feel the difference even when they cannot name it. The cure is a small identity shift: walk in as the least informed person in the room, on purpose, and let the patient teach you. Every expert in this field was once a beginner who kept that habit.

Host: Guide, one more layer before we close. Someone listening is thinking, this all sounds lovely, but what about when the answers contradict each other, or the story just does not hang together? What does the well trained questioner do in that moment of honest confusion?

Guide: They ask the confusion out loud, respectfully. There is a question for that too: help me understand, because earlier you said the mornings are fine, and now the mornings sound hard. Said with warmth, this is not a gotcha; it is an invitation to resolve the tangle together, and the resolution is often where the real story has been hiding all along. Contradiction is not a problem in a history. It is a landmark. People's experiences are genuinely tangled, and the clinician who can sit comfortably with a tangle, and ask about it without judgment, gets histories that tidier questioners never see.

Host: Contradiction is a landmark. What a reframe. Let us recap today's journey. We learned that questions are the first instruments, the compass of the visit. We met the two families, open to find the story and closed to pin the facts, working in the rhythm of open first, closed later. We dropped the double barrel, disarmed the leading question, mined the gold in follow ups, and shaped it all with the funnel, ending on the patient's own biggest worry.

Guide: And notice how today stands on last episode's shoulders. Talking well created the room where good questions can breathe, and good questions are how talking becomes clinical. Next episode completes the trio. We have learned to speak and to ask; now comes the skill under both of them, the one that separates clinicians who hear words from clinicians who hear people. If today was about the questions leaving your mouth, next time is about everything that comes back, and what you do with it.

Why interrupting the first thirty seconds is a clinical error, what silence can extract that questions cannot, and the strange skill of hearing what is not being said. Bring your ears.
