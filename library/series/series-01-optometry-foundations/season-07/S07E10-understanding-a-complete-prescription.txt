Guide: With pleasure. Sphere is the overall focusing correction, the part of the lens that works the same in every direction of your gaze. Our second episode this season was devoted to it entirely. Guide: Cylinder is the correction for astigmatism, the extra blur that appears when the front of the eye is curved more steeply in one direction than in the others. Our third episode this season handled the cylinder, and our fourth handled its inseparable partner, the axis. The cylinder states how much astigmatic correction the eye needs. The axis states the direction in which that correction must sit, a figure that runs from zero around to one hundred eighty. The two travel as a pair, because an amount without a direction is unfinished information. Think of posting a parcel: the cylinder is the weight, the axis is the address, and a parcel with weight but no address goes nowhere useful. Where sphere speaks in every direction at once, cylinder speaks in one precise direction, and the axis is how we know which one.

Host: The parcel analogy settles it forever: amount and address, shipped together. They are grammatically married on the slip. Which brings us to the row that sits a little further down the page, usually with a short label like add. What is that one about?

Guide: Add is the near work allowance, extra plus power set aside for reading and other close tasks. Our fifth episode this season gave it a full conversation, and the short version is this: as the lens inside the eye loses its youthful flexibility, usually through the middle decades of life, near work starts demanding more focusing effort than the eye can comfortably spend. The add supplies that extra effort from the lens instead. It is typically the same value for both eyes, and it lives in its own row because it only applies up close. The distance numbers above it remain exactly as they were, untouched and unbothered, which surprises a lot of first time readers. The eye was never failing; it was simply asking for different help at different distances.

Host: Like a boost switch on a music system: same button for both channels, and it only changes the sound when you sit down close to the speakers. Guide: Prism is the gentle steering correction. Where sphere and cylinder sharpen the image, prism shifts where the image lands, so that two eyes can aim at the same point without strain. Our sixth episode this season covered it in full, and the values are usually small, each written with a direction such as base in or base out. Most prescriptions leave the prism row empty, because most eyes hold their alignment comfortably on their own. But when it appears, it earns its place: a tiny nudge that can dissolve headaches and doubling that no amount of sharpening could touch. Prism reminds us that seeing is a partnership between two eyes, not just a photograph taken by one.

Guide: Gladly. A plus lens gathers light rays toward each other, and a minus lens lets them spread apart, and that single difference in shape decides who each lens can help. We gave plus and minus lenses their own conversation recently, so one sentence each will serve today. Plus lenses assist eyes that under focus, and minus lenses assist eyes that over focus. The sign is not a grade, and it is never a verdict of good eyes or bad eyes. It is simply a direction of bending, chosen to match the direction in which the eye itself missed. But before we open the question bag, we have one piece of unfinished business, a myth that clings to prescriptions everywhere. Name it for us, and then let us take it apart with respect, because the people who believe it are being perfectly reasonable with the information they have.

Guide: The myth says a prescription is a report card, a grade on the health of your eyes, where mild numbers mean strong eyes and strong numbers mean failing eyes. It is an understandable belief, because the slip arrives from a clinical visit and clinical visits can feel like judgment day. But the prescription measures one narrow thing: the correction your eyes need so that the world focuses where it should. It says nothing about the health of the retina, the pressure inside the eye, or the clarity of the cornea, because the optometrist assesses all of that separately in the same visit. A gentle prescription can live beside a serious condition, and a strong prescription can belong to perfectly healthy eyes. The slip is a plan, not a verdict, and mixing up those two words causes needless worry.

First one is short and sharp: can the two eyes on one prescription be completely different from each other, like strangers sharing an address?

Guide: Absolutely, and it happens more often than people expect. Each eye develops on its own schedule, so one eye can sit comfortably close to perfect while the other needs real correction. The remarkable part is that the brain usually blends two slightly different images into one seamless view without complaint. The columns exist so that neither eye ever has to speak for the other.
